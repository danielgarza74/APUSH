SECONDARY SOURCE S7.19

Jefferson’s Ethics and Political Progress

Ari Helo

WORK Thomas Jefferson's Ethics and the Politics of Human Progress: The Morality of a Slaveholder

SELECTION Introduction, “On the Methods and the Thesis”

SOURCE LOCATOR Printed pages 12–13 · Supplied edition: 2014

Excerpt

The method of clinging to Jefferson’s own words about his intentions does not entail the view that he could not err in his logic. Nevertheless, most of the time Jefferson was much more circumspect in his formulations than later commentators have allowed. As to haphazard contextualization, it is notable that Jefferson’s probably decades-long liaison with his slave mistress, Sally Hemings, does not appear to have had any impact on his racist opinions about African Americans. Historians often find Jefferson subscribing to some fixed positions and then changing his mind about them. More philosophically oriented scholars tend to consider Jefferson’s beliefs as equally fixed, so as to compare them to what one now should think of science, ethics, ethnicity, and democracy. In fact, the crucial consistencies appear to lie in Jefferson being constantly disappointed in his largely political expectations about the promotion of material, intellectual, and ethical human progress.

The fundamental presumption of this research is that Jefferson’s keen awareness of living in an ongoing history helped him distinguish various extra-historical assumptions about the human condition from the assumptions based on historical evidence. Both kinds of assumptions remained equally distinguishable from his progressive assumption that humankind may experience genuine novelties.

Indeed, Jefferson appears to have kept his ethical convictions about universal justice and benevolence distinct from the historically verifiable notion of conflict-ridden human sociability. Such extra-temporal conceptions as natural law and providential benevolence remained for him non-disprovable hypotheses. However, history showed that human understanding had already gone through significant ethical changes. Jefferson discerned development in both the generally shared patterns of proper behavior as well as the literary definitions of such patterns. Thus, one finds Jefferson disagreeing with many authorities on the law of nature and nations, who still considered African slavery morally defensible. People obviously could transcend historically sanctioned forms of inequality.

The remedy was nothing less than the eighteenth-century ideal of free, representative democracy. Under democratic majority rule, justified violence could count as an acceptable method to preserve justice among equals. At the same time, extending justice to those still deprived of it demanded politics of benevolence on the part of the empowered, those currently endowed with equal political rights. But Jeffersonian progress offered no guarantees against temporary setbacks in even enlightened, democratic majority opinion. Eventually, Jefferson’s conception of individual self-determination remained subordinate to the simple practice of renewing one’s commitment to justice and benevolence every day, here and now, in concert with others.

In sum, one should understand Jefferson’s optimism about the future in political rather than in utopian terms. The politician’s present is equally future-oriented with that of a utopian. But the utopian needs only to define the desirable end result, not a policy for moving forward from here and now. The difference, therefore, arises with the politician’s attitude to the present as a result of the past and, thus, as the meaningful context of one’s future orientation. Changes may be imposed only on the current results of history, meaning for example, that the legal institution of slavery must be made illegal politically. As noted, whenever violence occurs, politics has failed.
