SECONDARY SOURCE S7.7

Impressment, Citizenship, and the War of 1812

Denver Brunsman

WORK The Evil Necessity: British Naval Impressment in the Eighteenth-Century Atlantic World

SELECTION Epilogue

SOURCE LOCATOR Printed pages 246–248 · Supplied edition: 2013

Excerpt

The British state refused to allow its sailors to escape uncontested. Although Britain never pursued an official policy of targeting U.S. citizens for impressment, honoring America’s independence was of secondary importance to reclaiming British deserters. As Lieutenant Mackenzie predicted, cultural similarities between British and American seamen caused mayhem distinguishing between the two groups on the high seas. British mariners took advantage of the situation to acquire fraudulent U.S. citizenship protections, which led the British government to disregard all certificates not issued directly by American customs officials (and even some that were). Moreover, individual navy commanders and press gangs often blurred differences between American citizens and British subjects to fill their shipping complements. The combination of honest mistakes and willful negligence resulted in the British navy’s impressing approximately ten thousand American seafaring citizens between 1793 and 1812.16

The United States considered each case a violation of its national sovereignty. Among wartime neutrals, it took a particularly hard-line position against impressment, objecting to the Royal Navy’s stopping and removing any sailors—regardless of nationality—from American merchant ships. The U.S. position was always informed by more than just economic considerations. The crisis over impressment joined a host of other humiliating affronts to the young republic’s ideals of independence, including the capture of the nation’s seamen by Barbary pirates, the interference of its commercial shipping by Britain and France, and the ongoing violence against its settlers by Native American groups in the western backcountry. Impressment had particular purchase with the American people because of their history as former subjects under the British crown. No issue since the American Revolution brought into clearer focus the differences between subjecthood in a monarchy and citizenship in a republic. In protesting the capture and resubjugation of their impressed countrymen under British rule, Americans defined for themselves what it meant to be a citizen of the United States: It was a choice, not an obligation.17

For different reasons, then, the United States and Britain both viewed impressment as vital to its independence. The high stakes kept them from reaching a diplomatic solution in the years before the War of 1812. The lowest point in their relations before America’s declaration of war in 1812 took place in June 1807, when HMS Leopard fired a broadside into the USS Chesapeake off of the Virginia coast after its captain, James Barron, refused to relinquish British deserters among his crew. The broadside killed three sailors and injured eighteen others (one of whom died soon thereafter). But, perhaps most appalling to the American public, a party from the Leopard then boarded the Chesapeake and removed four suspected British deserters. Three turned out to be American citizens; the fourth, Jenkin Ratford, was a deserter who had joined the Chesapeake under the alias John Wilson.18

Soon thereafter, news arrived in the United States of a still more threatening British policy for American shipping—the Orders in Council of November 1807. The Orders established Britain’s commercial blockade of Napoleonic Europe and prevented neutrals from trading directly with French-held ports. Between 1807 and 1812, about nine hundred American merchant ships fell victim to the commercial policies of the two belligerents. In response, the United States concentrated its foreign and diplomatic policy on regaining free trade rights and pursued a series of mostly self-defeating economic sanctions against Britain and France until the War of 1812.19

All the while, impressment remained a serious threat to American seamen and a popular issue in Democratic-Republican party newspapers before it appeared first among President James Madison’s list of grievances against Britain in his June 1, 1812, war message to Congress. American historians have argued for generations about the causes of the War of 1812, from impressment and the Orders in Council to American expansionist desires and the rivalry between the Republican and Federalist parties. One point is irrefutable: Impressment served as the key justification for the war once it began. On June 23, 1812, the British government repealed the Orders in Council without knowing (because of normal delays in transatlantic communication) that the United States had declared war on Britain five days earlier. Thereafter, America’s only remaining condition for peace was that Britain agree to stop impressing from American merchant vessels.20

Author’s Notes

16. For Britain’s policy on impressing Americans, see John W. Croker, “Minutes of Correspondence Transmitted by Croker on Impressment of American Seamen, Dated 21 February 1812,” FO 5 / 104, TNA. Determining the total number of impressed American seamen is admittedly an inexact science. I have settled on the number of 10,000 by comparing my research with the best existing studies on the issue: Zimmerman, Impressment of American Seamen, 266–67; Perkins, Prologue to War, 92; and S. T. Jackson, “Impressment and the Anglo-American Discord,” 52–53. From the beginning of the French wars in 1793 to the Peace of Amiens in 1802, the Royal Navy impressed about 2,000 American sailors. In 1812, the U.S. government reported that 6,257 Americans had been impressed, but the figures omitted those impressed before 1803 and after 1810. By adding these figures together, one arrives at about 10,000 for the entire period before the War of 1812. Donald Hickey has reached a similar conclusion in Don’t Give up the Ship!, 21. For the government’s original quarterly returns on impressed seamen, see Entries 930–33, 935–36, in Records Relating to Impressed Seamen, 1793–1815, RG 59, National Archives, Washington, D.C. The returns after the 1796 Seamen’s Protection Act are printed in U.S. Congress, American State Papers, 2:129–36, 269–80, 471–74, 593–95, 776–98; 3:36–79, 347–48; 4:56–95.

17. For the cultural impact of the impressment crisis, see Watts, Republic Reborn, esp. 265–74; Glenn, “Troubled Manhood”; and Glenn, “Forging Manhood.” For issues of citizenship, see Brunsman, “Subjects vs. Citizens.”

18. Tucker and Reuter, Injured Honor; Cray, “Remembering the USS Chesapeake.”

19. For the diplomatic run-up to the War of 1812, see Perkins, Prologue to War, and D. R. Hickey, “Monroe-Pinkney Treaty of 1806.”

20. For background to the debate over the war’s causes, see Perkins, ed., Causes of the War of 1812, and D. R. Hickey, Don’t Give up the Ship!, 12–47. The most recent major studies have emphasized the larger transnational, international, and Atlantic contexts of the war. See Latimer, 1812: War with America; J. Black, War of 1812; and A. Taylor, Civil War of 1812.
