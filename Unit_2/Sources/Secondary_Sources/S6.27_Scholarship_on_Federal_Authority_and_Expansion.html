Scholarship on Federal Authority and Expansion

Gautham Rao

WORK William J. Novak’s The People’s Welfare and the New Historiography of the Early Federal State

SELECTION Book Symposium essay

SOURCE LOCATOR Printed pages 230 · Supplied edition: 2017

Compiler’s context: This is Rao’s 2017 scholarly review essay about Novak and later scholarship, rather than an excerpt from Novak’s book. The discussion follows federal institutions beyond this unit, into the 1830s.

Excerpt

The story of the federal government’s incorporation of the Louisiana Purchase lands and the gradual territorial expansion of the nation has been a second area in which a federal government once considered diminutive has come to assume great importance. Here the connection to The People’s Welfare has less to do with legal doctrine in practice than in finding new venues in which to identify the state in action. Thus, for scholars such as Bethel Saler and William Bergmann, white migration into the old northwest and the west was a state directed activity aiming to expand the sovereignty of this society itself. Saler’s extremely important The Settler’s Empire explains the process of state-building that allowed the federal government to gradually and unsteadily incorporate Wisconsin into the Union in this way: “. . . the state referred to not just one but two interdependent constructs of individual and national ‘states’ that together defined U.S. federalism. Second . . . the concrete actions of the different ‘states’ or government in play—the central state, its agents in the field, and representative territorial legislatures.”13

My own dissertation would try to draw from Novak’s method in The People’s Welfare to the custom houses of the early American republic. The resulting book, National Duties: Custom Houses and the Making of the American State, argues the challenge of governing Atlantic capitalism transformed the early federal government from a diffuse structure patterned on early modern overseas empires toward a modern central government. Starved for revenue and desperate for political legitimacy in the shadow of the American Revolution, the architects of the first federal government emulated the British Empire’s practices of governance that emerged after the Glorious Revolution. In the nation’s capital, Alexander Hamilton built a central Treasury and put into motion the power to tax. But in practice, at the customhouses on the maritime frontier, federal officers negotiated their authority with the merchant capitalists whose taxes would constitute the lion’s share of the federal government’s revenue. In exchange for their taxes, Atlantic merchant capitalists secured the power to shape how federal officers collected taxes and administered commercial regulations. Though this negotiated authority created reliable revenue for the new federal government, it gave merchant capitalists profound influence over the inner workings of the state. This much became clear during the Jeffersonian era, as waterfront communities thwarted the embargoes and commercial restrictions that aimed to punish European commerce during the Haitian Revolution and the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars. After the War of 1812, legal and political reformers too aimed at the moral and material problems posed by merchant capital’s ostensible capture of the federal state. By 1836, officeholders, jurists, and politicians who contemplated the problem of the customhouse had established a key axiom of the modern liberal state: the necessity of separating the state and the marketplace. The stage was set for a bureaucratic expansion of federal governance that would occur gradually over the next century.14
