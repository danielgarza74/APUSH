Primary Source P7.35
Speech to William Henry Harrison
Tecumseh (Shawnee), as printed by Samuel G. Drake · August 1810
 
TOC FIT Chapter 7 · Essay prompt 7.4 (Necessary War or War of Choice? The War of 1812)
WHY HERE In this passage Tecumseh argues that land belonged in common to all Native peoples and that no single group could sell it, and he rejects the recent treaty cession. Students can use it as evidence of Native claims and resistance on the Northwest frontier before 1812, while weighing how much of the wording belongs to Tecumseh and how much to the interpreter and to Drake.
Historical Context

Tecumseh (ca. 1768–1813), a Shawnee war leader, worked with his brother Tenskwatawa, the “Prophet,” to build a confederacy of Native nations to resist American expansion. In the Treaty of Fort Wayne (1809), Indiana territorial governor William Henry Harrison had obtained about three million acres from several tribal leaders. Tecumseh denied that they had any right to sell. In August 1810 he came to Harrison’s capital at Vincennes with several hundred warriors, and the two men held tense councils that nearly ended in violence.

Tecumseh spoke in Shawnee through interpreters, and no verbatim record of this speech survives from 1810. The words below were first published by the Boston antiquarian Samuel G. Drake in The Book of the Indians (1836), more than twenty-five years later, and Drake did not identify his source.

Editorial note: CAUTION: this text is a later editor’s rendering of an interpreted speech. It survives only through Samuel G. Drake’s The Book of the Indians (first published 1836), and Drake did not say where he obtained it. Read it as a nineteenth-century English version of Tecumseh’s argument, not as his exact words. This reader reproduces the passage from the American Yawp Reader, omitting that site’s headnote. Two slips in that transcription are corrected here: a repeated phrase (“of my own fortune; and oh! that I could make”) is removed, and “hose” is corrected to “those.” The Yawp gives Drake’s title with a subtitle running “to the year 1841,” which belongs to a later edition, alongside the date 1836 and pages 121–22, so the exact edition behind its text is uncertain. Whether Drake’s printing contains more of the speech could not be checked here.
Document Text

It is true I am a Shawnee. My forefathers were warriors. Their son is a warrior. From them I take only my existence; from my tribe I take nothing. I am the maker of my own fortune; and oh! that I could make that of my red people, and of my country, as great as the conceptions of my mind, when I think of the Spirit that rules the universe. I would not then come to Governor Harrison to ask him to tear the treaty and to obliterate the landmark; but I would say to him: “Sir, you have liberty to return to your own country.”

The being within, communing with past ages, tells me that once, nor until lately, there was no white man on this continent; that it then all belonged to red men, children of the same parents, placed on it by the Great Spirit that made them, to keep it, to traverse it, to enjoy its productions, and to fill it with the same race, once a happy race, since made miserable by the white people, who are never contented but always encroaching. The way, and the only way, to check and to stop this evil, is for all the red men to unite in claiming a common and equal right in the land, as it was at first, and should be yet; for it never was divided, but belongs to all for the use of each. For no part has a right to sell, even to each other, much less to strangers — those who want all, and will not do with less.

The white people have no right to take the land from the Indians, because they had it first; it is theirs. They may sell, but all must join. Any sale not made by all is not valid. The late sale is bad. It was made by a part only. Part do not know how to sell. All red men have equal rights to the unoccupied land. The right of occupancy is as good in one place as in another. There can not be two occupations in the same place. The first excludes all others. It is not so in hunting or traveling; for there the same ground will serve many, as they may follow each other all day; but the camp is stationary, and that is occupancy. It belongs to the first who sits down on his blanket or skins which he has thrown upon the ground; and till he leaves it no other has a right.
