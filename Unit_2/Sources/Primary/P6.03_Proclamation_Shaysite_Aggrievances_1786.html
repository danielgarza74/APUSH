A Proclamation of Shaysite Aggrievances: An Address to the People of the Several Towns in the County of Hampshire, Now at Arms
Committee of the Hampshire County insurgents, Daniel Gray, chairman · [December] 1786

WHY HERE The insurgents’ own list of complaints shows what they said they were resisting: debt collection, state tax and finance policy, and the emergency measures the Massachusetts government took against them. Students can set these grievances beside the reactions of national leaders in 1786–87 and, later, beside the complaints of the western Pennsylvania whiskey rebels.
Historical Context

In 1786 farmers in western and central Massachusetts, many of them Revolutionary War veterans, faced heavy state taxes payable in scarce hard money and lawsuits for debt. County conventions petitioned for relief, and armed crowds began closing the courts to stop foreclosures and debt suits. The legislature responded with a Riot Act and a temporary suspension of habeas corpus. This address, issued by a committee of the Hampshire County insurgents and signed by its chairman, Daniel Gray, was printed in the county’s newspaper in early December 1786, soon after the insurgents gathered at Worcester.

The text survives because George Richards Minot, clerk of the Massachusetts House of Representatives, reprinted it in his 1788 history of the rising. Minot wrote from the government’s side, and he introduced the address as evidence of the insurgents’ plans.

Document Text

An ADDRESS to the People of the several towns in the county of Hampshire, now at arms.

GENTLEMEN,

WE have thought proper to inform you of some of the principal causes of the late risings of the people, and also of their present movement, viz.

1st. The present expensive mode of collecting debts, which, by reason of the great scarcity of cash, will of necessity fill our gaols with unhappy debtors, and thereby a reputable body of people rendered incapable of being serviceable either to themselves or the community.

2d. The monies raised by impost and excise being appropriated to discharge the interest of governmental securities, and not the foreign debt, when these securities are not subject to taxation.

3d. A suspension of the writ of Habeas corpus, by which those persons who have stepped forth to assert and maintain the rights of the people, are liable to be taken and conveyed even to the most distant part of the Commonwealth, and thereby subjected to an unjust punishment.

4th. The unlimited power granted to Justices of the Peace and Sheriffs, Deputy Sheriffs, and Constables, by the Riot Act, indemnifying them to the prosecution thereof; when perhaps, wholly actuated from a principle of revenge, hatred and envy.

Furthermore, Be assured, that this body, now at arms, despise the idea of being instigated by British emissaries, which is so strenuously propagated by the enemies of our liberties: And also wish the most proper and speedy measures may be taken, to discharge both our foreign and domestick debt.

Per Order,

DANIEL GRAY, Chairman of the Committee; for the above purpose.
