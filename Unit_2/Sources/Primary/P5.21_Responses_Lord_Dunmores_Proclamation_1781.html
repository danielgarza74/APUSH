Responses to Lord Dunmore’s Proclamation: Washington’s After Orders at Yorktown
George Washington · October 25, 1781
 
TOC FIT Chapter 5 · Essay prompt 5.3 (Whose Liberty? The Revolution and Slavery)
WHY HERE Six days after the British surrender at Yorktown, the commander of the Continental Army issues orders about the Black men and women who had sought refuge with the British. Students can examine what the orders assume about property and freedom, what they reveal about the actions enslaved people were taking, and how the victorious army proposed to respond.
Historical Context

In November 1775 Lord Dunmore, Virginia’s royal governor, promised freedom to enslaved people of rebel owners who would join British forces. Across the war, and especially during the British campaigns in the South after 1779, thousands of enslaved people fled to British lines; in 1781 many followed Lord Cornwallis’s army through Virginia, and some who left Mount Vernon that spring belonged to Washington himself.

Cornwallis surrendered at Yorktown and Gloucester on October 19, 1781. Washington issued these “After Orders” from headquarters near York on October 25 as a supplement to the routine general orders of the day. They direct officers of the allied French and American armies to deliver claimed fugitives to guards at the two posts, name David Ross to issue passes returning them to their owners, and refer to an earlier order of October 9 requiring officers to report Black people in their possession. The orders show the army’s intentions; they do not by themselves show how many people were actually returned.

Editorial note: Norton’s document 2.10 groups three responses to Dunmore’s Proclamation. The Norton Source Collection prints Washington’s After Orders from John C. Fitzpatrick, ed., The Writings of George Washington, vol. 23, pp. 264–65; this reader reproduces the same document from the Early Access transcription of the General Orders of October 25, 1781, on Founders Online. The routine orders of the day that precede the After Orders (parole, countersigns, officers of the day, guard and fatigue details) and the separate closing line prohibiting the discharge of firearms near camp are omitted. The other two Norton components are not reproduced because no open edition was located: the report from Williamsburg dated November 29, 1775, printed in the Pennsylvania Gazette of December 13, 1775; and Thomas Walker Jr.’s legislative petition of October 23, 1779, seeking freedom for William Beck (Library of Virginia, Legislative Petitions). Document Text
George Washington, After Orders, October 25, 1781

After Orders.

It having been represented that many Negroes and Mulattoes the property of Citizens of these States have concealed themselves on board the Ships in the harbor, that some still continue to attach themselves to British Officers and that others have attempted to impose themselves upon the officers of the French and American Armies as Freemen and to make their escapes in that manner. In order to prevent their succeeding in such practices All Officers of the Allied Army and other persons of every denomination concerned are directed not to suffer any such negroes or mulattoes to be retained in their Service but on the contrary to cause them to be delivered to the Guards which will be establish’d for their reception at one of the Redoubts in York and another in Gloucester. Mr David Ross will have the superintendency and will give passes to enable them to return to their Masters or where that is not practicable will have directions to make other provision for them. Any Negroes or mulattoes who are free upon proving the same will be left to their own disposal.

The Gentlemen of the American Army who have made return to the Orderly Office of negroes in their possession agreeably to the Order of the 9th instant are desired to deliver them to the above mentioned Mr David Ross this day or tomorrow.

The General Officer of the day is requested to establish a Guard in York and the Commandant of Gloucester another at that post for the reception of negroes agreeably to the above order.
