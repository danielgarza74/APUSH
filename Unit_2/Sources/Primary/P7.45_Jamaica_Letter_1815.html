The Jamaica Letter (“Reply of a South American to a Gentleman of This Island”)
Simón Bolívar · September 6, 1815
 
TOC FIT Chapter 7 · Background document for the unit (not assigned to an essay prompt)
WHY HERE Written in exile as the Spanish American revolutions faltered, the letter lets students compare how a South American revolutionary described colonial rule, Europe’s obligations, and the prospects for republican government with the arguments and outcomes of the North American Revolution and early republic.
Historical Context

Simón Bolívar, a Venezuelan-born creole leader of the independence movement in northern South America, wrote this letter in Kingston, Jamaica, in September 1815, after royalist forces had driven him from New Granada. Spain, freed from the Napoleonic wars, was sending an expeditionary army to reconquer its American provinces. The letter answers questions put to Bolívar by a British resident of the island, usually identified as the merchant Henry Cullen, and was evidently meant for a wider British and European audience whose help Bolívar hoped to win.

Bolívar surveys the state of the insurrections from Mexico to the Río de la Plata, indicts three centuries of Spanish rule, and speculates about the governments the new states would adopt. His doubts about federalism and about a single American republic contrast with the choices made in the United States; his references to Europe show how he framed independence as a matter of international interest.

Editorial note: The Norton Source Collection prints this letter in Lewis Bertrand’s 1951 English translation, which is protected; this reader instead gives a new translation by the compiler of the same passages Norton selects, made from the Spanish text. The letter’s textual history is complicated. Bolívar’s original manuscript has not survived; the letter first appeared in print in English, in Jamaica, in 1818, and the Spanish text was first published in 1833 in Francisco Javier Yanes and Cristóbal Mendoza’s Colección de documentos relativos a la vida pública del Libertador. The translation follows the version Wikisource labels “auténtica,” copied by Vicente Lecuna from the 1833 Yanes–Mendoza edition. The heading is the one printed in the 1833 edition; bracketed omission marks show where passages are left out. The translation aims to be literal rather than polished, keeping Bolívar’s long periodic sentences and his quotations from his correspondent’s letter.
Document Text

REPLY OF A SOUTH AMERICAN TO A GENTLEMAN OF THIS ISLAND

Kingston, September 6, 1815.

[. . .]

“Three centuries ago,” you say, “began the barbarities that the Spaniards committed in the great hemisphere of Columbus.” These are barbarities that the present age has rejected as fables, because they seem beyond human depravity; and modern critics would never believe them if constant and repeated documents did not bear witness to these unhappy truths. The philanthropic bishop of Chiapas, the apostle of America, Las Casas, left to posterity a brief account of them, drawn from the summary proceedings brought against the conquistadors in Seville, with the testimony of every respectable person then in the New World, and with the very suits that the tyrants brought against one another, as the most eminent historians of that time record. All impartial persons have done justice to the zeal, truthfulness, and virtues of that friend of humanity, who with such fervor and firmness denounced before his government and his contemporaries the most horrifying acts of a bloodthirsty frenzy.

With what emotion of gratitude I read the passage in your letter in which you tell me “that you hope that the success which then attended Spanish arms may now attend those of their adversaries, the much-oppressed South Americans”! I take this hope as a prediction, if justice decides the contests of men. Success will crown our efforts, because the destiny of America has been irrevocably fixed; the tie that bound it to Spain is cut; opinion was its whole strength; through opinion the parts of that immense monarchy were drawn close to one another; what once joined them now divides them; greater is the hatred that the Peninsula has inspired in us than the sea that separates us from it; it is less difficult to join the two continents than to reconcile the spirits of the two countries. The habit of obedience; an exchange of interests, of learning, of religion; a mutual goodwill; a tender concern for the birthplace and the glory of our fathers; in short, everything that formed our hope came to us from Spain. From this arose a principle of attachment that seemed eternal, even though the conduct of our rulers weakened this sympathy, or, better said, this attachment compelled by the force of domination. At present the opposite is the case: death, dishonor, everything harmful threatens us and we fear it; we suffer all of it from that unnatural stepmother. The veil has been torn; we have already seen the light, and they wish to return us to darkness; the chains have been broken; we have already been free, and our enemies seek to enslave us again. Therefore America fights in desperation; and seldom has desperation failed to bring victory in its train.

[. . .]

This picture represents a military theater two thousand leagues in length and nine hundred in breadth at its greatest extent, in which sixteen million Americans are defending their rights or are oppressed by the Spanish nation, which, though it was once the vastest empire in the world, is now reduced to remnants powerless to rule the new hemisphere or even to maintain itself in the old. And does civilized Europe, commercial and a lover of liberty, permit an old serpent, merely to satisfy its venomous rage, to devour the fairest part of our globe? What! Is Europe deaf to the cry of its own interest? Has it no longer eyes to see justice? Has it grown so hardened as to be thus insensible? The more I ponder these questions, the more they confound me: I come to think that the aim is for America to disappear; but that is impossible, because all Europe is not Spain. What madness on the part of our enemy, to aim at reconquering America without a navy, without a treasury, and almost without soldiers! For those she has are scarcely enough to keep her own people in forced obedience and to defend herself against her neighbors. Besides, can this nation carry on the exclusive commerce of half the world without manufactures, without products of the soil, without arts, without sciences, without statecraft? Were this mad enterprise accomplished, and supposing further that pacification were achieved, would not the children of the present Americans, joined with those of the European reconquerors, form again within twenty years the same patriotic designs that are now being fought?

Europe would do Spain a service by dissuading her from her obstinate rashness; for at the least it would spare her the expense she lays out and the blood she sheds, so that, fixing her attention within her own borders, she might found her prosperity and power on firmer bases than those of uncertain conquests, a precarious commerce, and violent exactions from remote, hostile, and powerful peoples. Europe herself, from motives of sound policy, ought to have prepared and carried out the project of American independence, not only because the balance of the world requires it, but because this is the legitimate and sure means of acquiring overseas commercial establishments. Europe, which is not agitated by the violent passions of vengeance, ambition, and greed, as Spain is, seems to have been authorized by every law of equity to enlighten her as to her own true interests.

[. . .]

How different it was among us! We were harassed by a conduct that, besides depriving us of the rights that belonged to us, left us in a kind of permanent infancy with respect to public affairs. If we had at least managed our domestic concerns in our internal administration, we would know the course of public business and its workings, and we would also enjoy the personal consideration that commands in the eyes of the people a certain mechanical respect that is so necessary to preserve in revolutions. That is why I said that we were deprived even of active tyranny, since we were not permitted to exercise its functions.

Americans, under the Spanish system now in force, and perhaps with greater force than ever, hold no other place in society than that of serfs fit for labor, or at most that of mere consumers; and even this role is curtailed by shocking restrictions: such are the prohibitions on cultivating European crops, the monopoly on products that the King reserves to himself, the ban on factories that the Peninsula itself does not possess, the exclusive privileges of trade even in articles of prime necessity, and the barriers between one American province and another, so that they may not deal with, understand, or trade with one another. In short, do you wish to know what our destiny was? The fields, to cultivate indigo, cochineal, coffee, sugarcane, cacao, and cotton; the lonely plains, to raise cattle; the wilderness, to hunt wild beasts; the bowels of the earth, to dig the gold that cannot satisfy that greedy nation.

[. . .]

From all that has been set forth we may draw these conclusions: the American provinces are fighting to emancipate themselves; in the end they will succeed; some will constitute themselves in regular fashion as federal and central republics; monarchies will almost inevitably be founded in the large sections; and some will be so unfortunate as to devour their own elements, whether in the present or in future revolutions; for a great monarchy will not be easy to consolidate, and a great republic will be impossible.

It is a grandiose idea to seek to form the whole New World into a single nation, with a single bond joining its parts to one another and to the whole. Since it has one origin, one language, one set of customs, and one religion, it ought, consequently, to have a single government confederating the different states that are to be formed; but this is not possible, because distant climates, diverse situations, opposing interests, and dissimilar characters divide America. How fine it would be if the Isthmus of Panama were for us what the Isthmus of Corinth was for the Greeks! Would that some day we may have the good fortune to install there an august congress of the representatives of the republics, kingdoms, and empires, to deal with and discuss the high interests of peace and war with the nations of the other three parts of the world. A corporation of this kind may come about in some happy epoch of our regeneration; any other hope is groundless, like that of the Abbé St. Pierre, who conceived the laudable delusion of assembling a European congress to decide the fate and the interests of those nations.
