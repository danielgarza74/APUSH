Townshend Acts and Nonimportation

This tobacco mentality colored Virginians’ opinions of British taxes. Beginning in the 1740s, planters engaged in a transatlantic consumer revolution that brought a flood of clocks, looking glasses, carriages, jewelry, and silverwork from England to the colony. However, much of this extravagance was funded by speculation on future tobacco sales, such that between 1766 and 1776, the debts of Chesapeake planters doubled. The situation became dire in 1772 when a global panic depressed tobacco prices. The planters blamed greedy merchants and aspirational lower classes for the situation, but some also looked inward at their own luxurious lives. Many saw a reduction of British imports as salutary for the morals of the colony and their own pocketbooks, so they supported the Virginia Association. However, the great planters remained skeptical of rabble-rousers who envisioned a more radical change for Virginia.

The coming of the American Revolution in Virginia was felt differently by the diverse elements of the colony. In 1770, there were approximately 500,000 people in the colony, but only about 10 percent were gentry. The rest of the white population consisted of small farmers, artisans, traders, and overseers; 40 percent of colonists were enslaved African Americans. Most colonists lived in the Tidewater region where tobacco dominated, but a growing number moved into the Piedmont and established farms there. Some pushed beyond the Blue Ridge Mountains into what is now West Virginia and Kentucky, where they encountered Indigenous hunters.

African Americans lived under an oppressive regime that denied them freedom and civil liberties. Nearly all of the Black people in Virginia were enslaved. Nevertheless, people of African descent built a distinctive culture, especially as the native-born reached four-fifths of Virginia’s Black population in the middle of the eighteenth century. Many African Americans participated in the religious revivals that heralded the Great Awakening, and they built a distinctive version of Christianity. On the eve of independence, enslaved Virginians pressed their enslavers for a free Sunday and greater protections for their families. Yet the economic turmoil caused by the colony’s overreliance on tobacco also destabilized Black lives. As the profitability of tobacco declined, many African Americans were transferred to wheat farms or urban trades.

Whites calling for the abolition of slavery were a distinct minority in late colonial Virginia and were mostly confined to the Society of Friends (Quakers). However, the nonimportation movement prompted the House of Burgesses to pass Virginia’s first laws interfering with slavery. In 1767 and 1769, the assembly doubled the duty on enslaved people, in part to reduce planters’ debts and drive up the price of tobacco. However, the Privy Council disallowed these duties, further angering Virginians over Britain’s control of their laws.

The imperial crisis worried the large planters who feared that a breakdown of patriarchal authority would undermine the institution of slavery. When the carelessness of an enslaved gardener named Johnny led to the destruction of a field on Landon Carter’s plantation, Carter berated the enslaved man, but Johnny replied, “He did not care.” Similarly, when a thrashing left Tony the carpenter unable to work, Carter grew exasperated, writing in his diary, “I might as well give up every Negroe if I submit to this impudence.” Nor were the planters’ fears unfounded, as several enslaved people took to heart the claims of freedom and used the opportunity to liberate themselves. In 1774, Isaac Bee, who was enslaved at Kingsmill, forged a pass, and escaped because he “thinks he has a Right to his Freedom.”

Non-elite whites also struggled with debt, especially those who planted tobacco in the Tidewater. Landless whites and the poor suffered hunger and homelessness, yet racism kept them from making common cause with Blacks. For those without great estates, the promise of Virginia lay in the Piedmont and beyond. Non-elite whites headed west beginning in the 1720s, often relocating without enslaved laborers. By 1763, more than 20,000 colonists lived west of the Blue Ridge Mountains, where they planted hemp, flax, and wheat instead of tobacco.

Ethnically and religiously, western Virginia stood apart from the Tidewater. The Shenandoah Valley was settled by Palatinate Germans from the Rhine Valley region and Ulster Scots, who brought their own distinctive versions of Protestantism with them. In 1776, the Shenandoah Valley hosted twice as many Presbyterian churches as Anglican ones, even though the latter were tax-supported; there were many Lutheran and German Reformed congregations as well. In the 1740s, the Great Awakening began in western Virginia with the evangelical preaching of itinerants like Samuel Davies. By 1774, there were fifty-four separate Baptist churches with 4,000 members in Virginia.

Politically, small planters adhered to the Tidewater elites, although the imperial crisis exposed friction among the colony’s white population. During the boycotts, small planters ignored the elites’ efforts to ban the importation of enslaved laborers. Class divisions mapped onto religious differences as the elites promoted an Anglican bishopric for America as a means of countering evangelicalism. Conversely, Baptists criticized Virginia’s noncompliance with Britain’s Act of Toleration and the jailing of several Baptist preachers.

The westward movement of colonists led to new conflicts with Virginia’s Indigenous peoples, especially the Shawnee, Mingo, and Lenape (Delaware). Many of these people had fought against Britain in the Seven Years’ War, although peace was secured through the Proclamation of 1763 and treaties negotiated at Fort Pitt in 1765. However, official prohibitions did not prevent hunters, squatters, and land speculators from heading west of the Appalachian Mountains. Violence followed as natives and newcomers raided each other’s towns, making the region “bloody Ground” according to Cherokee leader Dragging Canoe.
