The Proclamation

As enslaved people sought to turn the political chaos in the colony to their advantage, some white Patriots assured one another that their enslaved servants remained faithful, blaming the Loyalists for those who decamped to the British. Writing from Williamsburg, Robert Carter Nicholas reported that white “Tories of Norfolk are said to be the Ringleaders” of any movement to arm Virginia’s enslaved laborers. Although rumors held that “great Numbers of Slaves from different Quarters have graced their Corps,” Nicholas insisted that Dunmore was “using every Art to seduce the Negroes.” Collective self-deception, however, could not disguise the fact that many bondsmen within distance of British lines “flocked” to them. In early December, Virginia militiamen captured “Negro George,” who had fled his enslaver’s plantation near Suffolk. Under questioning, George confessed that “there are 400 Blacks” in Norfolk and that the runaways had been busy building an entrenchment.

Enslavers more realistic than Nicholas feared the worst. From Philadelphia, Thomas Jefferson wrote to his wife, Martha, warning her to remain as far inland as possible. Jefferson begged his brother-in-law Francis Eppes to take charge and ensure that his wife and their three-year-old daughter, Patsy, kept at “a distance from the alarms of Ld. Dunmore.” Jefferson’s anxiety about his wife and daughter appeared to reflect longstanding concerns that enslaved men sought not only liberty but white women. For his part, George Washington feared the impact of Dunmore’s proclamation on the emerging military situation. “If that Man is not crushed before Spring, he will become the most dangerous man in America,” he warned fellow Virginia planter Henry Lee. “Success will depend on which side can arm the Negroes the faster.”

As word of Dunmore’s proclamation rapidly spread beyond the Chesapeake, Washington’s concerns were proven accurate. In Monmouth County, New Jersey, Titus, a twenty-one-year-old farmhand enslaved by John Corlies, threw down his hoe and began walking toward Williamsburg only days after Dunmore issued his proclamation. He joined the Ethiopian Regiment before returning to New Jersey, where he became a feared guerrilla fighter going by the name of Colonel Tye. Operating out of a forested base called Refugeetown on Sandy Hook, Tye led a series of surprise raids across northern New Jersey that targeted wealthy, slaveholding Patriots.

Dunmore’s proclamation had other effects, pushing some Virginians who had been on the fence toward the Patriot cause. Richard Henry Lee wrote, “Lord Dunmore’s unparalleled conduct in Virginia has, a few Scotch excepted, united every Man in that large Colony.”

By early December, “the king of the blacks,” as Pinkney’s Virginia Gazette branded Dunmore, sent his forces, including the Ethiopian Regiment, marching south to Great Bridge, a chokepoint on the road from the Virginia interior to Norfolk. There they faced off against Patriot troops in the Battle of Great Bridge on December 9, which resulted in a stunning defeat for British and Loyalist forces. Most of the Ethiopian Regiment did not participate in the battle, having been deployed to another nearby crossing. According to some reports, the few former bondsmen present wore sashes emblazoned with the words “Liberty to Slaves.”

With Black refugees flooding out of the counties near Norfolk, enslavers tried a variety of measures, including propaganda, to curtail the nightly disappearances. Robert Carter, one of the largest enslavers in the colony, assembled his human property at his Coles Point quarters and warned them of Dunmore’s perfidy. The governor promised freedom for those who fought, Carter cautioned, but actually schemed to sell them to enslavers in the West Indies. According to Carter, the Black men uniformly vowed to take their wives and children and flee into the woods at the first sight of a British vessel. Pleased by this response, Carter was later stunned to learn that the moment a British privateer landed near Coles Point, thirty-two enslaved people placed themselves under the care of Dunmore’s forces.

Try as they might, enslavers were unable to prevent news of the larger world from reaching their enslaved workers, in part because they craved information, but also because their plantations were not as isolated as they appeared. Because of the need to roll the enormous hogsheads of tobacco onto waiting vessels, most large estates were situated along Virginia’s inland waterways, where enslaved people heard stories of foreign affairs. In light of the 1772 Somerset case—in which the English Court of King’s Bench ruled that an enslaved person on English soil could not be forcibly removed from Britain—it was not difficult for enslaved people to believe that Dunmore’s offer of freedom was sincere. Nine enslaved people from southern Maryland set sail for Norfolk in an open boat and three more boarded what they erroneously took for a British vessel, swearing to shed “the last drop of their blood in Dunmore’s service.” In early January 1776, Pinkney’s Virginia Gazette reported that Virginia militiamen had captured “upwards of thirty of Jack Dunmore’s hopeful gang.” When returned to their enslavers, many simply tried again. The fact that British soldiers were increasingly willing to encourage enslaved people “to leave their masters, and take up arms against them,” as “An American” wrote in Purdie’s Virginia Gazette in late March 1776, only convinced irresolute enslavers of the need to take up arms in what the writer called this “most just and holy war.”

By the time that Dunmore abandoned the Norfolk area for New York City in early 1776, roughly 300 liberated Black Virginians sailed with him. Three years later, British general Henry Clinton issued the Philipsburg Proclamation, which freed people enslaved by white Patriots throughout the rebellious colonies including the wives and children of Black combatants. At the war’s end in November 1783, the British Navy ferried precisely 3,000 African Americans from Manhattan to Novia Scotia. Roughly half of the refugees had fled the coastal areas between Maryland and South Carolina, largely due to Dunmore’s proclamation.
