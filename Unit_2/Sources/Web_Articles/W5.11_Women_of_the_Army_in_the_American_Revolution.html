Women of the Army

Following its establishment of an army in 1775, the Continental Congress defined camp followers in the 1776 American Articles of War as “all suttlers and retainers to a camp, and all persons whatsoever serving with the armies of the United States.” Sutlers were vendors officially licensed to sell goods to soldiers within a camp or on campaign; retainers were family members, servants, and volunteers; and those “serving with the armies” included civilian employees such as nurses. Most female camp followers were retainers, generally the wives of soldiers, but some women were also employed by the army as paid nurses, and a small number worked as sutlers.

American commanders requested written records of the number of dependent women attached to each regiment throughout the war. Based on these records, historian John Rees estimates that over the course of the war, the average number of female camp followers attached to the Continental Army was one for every thirty men, or 2 to 3 percent of the army.

It’s not possible to know how many Virginia women served as women of the army throughout the war. Many records were lost or are incomplete, and the number of camp followers fluctuated, with more women joining camps in winter when troops were not actively campaigning. According to Rees, returns from Washington’s Middlebrook, New Jersey, winter camp in April and May of 1779 show that the number of women attached to General Peter Muhlenberg’s 8th Virginia Regiment, General William Woodford’s 2nd Virginia Regiment, and General Charles Scott’s 5th Virginia Regiment fluctuated from a low of 44 women with Muhlenberg’s brigade in April to a high of 104 women with Woodford’s brigade in May, for an average of about 72 camp followers attached to each of the three regiments. Overall, hundreds of Virginia women likely served as women of the army.

Although General Washington considered women to be a “clog” on the efficiency of the army, especially if they were pregnant or traveling with children, he also recognized that the presence of wives was necessary for morale. His wife, Martha Washington, joined him at most of his winter quarters. Moreover, many soldiers did not have the means to support their families, and Washington explained in 1783 that when faced with the hungry families of soldiers whose homes had been destroyed by the British he was “obliged to give Provisions to the extra Women … or loose by Desertion … some of the oldest and best Soldiers in the Service.” Frontier violence in the west or British raids in the east may have led some Virginia soldiers’ wives to become camp followers.

Finally, as historian Holly Mayer notes, “Camp followers were necessary to the survival of the military.” Female camp followers helped with the day-to-day tasks of maintaining the army. They cooked, cleaned, and mended clothes, and cared for the ill and injured. The Continental Army was perpetually low on supplies, making women’s labor as laundresses all the more important for keeping soldiers’ clothing clean, which helped to curb typhus, a common disease known as “camp fever,” and other diseases carried by pests such as fleas and lice.

Camp followers had a variety of effects on the Commonwealth and the Continental Army. For instance, noncombatants’ movements could cause consternation in communities. In 1777, “the great Resort of Soldiers to the City of Williamsburg & it’s environs” caused the vestrymen of Bruton Parish Church to petition the House of Burgesses to ensure that the parish would not be responsible for providing economic relief to the “necessary Attendants on the Camp, most of whom are the poorest Sort of our People.” Yet these “necessary Attendants” performed labor that was crucial to sustaining Virginia soldiers.

Dependent women attached to the Continental Army typically received one ration a day, and unless they were specifically employed by the Continental Army as laundresses, nurses, or cooks, camp followers did not earn money for their labor. Some enslaved Virginia women had no choice but to become camp followers. Free servant women, both white and Black, accompanied their employers on campaigns as well. Newspaper runaway advertisements attest to the fact that enslaved and indentured women also voluntarily “went to camp” to follow the army.

Women camp followers were subject to army regulations and threatened with being turned out of encampments if they misbehaved. Washington issued twenty-five orders during the war addressing women camp followers. When on campaign, women were expected to travel with the army’s baggage so that the troops could move in a quicker and more orderly fashion. Women riding in the wagons intended for baggage was a perpetual matter of concern for Washington and his commanders, who worried about exhausting the horses and encumbering the supply chain. Most women camp followers had to walk, although orders given by Washington in New York in 1779 indicate that a few women camp followers did have horses of their own and were allowing them to graze on private property contrary to orders.

Soldiers and unmarried female camp followers were also examined by army surgeons for venereal disease. Orderly books from the Virginia forces encamped around the Virginia Peninsula in the spring and summer of 1776 record concern over the “numbers of women follow[ing] the Camp suspected to have the Veneral Disorder.” Officers ordered surgeons to examine them. Returns from a military hospital in Pittsylvania County in 1781 show a total of seven women treated for various ailments, including at least two treated for venereal disease and one who was pregnant.
