Selection Overview

Bode examines the Southern white Protestant doctrine of the spiritual nature of the church, which confined religion to saving souls and rejected social criticism as a Northern principle. He argues that this avoidance of politics had political consequences, making the church a powerful bulwark of the existing Southern order, the setting for the Populist critique in North Carolina that he studies.

Josephus Dan- iels, who since 1894 had made the News and Observer into the most influential newspaper in North Carolina, was an active lay- man in the Methodist church and a passionate advocate of the supremacy of the Democratic party in his state.2 The words he penned in 1903 expressed with unmistakable clarity not only the sentiments of most loyal churchmen in the South but also the as- sumptions that the region's rulers since the days of slavery had made about the proper sphere of religion. Southern white Protestantism traditionally adhered to the so- called doctrine of the spiritual nature of the church, which preachers had elaborated under the impact of the sectional con- troversy over slavery. The doctrine dictated that religion's pri- mary task wag a narrowly conceived evangelicalism. The church was supposed to have nothing to do with the life of the Christian in this world except as his activity related directly to his life in the world to come. Preachers exhorted sinners to seek salvation and rebuked individual breaches of the moral law. They believed that the concept of social ethics contradicted the spiritual nature of the church and that the church must shun anything that smacked of social criticism. They accused their northern colleagues of perverting the true Christian mission from saving souls to saving society. Long after the Civil War southern- ers continued to denounce the notion that the church had an obligation to "interfere" in secular matters as the "Northern prin- ciple" which made for the most "radical difference" between the Protestantism of the two sections.3 This critical difference, then, was what Daniels had in mind when he spoke of the "old-fash- ioned preaching of the old-fashioned gospel." In practice, however, the spirituality doctrine had profound social implications. Since few ministers questioned the basic as- sumptions of the social, political, and economic order in the South, the church became in effect a powerful bulwark of exist- ing institutions. The fact that conscious avoidance of politics had political consequences troubled very few. Moreover, southerners generally regarded the church as an institution so closely knitted into the fabric of the region's life that its very "purity" (as a "spiritual" church) paradoxically sanctioned cultural values. Southern traditions had such strongly moral connotations that to uphold them seemed not to violate the spirituality doctrine at all. To support consensual attitudes that were taken to be a part of the natural, divinely ordained scheme of things did not appear to involve meddling in politics. The southern "sects" thus assumed (according to Ernst Troeltsch's typology) the function of a "church" in a very real sense.
