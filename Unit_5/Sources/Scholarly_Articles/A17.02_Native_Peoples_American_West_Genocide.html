THE NATIVE PEOPLES OF THE AMERICAN WEST: GENOCIDE OR ETHNIC CLEANSING? GARY CLAYTON ANDERSON The crimes committed against American Indians have been a prominent part of the historiography of North America. More recently, the problem has been defining these crimes. This article attempts to establish a workable definition that can be applied to the period of contact and, in particular, to the American West. If western history has a common thread, it is that conquest and violence went hand in hand. This is not to say that American history east of the Mississippi River is devoid of such a theme; the despicable treatment of black slaves in the South, the Irish in northern cities, or even specific Native groups them- selves belies such an argument. But in the West, government officials, army officers, and common settlers often found themselves in disputes with American Indians, and this conflict is at the core of hundreds of books and studies. Indian tribes lost most of these contests, so it is crucial that historians look at the nature of the conflicts and try to under- stand what they meant—then and now. In many cases, if not most, non-Indians commit- ted crimes against Indians. The question here is what should we call those crimes? Beginning in the late 1980s, professors such as David E. Stannard, Ward Churchill, and Russell Thornton argued that what happened to the Indian resembled the “holocaust” and that it led to “genocide.” 1 Other colonial historians, including Laurence M. Indian-white conflict existed for many decades in the West, especially in California; perhaps this is where the best case might be made for genocide. Recent scholars who embrace this argument to some degree include Alex Alvarez, Roxanne Dunbar-Ortiz, Walter L. Hixson, Brendan L. Lindsay, and Benjamin Madley. Their work has credibility, compared with Stannard’s and Churchill’s polemic and poorly researched volumes, but also bears closer reading, especially in regard to definitions. 3 Given the proliferation of studies employing the terms genocide or ethnic cleansing, it seems important to look at the legitimacy of the claim, the direction of the debate, and perhaps where it will end up. Even in the worst cases of genocide in the twentieth century—the destruction of the Armenians, Jews, and Cambodians—people survived. Almost any definition for genocide does not require that all people of an oppressed group be murdered. But when it comes to American Indians in the West—and even some east of the Mississippi River—nearly all the major tribes and most of their popu- lations, as well as most minor tribes, have survived. Some thirty-seven recognized tribes exist today in Oklahoma, with a population of nearly 1 million Indians, and an- other twenty-two recognized tribes can be found in Arizona. The 2010 census demon- strates that New York City has the largest Native American population (111,000); Los Angeles has the second largest (54,000); Phoenix has the third largest (44,000); with Oklahoma City and Anchorage tied for the next two spots, at roughly 36,000 each. Certainly, “all Indian lives matter,” yet such numbers suggest that if genocide occurred in the United States, numbers deem it far less destructive than in other places around the world. If we examine the background of these Indian tribes and urban populations, one common thread emerges: most of them were moved at one time or another, oftentimes forcibly. Many now live on lands far distant from their homelands. The Delaware, for example, live mostly in Oklahoma.
