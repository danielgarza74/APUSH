Selection Overview

Jones argues that Thomas M. Cooley, usually cast as the chief legal champion of laissez-faire capitalism after the Civil War, deserves a different reputation. Calling "laissez-faire constitutionalism" an oversimplification, he portrays Cooley as a historically minded common lawyer devoted to the older Jeffersonian doctrine of equal rights, fittingly charged with enforcing the Interstate Commerce Act.

There is much evidence, of course, to substantiate this assumption, yet such a viewpoint oversimplifies and misleads, especially when expressed in such a phrase as "laissez-faire constitutionalism." When precisely used, laissez faire refers to a systematic economic theory; when the concept is used casually and applied to phenomena that are not strictly eco- nomic, one risks explaining away the concrete complexities of history. Thus Cooley has been explained away. Laissez faire meant different things to different men at different times. It certainly had different overtones for William Leggett, whose advocacy of the doctrine in the age of Jackson attacked class privilege and concentrated corporate power, than it had for David Brewer who applied the doctrine in the age of McKinley to defend class privilege and concentrated corporate power.4 If one chooses to use the term, Thomas Cooley might be consid- ered a proponent of Leggett's brand of laissez faire. But it would be better not to use the term at all in describing Cooley. He was an historically minded common lawyer, who was less concerned with abstract theories of economic liberty than with maintaining the older, broader, and more am- biguous doctrine of equal rights, a doctrine with deep roots in the common law and in Jeffersonian political thought. Cooley illustrates in an exception- al way the travails of the doctrine of equal rights from the age of Jackson to the age of McKinley. Seen in this light, he becomes less the villain of Progressive historiography than an ironic example of its tenet affirming a vital equalitarian tradition that stretched from Jefferson to Wilson and be- yond. It was appropriate that Cooley should have been given the responsi- bility for enforcing the Interstate Commerce Act, the major breakthrough in federal regulation of economic life. It was ironic, but still appropriate, that in post-Civil War America Cooley's continued Jeffersonianism should have been combined with a Burkean nostalgia. Cooley was forty-four years old when he published his treatise. He was then a Michigan Supreme Court Justice and a professor at the newly estab- lished law department of the University of Michigan.
