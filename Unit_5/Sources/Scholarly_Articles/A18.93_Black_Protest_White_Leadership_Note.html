MILLER Black Protest and White Leadership: A Note on the Colored Farmers' Alliance PpHE RELATIVE importance of class and race as dominant influences in American history (and as organizational concepts in American his- toriography) has long perplexed scholars, Marxists and non-Marxists alike. The notion that class interests were potentially capable of overrid- ing racial distinctions has been argued by historians as diverse as W.E.B. DuBois and C. Vann Woodward.1 However, recent historians have em- phasized the continuity and persistence of racism as a factor in American life. They have argued, with compelling evidence, that whites of diverse class and geographic and ideological orientations have believed in the ra- cial inferiority of black Americans and that it has been this economic class which has shaped the course of race relations in the United States.2 More specifically, historians studying Southern agrarianism in the late nineteenth century have taken issue with Woodward's sanguine view that in the 1890's a rudimentary form of biracial class consciousness in- fused Southern populism. William Chafe, in a study of black Populists in Kansas, has shown how blacks and whites in the Kansas Populist party differed in economic interests as well as in ideology. Both Robert Saun- ders and Charles Crowe have confronted Woodward more directly in their respective studies of Southern populism and Georgia Populist leader Thomas E. Watson. For both Saunders and Crowe, the commit- ment of white Populists to the economic and racial interests of black farmers was tangential at best.3 If historians have successfully questioned the applicability of Wood- ward's thesis for the 1890's, they have also taken issue with similar views emphasizing the predominance of common class allegiances over racial distinctions among those farmers who joined the alliance movements of the 1880's and early 1890's. Most important is an apparent anomaly: a large black organization committed to protesting both racial and economic injustices allowed whites to serve as its chief spokesmen. A brief look at this paradox can be helpful in illuminating the functional role played by white leaders in a black organization dur- ing this period. It serves simultaneously to set the Colored Farmers' Alli- ance more squarely into the context of black history. Whites were instrumental in the formation of the Colored Farmers' Al- liance from the very beginning. In fact, even before the Colored Alliance was organized, a black alliance was formed in 1882 in Prairie County, Ar- kansas, at the instigation of Milton George's Northern Farmers' Alliance. George had chartered the Arkansas alliance and other black alliances be- fore what developed into the Colored Farmers' National Alliance and Co-operative Union began with the establishment of a local alliance in Houston County, Texas, December 1886, most probably on the prompting of the Southern Alliance.5 Following the organization of the Houston County Alliance, several others were organized in the area and, at a meeting in the Good Hope Baptist Church at Weldon, Texas later in De- cember, these groups were consolidated into "The Alliance of Colored Farmers of Texas." Richard Manning Humphrey, a bearded white mis- sionary of Lovelady, Texas, was elected General Superintendent of the Texas group, which also adopted a Declaration of Principles emphasizing self-help and mutual aid. Humphrey continued in his leadership capacity when the black alliances which had been formed in various states came together at Lovelady in March of 1888 to form the Colored Farmers' Alli- ance. At this time, the white minister "of large frame and portly person" became the General Superintendent of the new body.6 Little is known of Humphrey's background. The son of immigrants from Northern Ireland, he was born in South Carolina in 1834. He served as a Captain of an Alabama Infantry Regiment during the Civil War and moved to Texas after the war ended. In Texas, he taught school, served as an unordained Baptist minister, and engaged in dissident politics.
