Selection Overview

Walden sets Booker T. Washington's rise against the erosion of Black political rights after Reconstruction, from the 1877 understanding that withdrew federal troops to the Lodge Bill's defeat and the 1894 repeal of remaining Reconstruction laws. Amid a national mood of appeasement, he argues, Washington linked industrial education with alliance with white Southerners, a course capped by the Atlanta Compromise.

THE CONTEMPORARY OPPOSITION TO THE POLITI- CAL AND EDUCATIONAL IDEAS OF BOOKER T. WASHINGTON In 1876, soon after the disputed election of that year, it was said that Reconstruction was coming to an end. Of course, the dispute raged on for a while but on March 2, 1877, an understanding was reached that permitted the election of Hayes in return for his promise to withdraw the last federal troops from South Carolina and Louisiana and to support bills for the internal improvements in the South. Seemingly, the so-called "Lost Cause" had won, for by the end of his administration, even Hayes had to admit that there was "still in our country a dangerous practical denial of the equal rights with respect to voting secured to colored citizens by the 15th amendment. '1 Two years later, Mr. Justice Harlan, dissenting in the de- cision on the Civil Rights Act of March 1, 1875, held that: since slavery ... was the moving or principal cause of the adoption of [the 13th] amendment, and since that institution rested wholly upon the inferiority, as a race, of those held in bondage, their freedom necessarily involved immunity from, and protection against, all dis- crimination against them, because of their race, in respect of such civil rights as belong to freemen of other races.2 As a result of this unhappy decision and of the federal government's attitude in the next year or so, there were many Negroes who supported Cleveland in the election of 1884. Al- though, it is true that Cleveland took cognizance of the fears of the Negro, especially in his inaugural address, it is unfor- tunate that he subsequently did virtually nothing for them. In the administration of President Harrison, however, the issues that had been so long simmering again came to the surface. In his first annual message to Congress, Dec. 3, 1889, after making clear that he expected little from the South, the Presi- dent opened up the attitude and the activity of the South to the granting of political and civil rights to the Negro. Despite his good intentions, the strength and strategy of the South de- feated measure after measure-and with the defeat of the Lodge Bill (for the federal supervision of federal elections) President Harrison admitted defeat, also. By one means or another, the vote of the Negro in the last decade of the nineteenth century had been rendered almost im- potent and his constitutional rights, already severely dimin- ished, virtually vanished with repeal of the remaining Recon- struction laws in 1894. At the same time it seems that there was a movement afoot to characterize the Southerner as a new type of man, a new generation who could now be counted on to treat the Negro fairly. Thus, in the face of the determination of the South not to grant the Negro equal rights, in view of the many political successes of the South, and with a national mood of appease- ment in the air-and after the tragic death of Frederick Douglass on February 20, 1895-the scene was set for the emergence of a new course and a new leader for the Negro. Easily the most striking thing in the history of the Ameri- can Negro since 1876 was the ascendancy of Mr. Booker T. Washington. Although free Negroes from 1830 up to the Civil War had striven to build industrial schools, and the American Missionary Association had from the first taught various trades-and some had also sought an honorable alli- ance with the best of the Southerners-it was Washington who first indissolubly linked these things.3 By virtue of his enthusiasm, unlimited energy and perfect faith, he changed what had been a bypath into a way of life. As W. E. B. Du- Bois said in 1903: To gain the sympathy and cooperation of the various elements com- prising the white South was Mr. Washington's first task; and this, at the time Tuskegee was founded [1881], seemed, for a black man, well- nigh impossible. And yet ten years later it was done in the word spoken at Atlanta: "In all things purely social we can be as separate as the five fingers, and yet one as the hand in all things essential to mutual progress."
