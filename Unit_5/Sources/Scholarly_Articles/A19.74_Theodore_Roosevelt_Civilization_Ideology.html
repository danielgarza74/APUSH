Theodore Roosevelt: Civilization as Ideology FRANK NÏNKOVICH Theodore Roosevelt was America's first modern president. Still, one may ask, how exactly was his foreign policy modern? If the modernity of h domestic progressivism seems obvious, the same is not true of his diplomac for despite pathbreaking achievements in both areas, the two have always seemed out of balance. While moral indignation, crusading zeal, and reform optimism were prominent features of his domestic reform leadership, Ro sevelt's reputation as the first modern American statesman is based, in co trast, on a macho diplomatic style and an almost indecent enthusiasm for U.S. participation in a world organized by force and power. In Howard K. Beale's classic iudsment. Roosevelt "saw world events and nolicies in terms of power. He was intrigued with power, with the problems of power, and with rivalries of power." This widely echoed assessment of his legacy has all the more resonance because Roosevelt, whose public life quickly assumed mythic proportions, always has seemed to embody America's violent rise to global prominence. Simultaneously and indistinguishably, man and nation came of age in a martial rite of passage that foreshadowed the great power concerns of America's maturity.1 Roosevelt would have been the first to dispute this historical verdict. Indeed, he proclaimed repeatedly over the course of his public life that the touchstone of his diplomacy was right rather than might. While the tradition of raison d'état afforded European statesmen a convenient exemption from the fetters of conventional moral scruples, Roosevelt always flaunted the golden rule as the standard of his statecraft, arguing that morality was appli cable in equal measure to individual and state behavior. According to William Widenor's recent assessment, Roosevelt's idealism, however sincere, "had to operate from a base of power." Even Richard Collin's revisionist cultural interpretation asserts that "Roosevelt clearly believed in the balance of power concept." That Roosevelt appreciated power is beyond dispute, but to use the most problematic term in the lexicon of politics to explain his policies is less than satisfying. Moreover, attributing his modernity to his supposed skill at power politics seems stranger still since this kind of diplomacy is perhaps the most ancient form of the art.5 Reduced to absurdity, this logic makes it seem as if TR's contribution to modernizing U.S. foreign policy consisted of traditionalizing it. If the United States had become a traditional kind of power, the logic would be appropriate. American policy in the twentieth century, however, has been impelled less by realpolitik than by a distinctly ideological and messianic thrust. From this perspective, Roosevelt's modernity must be sought, not in his thralldom to a military-strategic mentality, but in his contribution to the emergence of the liberal internationalism that dominated the policymaking of his successors. If one can believe Elihu Root, TR s secretary of state, therein lay his greatness. According to Root, Roosevelt was less a preacher of an old-time nationalist religion than a prophet of the "new diplomacy."
