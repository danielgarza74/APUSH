Selection Overview

Wright explains the Southern capture of the national cotton textile market by stressing capital accumulation and the gradual maturation of its labor force. Noting that Southern wages ran 30 to 50 percent below Northern levels as early as 1880, he asks why the triumph took fifty years, and links the Great Textile Depression of the 1920s to rising real wages in both regions.

During the last quarter of the nineteenth century, Southern cotton textiles began a steady encroachment on the national market, culminating in the collapse of the historic New England industry in the 1920s. Contemporaries believed, and historical studies have confirmed, that the cheapness of labor was the principal Southern advantage, reflected not only in lower wages but in the absence of regulations on wages, hours, child labor, and working conditions.1 As moderate a figure as Frank Taussig described the Southern expansion as "a semi-artificial and almost insensate growth, much promoted by the use of nightwork so widespread as to shame our civilization" [Taussig, 1931, p. 513]. This interpretation seems straightforward, but it leaves us with a question: why did it take fifty years for the South to triumph? With hourly wage rates 30 to 50 percent below Northern levels as early as 1880, as Lars Sandberg has written, the Southern advantage "now seems so obvious that the principal task of economic historians is to explain why it did not happen sooner" [Sandberg, 1974, p. 154]. As Figure I shows, the New England branch of the industry actually continued to grow in terms of both output and capacity up to 1920.
