Notes and Documents War-Related Deaths in the Philippines, 1898-1902 John M. Gates The author is a member of the history department in The College of Wooster. AT THE END of 1981, this author found himself engaged in an exchange of letters with Gore Vidal and others in the New York Review of Books. At issue was the question of how many Filipinos had died in the Philippine-American War, the exchange having been prompted by Vidal's preposterous statement that "some three million Filipinos" had been "slaughtered." Vidal had insuf- ficient knowledge of Philippine history to recognize that the 3,000,000 figure he had found was a misprint. His source had intended to place the number of Filipino deaths at 300,000.1 Finding that figure too low to sustain his contention that "our policy in the Philippines was genocide,"2 Vidal turned to a 1901 statement attributed to GeneralJ. Franklin Bell to argue that the Americans had killed at least 600,000 Filipinos. 368 PACIFIC HISTORICAL REVIEW The exchange of letters in the pages of the New York Review r resents only one recent example of a long-standing disagree m concerning the number of Filipinos who died as a consequenc the Philippine-American War. Because of the heated debate the nature of the U.S. Army's campaign in the Philippines, tempts to assess the Filipino loss of life began early, with a imperialists making the most of General Bell's 1901 comm that "one-sixth of the natives of Luzon have either been killed or have died of the dengue fever in the last two years."4 In a 1905 work, for example, Henry Parker Willis included both the Bell quotation and a reference to "a total death-toll of at least 600,000 persons." Willis then attempted to strengthen the case of those who claimed that the American conquest of the Philippines had been accompanied by tremendous loss of life by repeating the observation of "one unbiased Philippine observer" regarding the "disproportionate number of women and children" found in the Islands.5 With time, and repetition, the figure of 600,000 became a relatively common one, certain to appear, as it did in the New York Review, in any debate concerning the number of Filipinos to die during the Philippine-American War. The authority of Gen- eral J. Franklin Bell, who had directed the highly controversial campaign in Batangas, carried considerable weight in arguments concerning Philippine casualties. James A. LeRoy, who had worked as secretary to Governor Wil- liam Howard Taft in the Philippines, was quick to attack Willis and the anti-imperialists. In a review of Willis's book for the Politi- cal Science Quarterly, LeRoy observed that the time had come for "the quotation from 'General Bell'" to receive "its quietus." The claim that a sixth of Luzon's population had died as a result of the war, said LeRoy, was "based on an unverified newspaper inter- view, not with the well-known General James F. Bell [J.
