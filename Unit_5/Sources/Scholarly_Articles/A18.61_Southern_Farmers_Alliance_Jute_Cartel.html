The Southern Farmers' Alliance and the Jute Cartel By WwLutm F. HouLEs IN LATE JULY 1888 THE PRICE OF JurE, THE COARSE MATERIAL USED TO WRAP cotton bales, jumped from $.065 to $.107 per yard. Ten days later the price had climbed to $.125. Since wrapping one bale of cotton required approxi- mately seven yards of jute, the sudden price hike caused cotton growers to pay $.42 more per bale to prepare their crop for market. For southern farmers, who at the same time confronted profound economic difficulties, the price increase was a severe hardship. In response, the Southern Farm- ers' Alliance staged a boycott against the "Jute Trust," which contemporaries described as a sinister combination that had raised prices in order to entich its members at the expense of cotton producers. Although historians of the Alliance have addressed the boycott, they have done so briefly; conse- quently, the issue warrants more careful attention.' In addition, historians have not examined the so-called jute trust carefully enough to identify its members and to determine why it increased prices. This article will explore the subject by focusing primarily on Georgia, where the anti-jute campaign became strongest and where the Alliance set an example that other state alliances emulated. This essay will combine the methods traditionally used by historians of agrarian protest movements with the methods developed over the past three decades by business historians, particularly Alfred D. Chandler, whose works illuminate the rise of big business. The issue demonstrates the clash between agriculture and business at a pivotal point in the development of those two interest groups, and this study will explain the mutual restrictions that impinged on both businesses and agrarian protest. The expansion of cotton production in the South, as well as in other parts of the world, had driven prices down, but the operating costs that farmers confronted had steadily increased, particularly the exorbitant interest charges imposed by the crop- lien system. Caught between rising operating costs and falling commodity prices, many farmers had lost their lands and had fallen into the ranks of tenantry; others who clung to land ownership faced the threat of joining them.2 While southern cotton growers experienced continuing decline during the 1880s, that decade proved beneficial to many in the American business community. At that time, a trend, which had been underway since the 1850s, became far more pronounced as the economy changed from one in which small firms produced for local markets to one in which large businesses began operating on national and international levels. Contempo- raries frequently described the new business organizations as trusts and monopolies and charged that they used unfair methods to eliminate compe- tition. Public concern over the growth of big business quickly escalated, and antitrust sentiment reached its peak among cotton producers during the jute controversy in the late 1880s.3 The work of Chandler explains the several paths that business leaders followed in their quest for larger and more efficient forms of organization in the late nineteenth century. One route, usually described as vertical integration, arose in businesses in which a high degree of technological development contributed to cost efficiencies and in which large-scale production proved profitable. Vertically integrated firms worked to control all aspects of their businesses including the procurement of raw materials, maximum production, and a distribution network that included advertising, wholesaling, and in some instances even retailing. Under the leadership of a centralized team of professional managers, the vertically integrated busi- ness tried to operate as a single unit. However, effective vertical integration became possible for only a minority of American businesses.
