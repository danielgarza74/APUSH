Wilson, Carranza, and the Monroe Doctrine: A Question in Regional Organization MARK T. GILDERHUS Woodrow Wilson's efforts to redefine the Monroe Doctrine in multilat eral terms exhibited a principal attribute of his approach to statecraft. As an inveterate system builder and constitution writer, Wilson assumed a compati bility of interest with the nations of Latin America and attempted to create a functioning regional organization within the Western Hemisphere. The Monroe Doctrine, as traditionally understood, obstructed any such undertaking because it symbolized for Latin Americans the hegemonial and unilateral practices of the United States.1 By emphasizing natural harmonies, Wilson hoped to construct an integrated community in which the stable states of Latin America would join with the United States in exercising an international police power against disorder and intrusions. The proposed organization centered on Argentina, Brazil, and Chile, the ABC countries, and confronted them with a classic dilemma. Ever since the days of Simon Bolivar, Latin Americans had wondered whether they could risk such an association without compromising their own claims to sovereignty and independence. During the nineteenth century many favored a Pan-Hispanic alignment of Spanish-speaking states directed against the United States. When Secretary of State James G. Blaine called for the creation of a customs union and compulsory arbitration at the First International American Conference at Washington in 1889, the Latin American delegates shied away.2 Similarly, when Wilson became the champion of Pan Americanism, Venustiano Carranza, president of Mexico, feared the effects of too fond an embrace. Rather than seek new definitions of the Monroe Doctrine, he wanted an annulment. 104 DIPLOMATIC HISTORY The ensuing controversy between Wilson and Carranza over the political structure of the Western Hemisphere had important implications. As a diplo matic issue, it highlighted the complicated interplay of rival interests and ambitions separating Wilson from the Latin Americans and helped explain why his plan ultimately suffered defeat. As a historiographical issue, it revealed an aspect of Wilson's Latin American policies not fully appreciated by historians. Although scholarly literature on the subject abounds, much of it focuses on bilateral relations with specific countries and emphasizes the unilateral interventions of the United States.3 This approach has yielded rich results but also has obscured Wilson's search for an alternative. By redefining the Monroe Doctrine, he tried to create a multilateral system to secure Latin America's consent to the exercise of an international police power. Wilson aspired to close cooperation but never found the means to persuade the principal states and, indeed, hesitated to depend upon them too much in times of emergency. As many observers noted, the obligations assumed by the United States under the hallowed creed increased steadily after the war with Spain as the nation's interests expanded to the Caribbean region. What began in 1823 as a prohibition against further European colonization in the New World became, around the turn of the century, a justification for U.S. dominance. As Secretary of State Richard Olney proclaimed in 1895, "today the United States is practically sovereign on this continent, and its fiat is law upon the subjects to which it confines its interposition."
