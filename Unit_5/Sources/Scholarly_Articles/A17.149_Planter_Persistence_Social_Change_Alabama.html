Selection Overview

Wiener tests the traditional view that war and Reconstruction destroyed the South's slaveholding elite. Using census real estate values for five counties in Alabama's black belt from 1850 to 1870, he finds that the planter elite was less stable before the war and less transformed after it than assumed, and that continuities in elite mobility outweigh the changes.

Wiener Planter Persistence and Social Change: Alabama, 1850-1870 In terms of social mobility, the American South has been seen as a special case. The traditional view emphasizes the discontinuity between antebellum and post- war mobility patterns. Although there is no consensus among his- torians, the antebellum South is seen by many as a society with a relatively stable, persistent elite of slaveholding aristocrats; in the prevailing view, war and Reconstruction destroyed the elite, and brought about a sharp increase in upward mobility among urban businessmen between the end of the war and the turn of the cen- tury. This interpretation does not rest on a systematic study of southern patterns of mobility. Modern quantitative studies of the mid-nineteenth century have thus far focused almost exclusively on the Northeast and what is today the Midwest. We know about Trempealeau County, Wisconsin, and Wapello County, Iowa, and east central Kansas; we know about urban centers like Boston, Philadelphia, Poughkeepsie, and Waltham. The South is notably absent from this list. 1 The present study seeks to fill this gap. Evidence from the Alabama black belt suggests that the traditional view of the planter elite exaggerates both its antebellum stability and its postwar transformation. The continuities in pre- and post-war patterns of elite mobility are more impressive than the changes, and the similarities between southern and national patterns are more sig- nificant than the differences. The manuscript schedules of the United States census of population are an invaluable source, both for identifying the South's planter elite and for studying its transformation through the Civil War decade.
