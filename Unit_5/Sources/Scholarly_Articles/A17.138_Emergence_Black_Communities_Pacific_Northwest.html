Selection Overview

Taylor describes how roughly ten thousand Black migrants to the post-Civil War Pacific Northwest settled mainly in cities, founding churches, fraternal orders, newspapers, and businesses. Tracing Portland's community and William Gross's role in shaping Seattle's, he finds that Black settlement endured where urban economies grew but faded in mining towns such as Helena and Butte when mining declined.

342 THE EMERGENCE OF BLACK COMMUNITIES IN THE PACIFIC NORTHWEST: 1865-1910 Quintard Taylor* During the Post-Civil War period, approximately ten thousand Afro-Americans participated in the migration of two million people to the Pacific Northwest (Idaho, Montana, Oregon and Washington). However, unlike the majority of new- comers who settled in rural areas, these black migrants generally settled in cities creating the core of Afro-American community life in the region. They established churches and fraternal orders. Newspapers and other business enterprises were founded to serve a black clientele, and Afro-Americans began to respond in an organized manner to infringement of their civil rights. Many of these early efforts were weak and often failed, but they represented the initial steps in the evolution of black communities in the Pacific Northwest. Much of the early community institutional growth occurred in the larger coastal cities such as Seattle and Portland. But a surprising amount also took place in smaller towns such as Roslyn, Washington; and Helena and Butte, Montana, which through this period offered job opportuinities in domestic service, unskilled labor and some skilled trades. Once in the cities, they organized (when their population was sufficient) community institutions to establish social roots in a new and unfamiliar setting and in response to the ostracism they faced from the white population. The growth and longevity of the black population were linked to the develop- ment of the entire city. In Seattle and Portland, economic opportunity and the general attractiveness of dynamic metropolitan areas created stable and perma- nent, although slow-growing, black communities. However, in smaller cities black settlement could be short-lived. The fortunes of Helena's and Butte's black com- munities rested upon the success of mining activities. When those activities de- clined many Afro-Americans migrated to other areas. Small communities re- mained in both cities after 1910, but they no longer had their earlier vitality and dynamism. Other black communities such as Roslyn (which at one time rivaled the Seattle black community in size) disappeared entirely, while still other com- munities in Spokane, Yakima, Tacoma and Pocatello were just emerging by 1910. The region's first black community evolved in Portland in 1860 when a number of artisans and unskilled laborers settled in that city. Portland's blacks encouraged migration to their community. In 1880 they formed the Port- land Colored Immigration Society to lure blacks to the city. I By 1883, the People's Church became the First Methodist Episcopal Zion Church and eleven years later, Mount Olive Baptist Church was established.2 By 1900 Portland's 775 blacks constituted 65% of the total Oregon Afro- American population. The community was originally centered on the west bank of the Willamette River around Second Street. As in many cities in the East, this community and the "Red Light" district were in close proximity and a number of blacks were involved in gambling and prostitution.3 After 1890 the community relocated across the Willamette River because Portland's industrial district was encroaching on the older residential area. The two churches moved east of the river. They were followed by small businesses and by 1910 nearly the entire Portland black community lived east of the Willamette in what became known as the Williams Avenue district.4 Like Portland, Seattle attracted a small group of black laborers, artisans and domestic servants during the 1870s and 1880s. However, most community institu- tions did not emerge in that city until the 1890s. Their evolution can be traced to the efforts of one person-William Gross. Gross is credited with founding the first black-owned business in the city and later determining the geographical location of the city's black community. He was born in Washington, D.C. in 1835, the son of a black restaurant owner. In 1861 he moved to Seattle and opened the city's second hotel, called "Our House." The hotel was destroyed by the Seattle Fire of 1889 and Gross retired to his ranch on the outskirts of Seattle.5 In 1890 Gross received a farm east of the city in payment of a debt. He planned to cultivate the land and therefore built a house on his new property. However, as the city rapidly expanded in the 1890s, Gross sold parcels of his land to blacks who had just arrived or who had previously lived near the commercial district. A small community developed around his homestead. After 1900 increasing housing dis- crimination forced other black newcomers to live in the area. Seattle's "Central District" or black community grew from this area along the northeastern end of Madison Avenue.6 Gross became involved in the community that sprang up around him. He founded the First AME Church in 1890 and recruited its first pastor. Three years later he helped organize Mount Zion Baptist Church. Gross actively, and in some cases singlehandedly, recruited Seattle's first black doctors and lawyers. Because of his wealth and intimacy with many of Seattle's oldest families, Gross became an intermediary between the city's civic and political elite and the emerging black community, a role he held until his death in 1898.7 Black communities evolved in the region's smaller cities as well. By 1900 four Montana cities, Helena, Great Falls, Butte, and Anaconda, had sizeable popula- tions, as did the Washington cities of Spokane, Tacoma and Roslyn.
