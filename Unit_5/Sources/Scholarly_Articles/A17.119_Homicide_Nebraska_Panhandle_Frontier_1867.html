HOMICIDE ON THE NEBRASKA PANHANDLE FRONTIER, 1867–1901 ERIC MELVIN REED This article contributes to the discussion of frontier violence in two ways: first, by providing an account of homicide rates and characteristics for a large sub- region of the Great Plains heretofore unstudied in terms of homicide rates, and second by offering an integrated theory of frontier violence. I argue that the “farmer’s frontier” of northwest Nebraska was as deadly violent as almost any region of the West yet studied and that structural factors—particularly social disorganization and the predominance of a violent subculture—offer the most promising explanations for why the Nebraska panhandle frontier (and the West in general) initially experienced exceptionally high homicide rates but eventually settled into one of the most peaceful places on earth. On January 26, 1895, Pvt. Jesse Williams, an African American cavalryman stationed at Fort Robinson, Nebraska, stabbed and killed a fellow trooper, Pvt. Jesse Plouton, in a brothel over an argument about a white prostitute. In many ways, it was a typical late nineteenth-century homicide in north- west Nebraska. The participants were young, drunk, and acquainted. The context for the killing was a rural ecology that reflected an array of social disorganization factors leading to weak social controls—factors including unstable population rates, nascent and weak social institu- tions (including undeveloped and underdeveloped criminal justice systems), a low presence of women and families, and a pervasive presence of vice industries. Thanks to the efforts of many historians, we now have a growing body of litera- ture—and a growing body of datasets—with which to analyze frontier violence. A con- sensus appears to be forming around the standpoint that the U.S. West of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries was extraordinarily violent—at least in terms of homicide and at least in comparison to most developed Western nations. If one strong rebuttal remains, it stems from a gap in the scholarly literature: Much of the evi- dence showing high rates of frontier homicide comes from regions and locations where popular culture already predicts high rates of violence—namely cattle towns, mining towns, and rapidly growing urban centers with ethnically diverse populations.2 What is needed are more studies of homicide in large regions of the West unified by geogra- phy, climate, culture, and experience but not typically associated with violence. In short, we need more studies of homicide rates on the “farmer’s frontier.” In this study, I help fill that gap by investigating homicide in the Nebraska pan- handle during the first thirty-five years of predominantly white settlement. The results of my investigation suggest that the rural, ethnically homogenous, agricultural High Plains were as lethally violent as locations made famous by popular culture. In terms of homicide rates, the Nebraska panhandle frontier was more violent than any of today’s modern U.S. cities. It was also extremely violent in absolute numbers. Even excluding an Indian massacre at Fort Robinson in 1878–79, nearly twice as many people were killed in the panhandle between 1877 and 1901 as between 1977 and 2001—even though the average regional population was fewer than twenty-three thousand persons in the first period and had grown to more than ninety-three thousand persons a cen- tury later.3 My second aim in this study is to offer an integrated structural theory of frontier violence. As the consensus around frontier violence solidifies, the need for more sys- tematic explanations increases. My own belief, rooted in modern criminological con- cepts from social disorganization theory and subcultural theories of violence, is that western settlement entailed a unique form of social disorder that undermined attempts by the dominant culture to formally and informally control social behavior.
