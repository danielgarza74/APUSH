Corrothers's Literary Quest for Black Leadership KEVIN GAINES Princeton University THE DEATH OF THE AFRICAN-AMERICAN POET, AUTHOR, AND MINISTER James Corrothers in 1917 might have gone unnoticed if not for a brief, yet telling, obituary by W. E. B. Du Bois. Du Bois marked the occasion as ''a serious loss to the race and to literature," and he went on to describe Corrothers as "a man whom not only white people, but the colored people themselves did much to deprive of a real chance; yet he made his rugged way." Du Bois's eulogy described the difficulties stoically recounted in Corrothers's recently published autobiography, In Spite of the Handicap. Du Bois's tribute implied that Corrothers had been a controversial figure, an impression confirmed by his auto- biography and the rest of his varied literary output, including a curious early work entitled The Black Cat Club (1902)- a volume of humorous, journalistic sketches, featuring Negro dialect, which depicted black America in transition as rural blacks fled the New South for, among other locales, the south side of Chicago. Although that work was praised by a few black critics, Corrothers denounced it in his autobiography: "I have grown to consider the book a very poor one and regret ex- ceedingly that it was published." He justified this lapse as a crime of necessity, "with expenses to be met and the responsibility of a wife and a little son on me."' Corrothers's autobiography was his solution to the racial constraints of the literary marketplace that black writers faced at the turn of the century. In pursuit of white audiences or publication in the better paying magazines, Corrothers, like many others, was compelled to carve out, through Negro dialect, a narrow space for expression between the minstrel-derived representations of blacks in popular lit- erature and journalism and the defensive response of those black writers and spokespersons who espoused an assimilationist, developmental ideology of racial uplift. Through racial uplift ideology, elite blacks generally opposed racism by emphasizing class differences as a sign of what they called race progress and thereby constituted themselves as a "better class." Unlike most black advocates of uplift, however, Corrothers carried its logic of class stratification to divisive extremes. He used Negro dialect and minstrel images of other black men in his autobiography to validate his class superiority and fitness for leadership. Furthermore, aggression was encoded in Corrothers's use of dialect, as he deployed minstrel stereotypes of primitive, disorderly black men whom he oc- casionally mastered in his narrative through physical violence. Instead of choosing the well-worn, responsible path of racial uplift ideology over minstrel stereotypes, Corrothers's autobiography often merged the two, and he evoked minstrelsy through Negro dialect in order to affirm himself as the embodiment of assimilation and uplift. As if to atone for his self-serving use of minstrelsy, at rare moments in the autobiography-as he had done earlier in The Black Cat Club-Cor- rothers refigured minstrelsy with sympathetic representations of urban black vernacular cultures. Both unique and representative, Corrothers ' s works tell us a great deal about the intertwining of various versions of racist discourses in his day, specifically the largely unexamined minstrel nonsense residing within "respectable" race theories described by the benign concept of assimilation. Corrothers was a man of considerable energy and talent. He was born in southern Michigan in 1869 in a community founded in the antebellum period by fugitive slaves, free blacks, and abolitionists.
