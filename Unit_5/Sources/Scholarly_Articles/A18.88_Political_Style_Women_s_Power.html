Political Style and Women's Power, 1830-1930 Michael McGerr A kind of historical road construction project is underway in the study of American women's politics. The long, bumpy path of female political history-full of twists and turns and a sharp corner at the Nineteenth Amendment in 1920-is being straightened and smoothed out. The best recent work emphasizes the essential con- tinuities in the political behavior of women. Denied the vote, nineteenth-century women pioneered pressure group politics through voluntary associations outside the party system; the enfranchised women of the twentieth century, we are told, per- petuated the voluntarist style. From this perspective, suffrage and the suffrage move- ment become less significant. The road is pretty much the same before and after the vote; the voluntarist traffic hardly needs to slow down at the sign for the Nine- teenth Amendment.' Much of American women's politics in the years before 1900 and after 1920 may well be a tale of voluntarism, but the period in between is another matter. The early twentieth century was a distinctive era in the political history of women, rich in pos- sibilities, when the suffrage movement developed a variety of alternatives to the voluntarist style. After 1920, activist women rejected those alternatives in favor of pressure group lobbying. Women's politics is a story, not of continuity, but of dis- continuity. The road turned sharply in the early twentieth century- and then turned sharply again in the 1920s. Those turns raise some difficult questions. Why did the political style of the suffrage movement change so dramatically in the 1900s and 1910s? And why did most activist women in the 1920s abandon the style that had won them their great- est victory? Any answers to these questions must be speculative, but some parts of the solution appear fairly clear. The evolution of female political styles seems linked to the class origins of women activists, to the peculiar timing of the last stages of the suffrage movement, and to the development of male political styles. Women's nineteenth-century experience constrained their political choices in the early 1900s. Those turns in the road-in the first and third decades of the century-should also give a greater sense of contingency to women's political history. The voluntarist style was not an inevitability. Rather, it was one of a set of alternatives. The history of women's political styles offers more than one means for women to mobilize and seek power. While the story of female politics suggests how the past shaped women's choices, it also reveals that women had choices to make. The choices were important. We tend to dismiss style in politics. It is a negative term in contemporary political discourse, the wrong side of a dichotomy between "style" and "substance." But style-the different ways that people perceive, speak, and act politically-has critical consequences for individuals and society. For one thing, the dichotomy of style and substance is false; the two are bound together. The substance of politics - by which we usually mean major policy questions - does not exist in isolation. Issues do not appear out of thin air; they are shaped by polit- ical style, by the ways that politicians, parties, pressure groups, and media define, present, or ignore them. Political style acts as a kind of filtering lens that colors some objects, reshapes others, and obscures many altogether. Even in dismissing style, we implicitly acknowledge its mediating power. We deplore style partly because of the belief that it can deceive people and warp their decisions.2 Moreover, style not only affects the perception of substance; it is substance. The forms of politics are critical measures of social freedom and equality. As the system of symbols and institutions that bind people to politics and government, style can make it easier or harder for men and women to translate their political impulses into political action. Do media present issues in symbols and language that en- courage or discourage popular understanding? Do the rituals of elections reinforce the authority of elites or give the mass of people a sense of power? The answers to such questions help determine whether democracy is real or illusory. And in the United States over the last two centuries, those answers have varied significantly. In the nineteenth century, when political campaigns celebrated the people and in- volved regular mass demonstrations in the streets and fields, enfranchised men had a strong sense of their own authority and found it relatively easy and meaningful to vote. In the twentieth century, the dominant political styles encourage passivity; enfranchised men and women, watching television at home rather than marching in the streets, generally lack a sense of power and of their connection to politics.3 Although a useful analytical distinction, the style-substance dichotomy also car- ries with it an accumulation of unwarranted assumption and prejudice. We should remember that the notion of a dichotomy is not an immutable fact of nature, but a historical product, the outcome of American political development.
