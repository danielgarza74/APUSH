ARTICLE THE SHAPING OF THE AMERICAN LABOR MOVEMENT William E. Forbath* The great puzzle in the history of the American labor movement has been why American workers did not develop a class-based political movement like their European counterparts. In this Article, Professor Forbath takes issue with the conventional explanation for American labor's distinctive path - workers' innate acceptance of a free market ethos reinforced by their social and economic mobility. Professor Forbath argues that this explanation fails to take into account recent social history indicating that nineteenth-century American workers were neither distinctively mobile nor committed to vol- untarism. Rather, the nineteenth-century American labor movement was much like Europe's labor movements in pursuing a radical vision of social and political reform. But the turn of the century saw a change in the attitude of American trade unionists. Professor Forbath links this change to the encounters of the American labor movement with the legal system. He shows how courts' interventions against protective legislation and strikes led the labor movement to abandon its broad reform ambitions in favor of an anti- statist outlook. Anti-strike decrees made repealing the judge-made law of industrial disputes the central theme of the movement's newly narrowed political agenda. Labor leaders adopted the liberal, laissez-faire language of the law as a framework within which to contend for legitimacy and relief. They exploited the ambiguities of common law doctrine and mined the radical veins of constitutional tradition to create an alternative version of labor's rights. Workers' mass defiance of the official legal order led New Deal lawmakers to make much of labor's alternative "law" and "Constitution" their own. The reason for the paltriness of American labor law, Bok concludes, lies in the historical failure of American workers to forge a labor party or any broad, class-based political movement to press for more generous and inclusive legal protections.2 This failure is due to organized labor's historical devo- tion to voluntarism.3 What part did the legal order itself play in the story? According to Bok it was an "insignificant" one. There were, to be sure, "several unfavorable" judicial decisions in the late nineteenth and early twen- tieth centuries, but these were surface phenomena. They had no important influence on labor's political endeavors, Bok writes, because the American labor movement was born with an "individualistic" outlook; it always spurned broad, class-based reform in favor of either "individual advancement out of the working class" or cautious and pragmatic trade unionism.4 This comparatively narrow labor vision and politics sprang from deep-rooted features of American society.
