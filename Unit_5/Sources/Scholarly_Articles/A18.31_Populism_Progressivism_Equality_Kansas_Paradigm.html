GENE CLANTON POPULISM, PROGRESSIVISM, AND EQUALITY: THE KANSAS PARADIGM From the vantage point of the 1940s, William Allen White, the Kansas editor, reminisced about the People's Party in more gen terms than those he had used in the 1890s when he earned a national reputation as a Populist baiter. In his later view, the Populists had been attempting "to use government as an agency of human welfare[,] . . . to establish economic as well as political equality, to help the underdog, to cut down some of the privileges that wealth carried by reason of its size and inherent power." Populism, he said, "was all Karl Marx, highly diluted...."1 White also ascribed essentially the same meaning to the progressive movement with which he had been so conspicuously associ- ated as a Republican. In his words, "We were trying to establish . . . more equitable human relations." The progressives had been "both- ered" by the disadvantaged and "unconsciously were sucking at the pabulum of the old Greenbackers and Grangers and Populists . . . intent upon making wholesome dog biscuits for the under dog [sic]."2 It is interesting to compare White's interpretation of the meaning of Populism and progressivism with that of Jerry Simpson. When the former Populist congressman returned to Kansas from New Mexico for a visit in 1905-a point at which the new reform ferment was clearly under way throughout the nation-he momentarily found himself something of an attraction to the press. To one reporter Simpson re- marked: "I met some of my old Republican opponents to-day and they said to me: 'Oh, Jerry, you ought to be in Kansas now. Kansas is all Populist now.'
