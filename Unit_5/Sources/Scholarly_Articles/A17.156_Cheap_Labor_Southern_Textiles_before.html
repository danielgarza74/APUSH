Selection Overview

Wright asks why early cotton textile manufacturing flourished in the North rather than the South, where wages appeared lower. Setting aside New South writers who blamed plantation slavery, he argues that before about 1875 the region generally lacked truly cheap labor, because what mattered was the quality of workers attracted and the geographic scope of labor markets, not wage levels alone.

Cheap Labor and Southern Textiles before 1880 GAVIN WRIGHT Labor costs historically have been decisive in determining the location of cotton tex- tile production. Despite an apparent advantage in wage rates, however, the southern industry did not achieve sustained relative progress before about 1875. This study argues that in most times and places the region did not have "cheap labor" before this date. What matters is not just the level of wages in any year, but the quality of labor attracted at this wage and the geographic scope of the labor market within which firms operate. The scope of the labor market depends in turn on property rights and incentives toward recruitment activity. W^], HY did the early cotton textile industry achieve such success in the U.S. North and not in the South? To Albert Bolles, writing his In- dustrial History in the 1870s, southern advantages seemed so numerous that the question defied an economic answer: Nine-tenths of the new factories were put up in New England, New York, and Pennsylva- nia. That was not the part of the United States in which the manufacture could be carried on to the best advantage. The climate was dry and cold, entailing a large expense in warming and steaming the air of the mills. Wages were high in that part of the country. The factories were situated many hundreds of miles away from the cotton growing re- gions, entailing another large expense... The better place for the factories would have been in the Southern states themselves. There the climate was mild, the wages of free la- bor were low, baling, hooping, and pressing would have been almost entirely avoided, and transportation would have been only a nominal charge. The waterpower of the South was as abundant and cheap, too, as that of the North. In the North, however, the population was denser, the climate was more invigorating, and the spirit of industry has taken posses- sion of the people... The South preferred the charms and independence of the agreeable agricultural life. In numerous publications in state historical journals, Richard W. Griffin and others have documented the emergence and growth of an antebellum southern cotton industry, and have shown that there were basic continuities in location and management between these origins and the postwar developments.6 It would be difficult to deny the force of this accumulated evidence, but at least three relevant questions have not yet been answered clearly: (1) Why was the South a full genera- tion behind the Northeast in cotton textile development in the early nine- teenth century? (2) Why, after a promising surge during the 1840s, did southern cotton textiles make virtually no further progress during the 1850s? (3) Why did it take ten to fifteen years after the Civil War for the southern industry to begin its accelerated growth?
