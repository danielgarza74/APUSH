Selection Overview

Painter argues that one phase of the 1879 Exodus to Kansas, the Kansas Fever Exodus, was millenarian: freedpeople equated slavery with damnation and expected the federal government to carry them to free land in Kansas. She describes Exodusters crowding Mississippi River landings that spring, fleeing feared reenslavement, until steamboat captains pressured by planters and merchants refused to carry them.

MILLENARIAN ASPECTS OF THE EXODUS 331 MILLENARIAN ASPECTS OF THE EXODUS TO KANSAS OF 1879 Millenarism is not usually associated with American historiography, being most often identified with medieval or syncretic Christianity in Europe or the modern developing world-in the twentieth century, the cargo cults of Micronesia come to mind. In this country, the Ghost Dance, the Mormons, and perhaps the elitist colonies such as Brook Farm evoke echoes of millenarism. But the list should be lengthened to include at least one Afro-American movement in the nineteenth century. Eyewitness testimony has convinced me that one phase of the Exodus to Kansas of 1879 was millenarian in tenor and expectation. I suspect, further, that millenarism played a significant part in the movement of masses of blacks to the Union lines during the Civil War and again in the 1880s and 1890s in back-to-Africa excitements. Since I have not examined either of these other phenomena closely, let me limit my discussion of Afro-American millenarism to the Exodus to Kansas of 1879.1 The millenarian phase of the Exodus fits into the wider migration of South- ern blacks to Kansas and the West following Reconstruction, and it is only one facet- albeit the most sensational-of this much broader movement. The names of Benjamin "Pap" Singleton and Henry Adams are frequently mentioned concerning the Exodus, but neither of them participated in the millenarian movement, which for purposes of discussion I term the Kansas Fever Exodus. Norman Cohn's definition of religious millenarism is handily succinct, and with some tinkering it applies to the Kansas Fever Exodus very nicely. Strictly speaking, the Exodus was not a religious movement, but the freedpeople's concepts of slavery and freedom carried affective, almost transcendent connota- tions. Slavery was the same as damnation; freedom, real freedom, meant salva- tion. Cohn says that religious millenarism entails a salvation to be accomplished by a supernatural agency. Salvation, for the Exodusters (as the millenarians were termed), was to be accomplished by the Federal government, which in their eyes, exercised extraordinary, marvelous powers. It had given them their first, nominal freedom, delivering them from the everlasting fire of slavery. Cohn's definition includes four other characteristics; salvation is to be collec- tive, terrestrial, imminent, and total.2 The Kansas Fever Exodus unambiguously exhibited all these qualities. Limited to the colored people of the South, the Exodus concerned a clearly defined and coherent group. Salvation was also terrestrial, to be realized in Kansas (Bleeding Kansas, the Kansas of John Brown). Both salvation and damnation were imminent, for the Kansas Fever Exodus bore a strong, urgent, and dual temporal dimension. The movement was as much a fleeing from reenslavement as a flocking to freedom. Finally, salvation in Kansas was total-millenarians made no future plans. 332 journal of social history The Kansas Fever Exodus reached massive proportions in the first half of March 1879. At Delta, across the river from Vicksburg, P.B.S. Pinchback encountered large groups of Exodusters on the banks of the Mississippi. In New Orleans, Pinchback had heard talk of the Exodusters, but he was ill-prepared for the sight confronting him in Madison Parish: on nearing the Delta ferry landing, [I found] the banks of the river literally covered with colored people and their little store of worldly goods. The crowd then awaiting transportation at this point was estimated at about 300, but I learn that it had swollen to 500 by yesterday, all of whom took their departure in the St. Louis packet, Grand Tower, for Kansas....They are...absolutely panic stricken....Every road leading to the river is filled with wagons loaded with plunder and families who seem to think anywhere is better than here. 3 Once they reached St. Louis the Exodusters were lost; they had no idea whatever of how to reach Kansas from there. For them, further concrete plans were superfluous. Faith sustained their flight. Their success in leaving the South spurred hundreds of othersr who lined the banks of the Mississippi River into Arkansas or prepared to set out by train or wagon through northern Texas. During March, April, and May, hundreds of Exoduster families took the St. Louis packets on the first leg of the journey to Kansas. By late April, however, they faced growing difficulty in stopping the boats. Observers reported camps of 100 to 150 Exodusters each waiting at Skipwith's Landing, Bullit's Bayou,and Natchez-under-the-hill on the Mississippi side, and at Delta, Waterproof, Good Hope, and Vidalia on the Louisiana side.4 At Vidalia, a group of about ten families from the area of lake Concordia, just inland from the river, camped at the landing on April 23. Their numbers increased as other families joined themf but three weeks later they still had not succeeded in stopping a boat. The families across the river at Natchez-under-the-hill experienced similar frustra- tions.5 One stranded Exoduster wrote that members of his camp in Concordia Parish, Louisiana, had 'shailed boats but they worlt stop. The Democrats have monopolized the thing."6 Steamboat captains had been censured by planters and St. Louis merchants for encouraging the Exodus and ruining the Southern economy by taking away its labor force. Their resultant refusal to take passen- gers caused severe physical hardship, which finally broke the back of the movement in May. The many thousands who disembarked in St. Louis secure in their faith in the Kansas Fever myth, believed that all black people would face reenslavement if they remained long in the South. But if they would make the first move, they would realize true freedom in the Free State of Kansas-not the sham freedom they had known in Mississippi, Louisiana, and Texas since 1865. The Federa] government, the especial guardian of the freedpeople of the South, would assure them free transportation to Kansas, free land, and free tools and subsistence until their first crops could be harvested. The beauty of the Kansas Fever myth was its opening a way out of the South for the thousands of black families long wishing to leave. Their chronic lack of money was the great obstacle blocking their exit for relocating in the West ordinarily required, at rock bottom, two hundred dollars.
