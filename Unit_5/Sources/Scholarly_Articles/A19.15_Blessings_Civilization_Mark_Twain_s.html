Following the Equator chronicled the round-the-world tour Clemens undertook in 1895 to clear his debts from the disaster of the Paige typesetter investments. In effect, the tour provided a first-hand look at the British Empire and the variegated cultures under its dominion. One stop, however, brought him to the Republic of Hawai'i, a political entity in existence only because President Cleveland had refused to annex the islands after the monarchy was overthrown in 1893. Cholera kept Clemens from lecturing in Honolulu, and his representation of Hawai'i in Following the Equator is often nostalgic. Nevertheless, early in his career as Mark Twain, when the Hawaiian monarchy was a thriving enterprise, he had both written and lectured on Hawai'i, with commentary that usually included the volatile issue of annexing the islands. This essay will examine the commentary on Hawai'i as the deep back- ground to Clemens's thinking about colonialism and American empire at the turn of the century. The example from the Puddn'head Wilson calendar in Following the Equator can be taken as representative of all of Clemens's most scathing satirical efforts on behalf of the anti-imperialist movement, including "To the Person Sitting in Darkness" and "King Leopold's Soliloquy." What I will show is how his representations of Hawai'i move toward that scathing tone over the years, serving as a prelude to those well-known satires. In Zwick's account, the letters Clemens wrote for the Sacramento Union during that first visit "supported the arguments for U.S. annexation of the islands. [Clemens] used that material to argue for annexation in his early lectures on Hawai'i but reversed his position on the issue long before reworking the letters from Hawai'i into Roughing Ii" (75). Zwick's assertion that Clemens originally supported annexation and later changed his mind rests on two other claims. The first is Albert Bigelow Paine's declaration in his biography that Clemens in the early lectures on Hawai'i "advocated the idea of American ownership of these islands, dwelling at considerable length on his reasons for this idea" (1:293). Paine footnotes this comment to direct the reader to an appendix containing an excerpt from the first performance of the first Mark Twain lecture, "Our Fellow Savages of the Sandwich Islands," delivered in San Francisco on October 2, 1866. The excerpt opens with a section entitled "Hawaiian Importance to America," which appears to be two paragraphs quoting a newspaper account of the lecture. A one-sentence editorial headnote precedes the quoted paragraphs and summarizes the lecture by noting that Mark Twain had given a "full elucidation of the sugar industry ... its profits and possibilities" (4:1601). The quoted material makes two key points: the substantial revenues the Kingdom of Hawai'i pays into the U.S. treasury through the duties on its sugar; the astonishing productivity of Hawai'ian agriculture, cotton and rice as well as sugar. Then comes an ellipsis, followed by a sentence: "The property has got to fall to some heir, and why not the United States?" Clearly, this sentence motivates Paine to say that Mark Twain advocated American ownership of the islands, which Zwick uses for his claim that Mark Twain changed his mind about annexation in the 1870s after originally favoring it. Relying on Albert Bigelow Paine for a key fact is a little bit like driving on a flat tire: it's fine, as far as it goes, but you are not likely to reach your destination with dispatch. Paine's editorial habits have a tendency to puncture arguments as much as to convey them. Skepticism seems prudent. In this case, one should be skeptical because Paine's ellipsis white-washes a crucial part of the lecture, one suggesting that Clemens was at best ambivalent about annexation talk, even in his first view of the islands. Moreover, the material deleted from the lecture resembles in its basic comic tactic Clemens's most famous anti-imperialist essay, "To the Person Sitting in Darkness," written nearly thirty-five years later. 52 Mark Twain Annual, 2008 JAMES E.
