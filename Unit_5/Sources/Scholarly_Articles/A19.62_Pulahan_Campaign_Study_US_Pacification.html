The Pulahan Campaign: A Study in US Pacification Brian McAllister Linn The Pulahan revolt of 1904 to 1907 on the Philippine islands of Samar and Leyte is usually treated as a minor footnote in the dramatic events surrounding the U.S. conquest of the Philippines. American writers treat it as little more than a romantic imperial skirmish against mach ete-wielding fanatics; Philippine scholars focus on the sectarians' religious beliefs.' As a result, virtually nothing is known about either the Pulahanes' military tactics or the pacification campaign that eventually subdued them. This lack of attention is surprising, for the Pulahan campaign is one of the few case studies of American 'small wars' techniques. Fought immediately after the Philippine War of 1899-1902, it allowed US officers to put into practice the lessons they had learned in that earlier conflict. At the same time, it was the first campaign in which the Army had to operate within the bounds dic tated by the civilian colonial government. Finally, the suppression of the Pulahan rebellion required US Army officers to form alliances with the inhabitants, some of them recent enemies, and incorporate local units into pacification duties. This long-ignored conflict has more than a little relevance today, when conventional forces may expect to encounter zealots sparked with near-suicidal courage and communities torn by class and religion, while enduring constant scrutiny by their civilian supervisors. The Pulahan uprising had its roots in historic internal tensions on Samar between highlanders and lowlanders, tensions which were gre atly exacerbated by the bitter fighting between American and Filipino forces during the Philippine War of 1899-1902. At the time of the campaign there were no cross-island roads: most travel from the interior or along the coast was by banca (canoe) or along barely discernible trails. Samar's most important pro duct was hemp; valued as a source of cordage for agriculture and ships, it was grown in the mountainous interior by farmers who then carried it down to the coastal towns. The hemp trade benefited port towns, where merchants and officials too often cheated the impoverished and uneducated growers. Some, unwilling to tolerate such treatment, became ladrones (brigands), who, in return for food, shelter and infor mation, protected villagers from bandits, rapacious landowners or government agents. In the last decades of the nineteenth century the Dios Dios sect fused traditional folk religion with contemporary griev ances. Residing in highland communities, devotees raided the coastal towns and terrorized merchants, clerics and government officials. By the outbreak of the Philippine Revolution in 1896, Samar was torn by deep and worsening social divisions. The American occupation would intensify them and make the island synonymous with bloodshed and savagery.3 During the Philippine War, Samar was the scene of some of the most vicious fighting in the archipelago. The revolutionary leader Vicente Lukban formed an uneasy coalition of landowners, tenants, ladrones and sectarians. Guerrillas attacked and burned towns garrisoned by the US Army, forcing much of the population into the mountainous interior. Filipinos suspected of giving aid to the soldiers were kid napped, tortured, mutilated or killed. Not until mid-1901 was the Army able to begin a systematic pacification campaign, seeking through crop destruction, long-range patrols and the establishment of interior camps to break up the guerrilla strongholds and drive the population back to the occupied towns. After the massacre of an infantry company at Balangiga on 28 September 1901, the campaign escalated: crops, livestock, fishponds, houses and warehouses were destroyed. Such harsh measures forced the last revolutionary forces to surrender on 27 April 1902, but the Dios Dios, reinforced by recalcitrant guerrillas and brigands, rejected all peace feelers.
