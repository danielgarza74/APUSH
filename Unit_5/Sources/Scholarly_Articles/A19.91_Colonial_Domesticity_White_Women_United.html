Selection Overview

Rafael reads American women's writings from the early years of U.S. rule in the Philippines, beginning with Helen Taft's rapturous description of tropical nights in Manila. He argues that such accounts domesticated the colony, turning conquest into a sentimental, homelike space, and questions the myth of American exceptionalism that cast U.S. empire as more benign than European rule.

Colonial Domesticity: White Women and Rafael United States Rule in the Philippines I began to love the tropical nights and to feel that I had never before known what night can be like," wrote Helen Taft, wife of then Governor General of the Philippines, William Howard Taft, describing in 1902 the view from her veranda at the Malaca-nang Palace. "The stars were so large and hung so low that they looked almost like raised silver figures on a dark blue field.... The wonder- ful sunsets and the moonlit nights have tied more American hearts to Manila and the Philippines than all other charms combined. And they are both indescribable." 1 To say that the view of the tropics is "indescribable" is, of course, to describe it in terms of the touristic sublime. Composed into a view, the space of empire becomes available for private consumption and sentimental regard. Performed from the vantage point of the colonial home-in this case, the governor general's palace forcibly taken from the Spanish colonizers and later deeded to the postcolonial Philippine government-the consumption of that view becomes a feat of domes- ticating what is "native" and "natural" into aspects of the colonial that are at the same time national. The "charms" of the tropics thus be- come signs for the benevolence of conquest, part of a complex of imperial aesthetics that allows Taft to feel at home while she is away from home. Helen Taft's remarks are not unlike those of other American women writing on the Philippines during the formative period of United States rule (1899-1910) in the way they invest colonialism with the sense of the domestic and the sentimental. This essay explores the nature of those investments. Integral to the construction of colonial modernity American Literature, Volume 67, Number 4, December 1995. 640 American Literature in the late nineteenth and early twentieth century, colonial domesticity in the tropics heralded the conjunction of "whiteness" and "femininity" as a sign of public entitlement as well as a source of private ambiva- lence.2 Colonial sociality in this period reflected and refracted the glob- alization of Western bourgeois notions regarding the gendered and racialized embodiments of both rulers and ruled. In the notion of the domestic, American imperialism in the Philippines, like its European counterparts in Asia and Africa, furnished a public idiom for repre- senting as well as containing the private lives of its most privileged agents. Domesticity as a discourse of colonial modernity assumes that the structures of "public" and "private" are mobile and indefinitely reproducible, capable of translation across cultural and bodily spaces. By focusing on the American formations of colonial domesticity in the Philippines, I ask about the conditions that alternately enabled and dis- abled the construction of the uneasy divide between the public and the private in the empire, and about the ways this ambivalence generated phantasms of "home" that were at once in and out of place. Much of the available North American scholarship on white women and imperialism has tended to focus on British, French, and Dutch co- lonial societies, often evading the presence of Euro-American women in United States' possessions. Such an evasion is in part a function of the myth of American exceptionalism, that is, of the general tendency to regard American imperialism as an aberration from the otherwise democratic trajectory of the nation or at least to see American imperial interventions, especially in the Philippines, as far more benign and "progressive" than their European counterparts.3 This essay seeks to question those assumptions, situating its critique of colonial do- mesticity in relation to both past and more current inquiries into the constitutive role of empire in the history and discourse of American nationhood.4 By asking about the formation of colonial domesticity in what was the only formal colony of the United States in Asia, I seek to specify the ways by which gendered and racialized ideas about national agency became instrumental in the elaboration of globalized relations of power on the level of individuated, localized bodies.
