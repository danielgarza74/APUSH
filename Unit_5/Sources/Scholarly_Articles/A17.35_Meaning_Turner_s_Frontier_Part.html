A MEANING FOR TURNER'S FRONTIER PART II: THE SOUTHWEST FRONTIER AND NEW ENGLAND* 1 T HE South, as well as the North, had its frontier. While many a Massachusetts family in the 1 820s may have watched its younger sons departing for the Illinois wilderness, so many a young man from Virginia might at the same time have been found pushing into the unoccupied lands of Alabama and Mississippi. By Jackson's time, wave after wave of yeomen farmers of modest means had come to settle in that country, honest folk of democratic virtue who would form the bulk of the population and from whose sons, a genera- tion hence, would be recruited the heart and body of the Southern army. These people, like those of the Old Northwest, would form their pioneer tradition; their lore is likewise charged with logrollings and skirmishes with the redskin; they too would enact the primitive level of democratic experience. There is many a sense in which the life and institutions of Alabama and Mis- sissippi may stand as the epitome of Southern democracy. And yet it must be said that the similarity is principally onie of style. Few tales have oftener been retold than that of Colonel Dabney at the cabin-raising; few are richer in symbolic meaning and none more tersely circumscribes the essence of Southwest egalitarianism. Thomas Dabney, a Virginia planter who had just established himself in the newly settled cotton country of Mississippi and who had been invited to help raise the cabin of one of his poorer neighbors, appeared on the appointed morning with twenty slaves, directed their labors all day long, and did not leave until the last board had been nailed to the roof. He was never asked to another cabin-raising. In just this sense might the spread of Southern Jacksonian democracy be understood; in no way did it challenge the political and social arrangements implied in, say, Dabney's ownership of twenty slaves, but simply represented a kind of taking-off of gloves. We have already proposed a working definition for "political de- mocracy" which was featured by certain restrictions.2 We have said that its operation might be observed most strikingly in a frontier setting. But now here are two contemporary frontiers- the Old Northwest, which has been offered as a model, and the Mississippi Territory, which has already been hedged about with ambiguities. We permit the frontier atmosphere of Mississippi to be called a "democratic" one, hinting at the same time that "democracy" may be something less than accurate as a term for describing the ante-bellum cotton kingdom. Actually there are grounds for each of these presumptions. Both frontiers were "democratic"-just as Craftown and Hilltown were.3 But our hypothesis should also furnish us criteria for finding differences- for seeing how under specific tests one situation might be called more democratic than another, and for discovering the mechan- isms which make it so. A high rate of diverse and complex community problems, according to the argument, tends to guarantee the persistence of a democratic situation. It is, moreover, very difficult for the leadership in such a setting-for instance in a small, growing town-to develop or maintain a rigid and "oligarchic" character.
