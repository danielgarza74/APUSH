Smith argues that the traditional portrait of William Graham Sumner as a social Darwinist is inaccurate. Drawing on two recently discovered unpublished manuscripts, he contends that Sumner rejected social Darwinism late in his career, a shift obscured because Albert G. Keller withheld those manuscripts and gave The Science of Society its evolutionary framing.

WILLIAM GRAHAM SUMNER AS AN ANTI-SOCIAL DARWINIST NORMAN ERIK SMITH Project Talents Wilmington College This article argues that the traditional portrayal of Graham Sumner as a social Darwinist is inaccurate. Based mainly on two of Sumner's unpublished essays just recently discovered at the Yale University Library, it shows that Sum- ner's mature views on social change were quite different from those of his student Albert G. Keller and from those found within The Science of Society (which was published 17 years after his death, yet which lists him as a coauthor). Unlike Keller and The Science of Society, Sumner clearly rejected social Darwinism during the final decade of his career. Although students of the history of sociology have acknowl- edged the importance of William Graham Sumner's social structural contributions (folkways, mores, institutions), they have tended to view his contribution toward an understanding of social change as inconsequential. He has been portrayed as a relic from the past who adhered to a "stark, drastic, social Darwinian philosophy" (Stern, 1934: 463) of "the hard-bitten sort."' (Hofstadter, 1971: 11) However, a recent study by this author has discovered that Sumner underwent a major intellectual shift during the final and most sociological period of his career (1899-1910). Rather than endorsing social Darwinism, Sumner explicitly rejected it. This major turn of events, however, has gone entirely unnoticed by students of the history of sociology. AUTHOR'S NOTE: The author wishes to acknowledge the helpful suggestions and criticisms made on this article by Roscoe C. Hinkle of The Ohio State University. Smith / SUMNER AS AN ANTI-SOCIAL DARWINIST 333 There are a number of factors which have contributed to this state of affairs. Sumner did not systematically deal with social Darwinism (nor with social change in general) in his published writings during the period 1899-1910. However, he had not lost interest in the problem of change: he had worked from 1899 until about 1905 on a manuscript which was to be his major statement on the subject. This manuscript (which Keller reworked and published as The Science of Society) remained unfinished at his death. Furthermore, he had intended to include in Folkways (and had in fact written) a chapter on social change in which he stated his opposition to both social evolutionism in general and social Darwinism in particular. It was only because of pressure from his student Keller and others at Yale that he omitted that chapter (Keller, 1933: 36-37). He had planned, however, to publish this chapter, and other essays on evolution, at a later date (Sumner, 1906: iv-v). Unfortu- nately, his illness and death prevented this. The problem is further compounded by the fact that these pertinent essays were not included in the collections of his essays which were published posthumously. Keller, who edited these collections, acknowledged the existence of at least two Sumner manuscripts dealing with social Darwinism and social evolutionism (Keller, 1915: 328). Despite Keller's stated intentions, they were never published. To make matters worse, he withheld these essays from the Sumner Collection at the Yale University Library.2 Consequently, these important manuscripts were unavailable for scrutiny. Past analyses of Sumner have relied heavily on The Science of Society, a three-volume text published under the joint authorship of Sumner and Keller in 1927, 17 years after Sumner's death.
