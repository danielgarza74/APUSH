NOTES AND COMMENTS The Reick Telegram and the Spanish-American War: A Reappraisal LEWIS L. GOULD One of the most controversial subjects relating to the origins of the Spanish-American War is the attitude of the business community toward the approaching conflict in the winter and early spring of 1898. Writing the 1930s, Julius W. Pratt found that "the overwhelming preponderance general business opinion" opposed intervention in Cuba. "After the middl of March 1898," Pratt went on, "some conservative forces came to th intervention inevitable on humanitarian grounds, but many of the m influential business journals opposed it to the end."1 Three decades later Walter LaFeber challenged Pratt's thesis. In The New Empire: An Interpretation of American Expansion, 1860-18 (1963), LaFeber contended that business attitudes began to shift "duri the last two weeks in March when influential business spokesmen began t welcome the possibility of war in order to end the suspense which shroud the commercial exchanges." Philip S. Foner agreed in 1972. "With t support of a substantial section of, if not the entire, business community McKinley moved to war," he wrote. The LaFeber thesis has not entir supplanted the Pratt argument, but the role of the business community legitimizing war with Spain receives a greater emphasis among diplomatic historians in the wake of LaFeber's work.2 This essay focuses on a single piece of evidence bearing on the problem of business opinion toward war with Spain. 194 DIPLOMATIC HISTORY William C. Reick, city editor Young, a prominent former dated 25 March 1898, which r here now believe we will have war. Believe all would welcome it as a relief to suspense. Mr. H. sending you most important letter from Mr. B. He also agrees on these lines therefore don't think necessary now mince matters." The wire was sent from New York City and bears on it the time 4:52 P.M.3 Apparently the first use of this telegram as a refutation of the Pratt thesis occurred in 1960 in an article titled "The Spanish-American War: Business Recovery and the China Market: Selected Documents and Commentary" in Studies on the Left. Thomas McCormick, then at work on the research that led to his book China Market, furnished the Reick message and other documents to the editors, and they used them to show that "with the support of big corporations McKinley moved to war." A year later Ernest R. May also incorporated the telegram into his analysis in Imperial Democracy: The Emergence of America as a Great Power (1961). May was not trying to revise the Pratt thesis but rather to show the disintegration of the peace sentiment in the country. "The New York Herald changed its editorial policy just as the Chicago Times-Herald had," he said, and he observed, before quoting Reick, that the "city editor wrote confidentially" to the president.4 LaFeber drew the most significant conclusions from the Reick telegram in The New Empire. It was, he said, "Perhaps the most influential note the President received that week" in March 1898 when McKinley made key decisions about war with Spain. Arriving at the White House on 25 March from Reick, "a trusted political advisor in New York City and city editor of the New York Herald," the wire was, as LaFeber depicted it, linked to "an ultimatum to Spain" that McKinley and Assistant Secretary of State William R. Day presented on 27 March 1898. Since LaFeber, other historians such as Philip S. Foner and Charles S. Campbell have noted the message's impact on the McKinley administration.5 'William C.
