Selection Overview

McFarland presents Dorman B. Eaton, author of the bill that became the Pendleton Civil Service Act of 1883, as a representative genteel reformer whose single theme was nonpartisanship. A lawyer from an old New England family and a loyal Republican, Eaton blamed the spoils system for undermining public service and learned partisanship's destructive influence in New York's crusades against the Tweed Ring.

Eaton is seldom remembered today except, perhaps, as the author of a bill that does not even bear his name-the Pendleton Civil Service Act of 1883. Certainly Eaton is less well known than the other leading genteel reformers-E. L. Godkin of the Nation, George William Curtis of Harper's Weekly, or Carl Schurz, the German-born liberal- whose public careers were broader in scope.' Yet it is the very narrowness of Eaton's interests and the intensity with which he explored a single theme, nonpartisanship, on which his importance rests. Eaton advocated the essentially progressive goals of efficient municipal administration and of civil service reform for all levels of American government. The impetus for his program, however, was actually conservative, for Eaton believed that excellence in public service had been undermined by the spoils system, a legacy of the Jacksonian revolution. Eaton's views, mixing as they did modern and reactionary tendencies, were representative of a major nine- teenth-century reform type, the genteel progressive. Eaton was a member of the Yankee exodus-a New Englander who left the region to seek a career elsewhere.2 Born in 1823 in Hardwick, Ver- mont, the son of a Unitarian minister, and descended from an old New England family, Eaton received his undergraduate education at the Univer- sity of Vermont. Eaton quickly established himself as one of New York's brightest young lawyers. With William Kent he coedited two editions of Chancellor Kent's famous Commentaries on American Late. Late in the 1850s Eaton, in part- nership with Kent and George Bancroft's nephew, John Chandler Bancroft Davis, opened a firm which became legal counsel for large corporations such as the Erie Railroad.3 During these formative years prior to the Civil War, Eaton acquired several points of reference that shaped his later ca- reer: a thorough legal knowledge which enabled him to become the drafts- man of many reform laws, an enduring attachment to the Republican party, and a reverence for the high professional standards of the old New York bar.4 Eaton's interest in reform dated from 1864 and coincided with the initial steps against municipal corruption in New York City. This was a campaign which culminated in 1871, when the notorious Tweed Ring was broken. The city's reform crusade evolved as a series of separate efforts, each against a different opponent, but each feeding on the previous movement and becoming thereby successively more effective. At first, the reform groups were few and consisted mainly of the Citizens Association and the Union League Club. These organizations sought to improve such inefficient and much-neglected city services as the fire, health, and police departments. In 1868 the focus of reform shifted to the Erie Ring, when respectable stockholders of the Erie Railroad declared war on Jay Gould and Jim Fisk, under whose parasitical management the Erie was being milked of its re- sources. Finding that the Ring had powerful allies in the judiciary, the re- formers initiated a movement to improve standards in the bench and bar. Finally, the Citizens Association, Union League, and Bar Association were joined by the Times and Harper's Weekly in an assault on the most tena- cious group of all, the Tweed Ring.5 As a prominent participant in these movements, Eaton received a valuable practical education in the pervasive and often destructive influence of partisanship on civic affairs.6 'James Kent, Commentaries on American Law (8th ed., 4 vols., New York, 1854), I, iv; Dorman B.
