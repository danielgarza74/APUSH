Cooperatives and the Movement Culture of Populism 867 the cooperative and monetary reform programs of the Farmers' Alliance and People's party as presenting valid alternatives to the capitalistic corporate and bureaucratic structure of twentieth-century America. For Goodwyn, Populism held much more democratic promise than a liberal reform of American capitalism, an interpretation of the movement popularized by John D. Hicks and followed by many historians. For Goodwyn, Populism was much more than a reform movement. It offered a humane alternative to either corporate capitalism or bureaucratic socialism; it envisioned a popularly led, truly democratic, cooperative commonwealth. As such it provided the last chance for a radical change in the nature of American capitalist society.3 At the heart of the mature Populist vision of a better America was a commodity-based, democratic monetary policy that had been developed within a "movement culture" created during the desperate struggle to build a massive system of farmer-operated cooperatives. Goodwyn's concept of the relationship between a movement culture, the cooperative movement, and financial reform is central to his interpretation and is stated many times in both versions of his work. The following excerpts summarize this major component of Goodwyn's hypothesis. To describe the origins of Populism in one sentence, the cooperative movement recruited American farmers, and their subsequent experience within the cooperatives radically altered their political consciousness. The agrarian revolt cannot be understood outside the framework of the economic crusade [of cooperation] that not only was its source but also created the culture of the movement itself.4 The cooperative movement became the recruiting vehicle through which huge numbers of farmers in the South and West learned to think about a new kind of demo- cratic possibility in America. The central educational tool of the Farmers Alliance was the cooperative experiment itself. The massive effort at agrarian self-help, and the op- position it stimulated from furnishing merchants, wholesale houses, cotton buyers, and bankers in the South and from grain elevator companies . . . and bankers in the West, brought home to hundreds of thousands of American farmers new insights into their relationship with the commercial elements of American society. Reduced to its essentials, the cooperative movement recruited the farmers to the Alliance in the period 1887-91, and the resulting cooperative experience educated enough of them to make independent political action a potential reality.
