Rednecks and Merchants: Economic Development and Social Tensions in the South Carolina Upcountry, 1865-1900 Lacy K. Ford In his provocative analysis of southern exceptionalism, The Mind of the South, W. J. Cash searched for the meaning behind the skyscrapers that Progress, New South style, had built among the stark and simple red clay hills of the Carolina Piedmont. In particular, the Charlotte-based journalist sought to explain how Babbittry and Rotarianism could thrive in a region where hundreds of rustic yeomen had rallied behind scores of proud planters to defend slavery and the southern way of life just over a half-century earlier. Unlike the filiopietistic school of Progressive historians who praised the rise of the New South as "the progress of a mighty people, " Cash was not deceived by the seductive and self-congratulatory rhetoric of New South propagandists. Cash, after all, wrote his epic during the crippling depression of the 1930s, or the "Great Blight," as he preferred to call it. He had only to look around his native Piedmont to see farmers battling both boll weevils and bankruptcy or to view part of the region's "cracker proletariat" walking out of the textile mills on strike. Moreover, just three years before his book was published, Cash heard the Franklin D. Roosevelt administration label the South the "nation's number one economic problem." With poverty and wrenching class tensions so visible all around him, Cash knew that the champions of the New South Creed had promised far more than they had delivered. Still, despite, or perhaps because of, the New South's failings, Cash remained intrigued by the rapidity with which a new business civilization had been grafted onto the remains of the Old South. According to Cash, the spirit of the Old South, embodied in the planter colonels and generals who had worn the Confederate grey, not only survived the war but also emerged, wearing less romantic garb, to take the lead in the building of the New South. Cash believed that New South financiers and textile captains "belonged more or less distinctly within the limits of the old antebellum ruling class" and that as the progeny of the old planter elite, New South leaders were committed not only to the promotion of economic development but also to "a striking extension of the so-called paternalism of the Old South" to the industrial society of the New. Behind all the New South boosterism and talk of industrial development, Cash heard "the gallop of Jeb Stuart's cavalrymen" and saw the guiding hand of a rejuvenated planter elite.2 Just over a decade after Cash's book appeared, C. Vann Woodward presented a dramatically different account of postbellum southern economic develop- ment that quickly supplanted Cash's volume as the standard explanation of the origins of the New South. Unlike Cash, who emphasized the continuity between Old South and New South, Woodward insisted on the importance of the Civil War era as a watershed in southern history. Woodward's argument, as admirably summarized by Sheldon Hackney, maintained that the industri- alists and financiers of the New South were "not the sons of the old planter aristocracy" but "members of the new middle class . . . derived from the families of urban merchants or men of the professions." New South leaders were not interested in a restoration of Old South values but were instead champions of "laissez-faire capitalism and a changed way of life," seeking to revive an anemic South with a huge transfusion of "Hamiltonian-Whig- industrial" blood. Yet behind the ringing oratory and exaggerated claims of New South boosters, Woodward noted, lay a sordid reality of continued poverty and deprivation, not only for blacks, but for many whites as well. Moreover, Woodward blamed the postbellum South's long record of economic backwardness and stagnation, in large part, on the inability of the region's economic elite, the enterprising new class of town merchants and profes- sionals, to break the fetters of colonial dependence on the developing North.3 In recent years the much-publicized surge of Sunbelt prosperity and the consequent speculation that the South might once again assume the role of "arbiter of the national destiny" have prompted scholars to reexamine the questions about southern economic development and its social bases that so mesmerized Cash and Woodward. On the whole, Woodward's classic account has weathered well the test of time. Nevertheless, practitioners of the "new" history, armed with the latest interdisciplinary techniques, have turned their attention to the emerging contours of postbellum southern society and have produced important modifications of and challenges to Woodward's seminal argument.
