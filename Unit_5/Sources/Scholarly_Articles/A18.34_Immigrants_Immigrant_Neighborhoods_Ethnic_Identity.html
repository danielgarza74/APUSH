Selection Overview

Conzen reviews how scholars have understood the immigrant city neighborhood's role in forming ethnic identity and in assimilation. She traces urban ecologists and sociologists such as Park and Burgess, who treated the ghetto as a sheltered way station toward the American mainstream, and notes growing recognition that ghetto culture was a creative response to American conditions rather than a transfer of Old World ways.

Immigrants, Immigrant Neighborhoods, and Ethnic Identity: Historical Issues Kathleen Neils Conzen The immigrant neighborhood was for decades a distinctive element in the American urban landscape. Even today, two generations after the last of the great waves of European immigration, when brooding empty churches often bear the only witness to immigrant neighborhoods that once were, past ethnic identification may remain an important key to present neighborhood oc- cupancy, and newer migrant groups continue to find in the neighborhood a focus for acculturation to urban life. Confronted with the inescapable physical presence of ethnically identifiable neighborhoods, scholars long ascribed to the immigrant neighborhood a central role both in the emergence of ethnic identity among immigrants and in the subsequent process of assimilation. But several recent studies have questioned the degree of immigrant concentration in such neighborhoods and consequently the ability of the neighborhood to influence the adaptive process. Such conclusions, if valid, have important implications for current efforts of immigration historians to shift emphasis from the process of assimilation to the role of factors like the family, church, and workplace in encouraging the maintenance of ethnic culture and social distinctiveness. I However, a brief review of relevant scholarship, with special emphasis on the experience of German immigrants, suggests that the issue is by no means resolved; many questions about the relationship between residence and ethnicity remain unanswered, even unasked. To some of the first scholars to explore this issue, the early urban ecologists, ethnicity was a fundamental influence upon residential choice. The processes of selection, invasion, and succession which thus structured the city into neighborhoods were regarded as intimately linked to immigrant assimilation. As Robert E. Park put it, "change of occupation, personal success or failure- changes of economic and social status, in short-tend to be registered in changes of location."2 The same link between ethnic identification and neighborhood con- centration was readily perceived by scholars interested in immigrant assimilation per se. To some, the expanding ghettos, with their pathological environments and ostensibly foreign cultures, constituted undisputed evidence of the impossibility of immigrant assimilation. But others main- tained with Ernest W. Burgess that "the telling fact . . . is not that the im- migrant colony maintains its old-world cultural organization, but that in its new environment it mediates a cultural adjustment to its new situation."4 Assimilation, argued a 1920 study, proceeded via the neighborhood: sheltered from pressures for too rapid cultural change and comforted by the presence of fellow newcomers, immigrants could find within the ubiquitous ghetto the emotional security and economic and political bases necessary for movement up the economic ladder, out of the ghetto, and ultimately into a vaguely defined American mainstream.I This was a comforting argument for those worried about the consequences of ethnic diversity for American social unity and stability. It became the standard interpretation of more than a generation of sociologists and historians who charted what they saw as the gradual but inexorable assimilation of the immigrant. Some joined Oscar Handlin in placing greater emphasis than did the early sociologists on the traumas of ghetto life and the difficulties of escape. There was also increasing appreciation that ghetto culture was not a direct transfer of Old World culture but rather a creative response to situations not comprehended in the peasant villages of the homeland.6 As an American creation, ethnic culture could be nurtured only where a shared predicament and shared institutions supported the development of a community life.
