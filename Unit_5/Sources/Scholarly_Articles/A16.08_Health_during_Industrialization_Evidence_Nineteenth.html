Selection Overview

Carson uses records of Black and white men in Pennsylvania’s state prison system from 1829 to 1909 to measure health during industrialization through height. He finds that men from southeastern Pennsylvania, near dairy counties and urbanized Philadelphia, were consistently shorter than others, and confirms a midcentury height decline among white men that extended to Black men, amid rising wages and output.

Scott Alan Carson Health during Industrialization Evidence from the Nineteenth-Century Pennsylvania State Prison System This article considers the relationship among race, stature, and proximity to Pennsyl- vania ys nineteenth-century dairy-producing regions. Previous studies demonstrate a positive relationship between stature and access to dairy products. However, Pennsyl- vania 's dairy-producing region was also close to urbanized Philadelphia. Here a new dataset is used from the Pennsylvania state prison system to track the heights of black and white males incarcerated from 1829 to 1909. It is documented that both blacks and whites living in southeastern Pennsylvania near both dairy-producing counties and urbanized Philadelphia were consistently shorter than individuals born and incar- cerated elsewhere, indicating that the effects of urbanization dominated proximity to dairy production. Black inmates were consistently shorter than their white counter- parts. The well-known midcentury height decline is confirmed among white men and is extended to blacks as well. Industrialization and modernization frequently bring about rising incomes, wages, and life expectancy, particularly in the long run (Komlos 1987; Floud et al. 1990: 272-73). However, in the short run economic change also creates social turmoil, such as increasing inequality, a higher crime rate, and a more virulent disease environment, all of which lead to deteriorating biological con- ditions. Hence the overall impact of industrialization on biological conditions depends on which effect dominates. In the case of the Middle Atlantic states, eco- nomic growth was associated with greater factor mobility and greater income accumulation, which enhanced biological conditions (Easterlin 1971: 40-41; Soltow 1975: 103; Atack and Bateman 1980: 125; 1987: 87-92; Steckel 1983). However, the Middle Atlantic states also experienced rapid industrialization, high population densities, increasing transaction costs to acquire food, and more virulent disease environments, which impeded biological conditions (Atack and Bateman 1987: 156; Komlos 1987: 918). Stature measures the net effect of nutrition, environmental conditions, disease insults, and calorie claims for work; consequently, environment can influence a population's average stature (Eveleth and Tanner 1966). When diets, health, or physical environments improve, the average stature increases, but it decreases when diets become less nutritious, when disease environ- ments deteriorate, or when the physical environment places more stress on the body. Hence stature provides significant insights into the understanding of historical processes and augments the understanding of other nineteenth- century welfare measures. A common theme in nineteenth-century European and American anthropométrie samples is the existence of an early industrial growth puzzle, insofar as wages and output per capita increased while average physical stat- ure noticeably declined (Margo and Steckel 1982; Komlos 1987; Steckel and Haurin 1994; Cuff 2005). Stature also varied by socioeconomic status and nativity. Farmers were consistently taller than nonfarmers, and northeast- ern and Middle Atlantic males were shorter than other Americans (Sokoloff and Villaflor 1982: 463; Margo and Steckel 1983: 171-72; Fogel 1986: 500; Komlos 1987: 902; Steckel and Haurin 1994: 124-25; Komlos and Coclanis 1997: 441). Against that backdrop, this study considers whether these observed bio- logical patterns and explanations held for nineteenth-century black and white males in Pennsylvania's state prison system. Prison records are particularly useful for examining changes in biological conditions because they provide accurate stature measurements and are drawn from lower socioeconomic groups, the segments of society most vulnerable to economic change (Bogin 1991: 288; Riggs 1994: 64; Komlos and Baten 2004: 199; Godoy et al. 2005: 469-70). Three sets of questions are considered. First, how did Pennsylvania inmate statures compare to other American statures, and how were stature variations and dietary changes related to industrialization?
