Selection Overview

Duran asks whether federal subsidies under the Pacific Railroad Act of 1862 were needed to induce private investment in the transcontinental railroad. Against historians who saw the line as built ahead of demand, he finds investors expected it to be profitable after the 1859 Nevada mining boom, and argues subsidies reduced political risk rather than correcting a transport market failure.

Most economic historians propose that entrepreneurs did not expect the railroad to be profitable; the railroad was "built ahead of demand" or "premature." In this view, the Pacific Railroad Act was necessary to induce private investment, even if it was not efficient (Schumpeter 1939; Fogel 1960; Mercer 1982; Fishlow 2000). The approach adopted here examines the ex-ante private investment decision by entrepreneurs and investors to develop an estimate of expected profits from the transcontinental circa 1862. The analysis reveals a different view of the construction of the first transcontinental. First, instead of being premature or built ahead of demand because few farmers had settled along the route, the railroad was a response to transport demand. A key event was the 1859 Nevada mining boom, something overlooked in the existing literature. Up to that time little local traffic demand was expected. Prior to the Nevada boom, entrepreneurs planned to build a single-stage railroad from the Pacific Ocean to the Midwest, where it would connect to the eastern railroad network, to transport trade from California and China. Investment implied a large initial sunk cost and a wait of about ten years to complete the whole railroad, and only then would revenues start flowing.
