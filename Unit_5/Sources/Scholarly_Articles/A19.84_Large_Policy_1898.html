Selection Overview

Pratt questions the long-accepted view that the United States entered the war with Spain in 1898 with no thought of colonies, a view voiced by Bryce, Powers, and Latane, who saw the acquisitions as unforeseen. He points to newer work, including evidence in Roosevelt's and Lodge's correspondence, suggesting that some statesmen hoped to join the scramble for colonies before it was too late.

THE "LARGE POLICY" OF 1898 BY JULIUS W. PRATT It has for a long time been a generally accepted dogma among students of American history that the United States entered upon the war with Spain in 1898 without foresight of the pro- found results of that struggle upon her policy and her position in the world. This dogma, had the most respectable backing. James Bryce, usually a keen observer of American affairs, wrote Theodore Roosevelt on September 12, 1898, when it had become apparent that the United States was to become a colonial power: "How stupendous a change in the world these six months have brought. Six months ago you no more thought of annexing the Philippine Isles and Porto Rico than you think of annexing Spitzbergen to-day." H. H. Powers, in an a.ddress before the American Academy of Political and Social Science. in September, 1898, remarked that the United States had suddenly, within the space of a year, developed new desires and ambitions.
