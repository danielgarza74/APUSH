From Robber Barons to Industrial Statesmen: Standard Oil and the Business Historian' By DAVID CHALMERS DURING THE PAST TWO DECADES the American middle classes and the business community have become fast friends. The hostility and suspicion that divided them during the "progressive era" of Theodore Roosevelt and Woodrow Wilson and the "new deal" of Franklin Roosevelt have given way to mutual trust, which gas lobbies and submerged oil lands, coats of vicuna or mink, recessions and readjustments have not shaken. With much greater sobriety than the Nineteen Twenties, the American middle classes have accepted the dictum that the business of the United States is indeed business. One of the important factors in this outlook has been the reconciliation of the academicians and the business community. It has been a marriage of mutual respect. The academic economist is no longer pictured by his business confreres as a dangerous revolutionist. The scientist, psycholo- gist, and economist find themselves in commercial demand. College teach- ers spend their vacations on industry-financed scholarships acquainting themselves with the functioning of leading corporations. Executives, at least in the higher echelons, talk of the desirability of employing young college graduates educated in the liberal arts. On the nation's campuses the major post-war innovation has been the soaring growth of the schools of business administration. Business is not only public relations conscious but society conscious, and society is in- creasingly fascinated with the men who run the business world. The movies, television and the novelist catalogue the struggle of the business- men, not only for production, but also for social adjustment. On their part, the businessmen have opened up not only their files to the historians, but also their psyche to Fortune Magazine and the sociologists. I AN IMPORTANT PART of this productive alliance between the purveyors of the market place and the groves of academe has been the emergence of the business historians. And there was need for such an occurrence. Pre- viously what little business history there was had been written primarily by antagonists or by company publicists and apologists. The response has been little short of overwhelming,2 but what has been its values and its quality ? This study concentrates on the Standard Oil histories for several reasons. Standard was the first great American industrial giant organized for the production of goods. It was the object of the severest criticism and came to represent to the public mind all of the greatest evils of concentrated power. The story of Standard Oil has been focused around a single man of great ability and power-and great secretiveness, thus becoming the leading gothic tale of American industrial development. Perhaps for these reasons, the oil industry has received more attention from the historians than any other. Little has been spared in the way of talent, effort, and money. In a very real sense it has been a "pilot study" for the field of business history. The results have been magnificent-and alarming. It is with the latter aspect that this paper is concerned, pointing up some of the problems and the dangers, as illustrated in the writing of the history of Standard Oil. The business historians are revisionists. They believe that they must change the traditional, "misconceived" public attitudes. They have a strong sense of cause, and they place the crime of bad history at the door of all those who have written previously (and critically) of the Standard Oil Company. Only Allan Nevins has an occasional good word-and many bad ones-for the previous writers. Edward N. Saveth, writing on "What historians teach about business" placed the predecessors of the business historians in successive phases: first, the patrician historians, sons of the displaced merchant princes who by their very categorization had gone "sour" (to use Saveth's word) on business. The second phase was that of the muckrakers, writing out of "pecuniary" or "altruistic" (part of the new category of pejorative terms) motives, employing repetitive styles, resulting in "State capitalism" (another pejorative term), primarily concerned with selling copies. This has been followed by successive periods: Charles Beard and his disciples, the Marxists, and the world of Arthur Schlesinger, Jr., all leading to the Jefferson-Franklin Roosevelt-Harry Truman "Fair Deal" orientation.3 But if the critical historians wrote badly, it is with their ethics that the business historians really lose patience.
