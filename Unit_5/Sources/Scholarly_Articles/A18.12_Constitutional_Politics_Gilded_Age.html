Constitutional Politics in the Gilded Age By Michael Les Benedict, Ohio State University During the Gilded Age, constitutional issues pervaded the discussion of nearly all matters of public policy, including regulation of railroads, suppressing unsafe and fraudulent products, labor issues, and combating trusts and monopolies. The Democratic and Republican parties differed in their conceptions of federal power and state rights as well as on matters related to social order and personal liberty. They articulated these differences in political platforms and manifested them in their approach to public policy. The obsession with constitutional issues was not confined to the halls of Congress or the chambers of the Supreme Court. Constitutional discourse ran up from ordinary people and interest groups to public policy makers and down from policy makers seeking support based on fidelity to constitutional prin ciples. Ordinary people influenced constitutional policymaking not only through voting but through various means of making their views known. Advocates used all types of media to make constitutional issues clear to the American people. These ranged from formal treatises aimed at the intellectual elite to cartoons, caricatures, songs, and screeds. Politicians articulated constitutional positions in political platforms, congressional addresses, pamphlets, political and commemorative addresses, and stump speeches. Justices of the Supreme Court eschewed technical and abstract language in constitutional opinions, addressing them to a more general public than they did in other areas of law. In the end, constitutional policy was not deter mined through legal determinations of the Supreme Court but by the political decisions of the American people. In 1912, affirming his "Confession of Faith" before the cheering delegates of the Bull Moose convention that had nominated him for the presidency on the Progressive Party ticket, Theodore Roosevelt denied that Americans had surrendered to "any set of men, no matter what their position or their character," the right to decide fundamental questions of the powers of gov ernment. "The people themselves must be the ultimate makers of their own Constitution," he thundered.1 Fundamental constitutional questions could not be decided through narrow constructions of constitutional law. The American people must and had always decided them through what we may call "constitutional politics." From this perspective, a constitution establishes legally enforced boundaries to what government can do and how it can do it. These are a matter of law. Acting within those legal boundaries, pohcymaking institutions have a free rein to act as they find reasonable. This discretion constitutes the realm of politics. Thus constitutional analysts contrast the judicial branch of government from what they call "the political branches."3 Much of the con stitutional jurisprudence of the past fifty years has been a struggle to resolve what legal academics call the "counter-majoritarian difficulty" inherent in treating the courts' primary constitutional function to be upholding constitu tional norms against democratic decisions that violate them. Great constitutional historians of the late nineteenth and early twenti eth centuries?Francis Newton Thorpe, Homer C. Hockett, Andrew C. McLaughlin, and Hermann von Hoist?took a broad view of their field. But over the decades, constitutional historians came to share the view that law and politics were fundamentally different. Stressing the history of con stitutional law rather than constitutional politics, they paid scant attention to the social and political underpinnings of constitutional issues. By the 1960s, historians had largely lost interest in the field, leaving it to political scientists and especially, in recent years, to legal academics. Constitutional history be came largely the history of constitutional law, as understood by legal academ ics, trained and teaching in law schools, where law is conceived to be almost entirely as what judges decide in courts. In the old days, before the Journal of American History's "Recent Scholarship in American History" went online and entries were listed only under a single category, the constitutional items in the "Legal/Constitutional History" category consisted almost entirely of court cases, biographies of lawyers and judges, and the odd assessment of a state constitutional convention.
