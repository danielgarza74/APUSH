The Anti-Japanese Origins of the Hawaiian Annexation Treaty of 1897 WILLIAM MICHAEL MORGAN Before annexing Hawaii in 1898 the United States never had acquired territory outside the North American continent.1 As the first territorial step toward global rather than regional American power, Hawaiian annexation occupies a secure place in historical literature. In the late 1890s expansionists viewed Hawaii as a useful stopping point on the trade route to oriental markets and as a desirable naval base from which to defend the West Coast, protect commerce, and help guard the Pacific approaches to the projected Isthmian canal. Moreover, annexation meshed with the imperialist ideological currents of the day, such as social Darwinism and Anglo-Saxonism. These commercial, strategic, and ideo logical factors produced a successful annexation drive during William McKinley's presidency. 24 DIPLOMATIC HISTORY treaty's importance. Because Congress annexed Hawaii in July 1898 by joint resolution instead of by treaty, most historians understandably have focused on the events of early and mid-1898, when the annexationists abandoned the treaty and turned to the joint resolution offered by Repre sentative Francis G. Newlands of Nevada. This approach emphasizes the importance of such events as the threatened partition of China, Admiral George Dewey's great victory at Manila Bay, and Hawaii's partisan support of the American war effort against Spain. These celebrated happenings created very few annexationist votes, however. From December 1897, when Congress convened, through July 1898, when annexation took place, numerous press polls, canvasses by annexationists, and administration surveys showed Senate support for the treaty holding steady in the 55 to 59 vote range.3 (Sixty votes were needed for ratifica tion.) Counting paired senators, the Newlands joint resolution had fifty-six supporters when it passed the upper house.4 Similarly, annexationist support remained constant in the House throughout 1898.5 If, as these facts suggest, the pro-Hawaii vote was formed in 1897, then perhaps the treaty and not the exciting events of 1898 did the most to forge the annexationist majority. Second, the mysterious circumstances of the treaty's birth have prevented a proper understanding of the pact. McKinley several times told annexationist leaders that a Hawaiian treaty would have to wait for passage of the Dingley tariff bill, which the president considered vital to economic recovery and enlargement of government revenues through tariff revision.6 Nevertheless, in the midst of the tariff struggle he suddenly submitted an annexation treaty.
