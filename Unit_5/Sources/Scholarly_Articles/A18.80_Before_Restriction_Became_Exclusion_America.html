Before Restriction Became Exclusion: America’s Experiment in Diplomatic Immigration Control BETH LEW-WILLIAMS The author is an ACLS New Faculty Fellow in the departments of history and Asian American studies at Northwestern University. The Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) marked a turning point in the history of U.S. immigration control, but it was not as deﬁnitive a move toward gatekeeping as historians have suggested. Contemporaries called the 1882 law the ‘‘Chinese Restriction Act,’’ reserving the term ‘‘exclusion’’ for its successor in 1888. The rhetorical change paralleled an important shift in policy. During Chinese Restriction (1882–1888), the United States so valued its relationship with China that it made immigration restriction subject to diplomatic negotiation. Only after the Restriction Act failed and China signaled capitulation did the United States enact Chinese Exclusion (1888), which prohibited Chinese workers, asserted America’s sovereign power to exclude, and developed modern systems of enforcement. The transition from diplomatic Restriction to unilateral Exclusion represents a powerful aggrandizement of American power. Key words: Chinese Exclusion, Chinese immigrants, U.S. immigration policy, U.S.- Chinese relations, U.S.-Canadian border With the privilege of hindsight, historians portray the ‘‘Chi- nese Exclusion Act’’ of 1882 as a pivotal moment in the history of American immigration. The act declared Chinese laborers to be ‘‘undesirable,’’ barred their entry for the following decade, and explicitly denied naturalization privileges to all Chinese. Scholars have argued that the 1882 act was ‘‘extremely effective in limiting Chinese immigration in the ﬁrst two decades’’ and ‘‘effectively slammed the door on all Chinese immigration.’’ For evidence of this, they point to immigra- tion numbers in the years following the act. They note that only forty Chinese immigrated to the United States in 1886, ten in 1887 (an ‘‘all-time low’’), and twenty-six in 1888. 2 But this was not the reality for the people of the West Coast in the 1880s. At the Port of San Francisco alone, 7,744 Chinese gained entrance in 1886, 11,172 in 1887, and 12,842 in 1888. Later we will consider this discrepancy, but for now it is enough to say that the Chinese ‘‘invasion’’ continued after 1882.
