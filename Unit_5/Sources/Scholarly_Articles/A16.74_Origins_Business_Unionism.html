Selection Overview

Taft disputes Perlman's view that business unionism, focused on wages, hours, and collective bargaining within capitalism, was created by disillusioned socialist labor leaders of the 1870s and 1880s. He argues instead that it is the oldest American laborism, visible in early shoemakers' and printers' societies and fully developed by the labor movements of the 1830s.

ON THE ORIGINS OF BUSINESS UNIONISM PHILIP TAFT BUSINESS UNIONISM as a doctrine, and even more as practice, has been attributed to the influence of Gompers and his close coworkers and their succes- sors. It has been described as essentially trade conscious, rather than class conscious. .... It aims chiefly at more, here and now, for the organized workers in a craft or industry, in terms mainly of higher wages, shorter hours, and better working conditions, regard- less for the most part of the welfare of the workers outside the particular or- ganic group, and regardless in general of political and social considerations, except insofar as these bear directly upon its own economic ends. It is conservative ... and accepts as inevitable, if not as just, the existing capitalistic organiza- tion and the wage system, as well as existing property rights and the binding force of contract. It regards unionism mainly as a bargaining institution and seeks its ends chiefly through collective bargaining.' ORIGINS OF BUSINESS UNIONISM 21 business unionism was an indigenous growth or was rather the creation of dis- illusioned socialists and anarchists who transformed class consciousness into wage consciousness. The evidence suggests that business unionism is the oldest laborism in the United States, that it began with the first labor organizations established in the late eighteen and early nineteenth centuries, and that it was fully developed and promoted by the first labor move- ments of the 1830's. In fact, not only the characteristic views of the business union- ist on wages and conditions of employ- ment, but even such attitudes as the desire to abstain from political involve- ment and the doctrine of exclusive juris- diction were already clearly stated by the central labor bodies of the 1830's. What is more, there appears to be no reason to assume that the experiences of one gen- eration or decade were blotted out, even though the life of the unions in the early periods was short and their survival un- certain. In fact, unions primarily inter- ested in improving wages and conditions of employment existed in every decade, and instead of being a development of the 1870's and 1880's, they have through- out the nineteenth and present centuries exercised a major influence upon the thinking and policies of organized workers. It might be noted that a union is pri- marily a method for dealing with the problems of workers in a trade or calling. There is little reason for establishing this type of organization when wider social, political, or economic changes are sought. Let us, however, turn to the evidence. Associated effort among the workers prior to 1795 was temporary, having a single purpose and when this was ac- complished, the compact was dissolved. It was customary in all trades to call a general meeting of the trade whenever a matter of general importance presented itself.3 Such meetings were held in pri- vate homes and statements were drawn up embodying the demands of those at- tending on prices or wages to be paid. All signed their names to the document, and if a strike followed, regular meet- ings would be held. Once the issue was resolved the organization ceased to exist. Permanent trade societies followed. According to Samuel Logan, a witness in the first shoemakers' conspiracy trial, the first union organized in that trade, in 1792, required that newly initiated take an oath to "support such and such wages to the utmost of my power." The society was dissolved and later reorgan- ized "again for the support of wages; as soon as we could agree upon the increase, we raised our wages." "The turnout or strike of 1799 was occasioned by em- ployers attempting to reduce wages."4 In 1799, the Franklin Typographical Society of Journeymen Printers of New York was formed. It formulated the first complete wage scale for printers in New York City and struck for its enforcement. Like its contemporaries, the union con- cerned itself almost exclusively with wages and other terms of employment. About the same time, the Typographical Society of Philadelphia arose. It de- manded exclusive employment of union men in the trade, recognition of a "work- ing card," and a system of hiring closely resembling the union hiring hall.5 Nor can the activities of the New York and Philadelphia societies in the print- ing trade be regarded as unusual.
