Democracy, Domestication, and Doubling in the U.S. Colonial Philippines
Julian Go III
 
ARTICLE Democracy, Domestication, and Doubling in the U.S. Colonial Philippines
JOURNAL Political and Legal Anthropology Review 20, no. 1 (1997)

Selection Overview

Go examines American "democratic tutelage" in the colonial Philippines, which built a state mirroring American liberal institutions while placing Filipino elites under layers of American supervision meant to teach rule-bound, legal-rational government. He argues the outcome was neither simple adoption nor localization of democracy but a hybrid in which American forms and Filipino practices of power were kept apart, a doubling.

It is rather a plurality of "spheres" and arenas, each having its own separate logic; hence... the postcolonial "subject" mobilizes not just a single "identity", but several fluid identities... Achille Mbembe,"The Banality of Power and the Aesthetics of Vulgarity in the Postcolony" The so-called "democratization" of much of the world today is not exactly a new phenomenon. United States colonial rule over the Philippines, beginning some hundred years ago, perhaps prefigures today's ostensible novelty. After wrestling control over the archipelago from both Spain and the Filipino revolutionary government, the Americans set out to transform the Philippines into an independent, liberal democratic nation-state, to make it the "showcase of democracy in Asia." Colonial state-building in the Philippines thus followed a logic of "democratic tutelage": through exposure to the blessed American style institutions and habituation in democratic procedures and rules, the Filipinos would come to adopt democratic political identities. Such was, in the American administrators' eyes, "the greatest political experiment the world has ever seen" (Manila Times, Oct. 15,1907: 4). It goes without saying that this tutelary project would not play out precisely as the American administrators had planned nor hoped, that the Filipinos were not passive shapes nor blank slates to be molded nor written upon, that they would not unproblematically participate in the Americanized rituals ofdemocracy nor would they necessarily attach to such rituals Anglo-Americanized meanings. But on the other hand, it is not as if the Filipinos completely localized or domesticated the new democratic elements, as if they could not carry the semantical weight of the Americans' particular conception of "democracy", as if the Filipinos were but the Hispano-Malayan version of Sahlins's Hawaiins and "democracy" was their Captain Cook (before the "event" of course). No-what happened was neither here nor there, but somewhere in-between. Hence I will suggest that the making of American colonial democracy in the Philippines was precisely the making of the in-between, the making of a certain political hybridity-* certain hybrid state. This hybrid "state" registered at two levels. To draw from the Comaroffs' (1991: 5) useful distinction about the "state," hybridity registered both as an institutionalized political order and as a state-of-being. As a political order, hybridity was borne in the textbook sense: as an articulation, collapsing, or blending of two "species" into one, a combination of the political-institutional forms introduced by the Americans with extant, local political practices and meanings-the latter, as we will see, structured around kapangyarihan ("power") and reciprocity. But hybridity also registered as way of being, as a logic of political identity. And this, I will suggest, was not a hybridity in which two "species" were combined. Tutelage and the Terms of Liberal Goveramentality A bit more must be said, first, of the Americans' project of "democratic tutelage." Much was involved in that project indeed, but I wish to stress only two aspects of it here. First, the project centered around the construction of a colonial state apparatus mirroring the metropolitan, liberal-democratic state: it had local governments relatively autonomous from the federal level, a national assembly, liberal judiciary, and a sort of executive (all to operate ideally according to the seperation of powers principle), plus a system ofelections with a restricted but eventually expanded electorate-not to mention legalized political parties, an American-style bill of rights, and so on. Filipinos were to take a participatory role within this state-or, as the administrators called it, this "school of politics"-so that they could learn the ways of Anglo-democracy (May 1980: 41; Hayden 1950: 262-3; Jones 1924). The Filipino elite-the largely mestizo class who were educated, wealthy, and had had some semblance of political power under the prior Spanish regime-were initially to take the most active part in state management (Cullinane 1989: 15-48). Because the American policy-makers saw them (as opposed to the "ignorant" masses) as the most capable of political leadership, they arranged it such that the elite could monopolize the newly-created political offices at both the local and central level (Special Report of Taft [hereafter cited as SRT] 1908: 24-26). But at the same time, since Americans believed that the elites-cum-officials still needed tutelary guidance and discipline, they built a hierarchical system of supervision and surveillance into the state apparatus. Elections were to be closely safeguarded through complex procedures and rules and monitored by American watchmen; each locally-elected Filipino official was to be supervised by an American field agent; central-level bureaucrats and administrators were to be supervised by an American department head; the National Assembly was to be checked by the American-dominated Philippine Commission, and so on (Cullinane 1971; Jenista 1971; Laurel 1931: 136). Such an arrangement of state would enable the American administrators and field agents to provide "object lessons" in liberal governmentality to the Filipinos (Jones 1924). Most important about this program of "practical political education" are the "object lessons" which the elite were to learn. In essence, the lessons were to be in legal-rationality. In fact, in more ways than one did legal-rationality figure to the American administrators as democracy's equivalent. This is not surprising, considering that the early policy-makers and administrators were of the Northeastern, urban Progressivist mold which conceived of legal-rational reforms as the best means by which to realize proper democratic governance. The emphasis on legal-rationality is also not surprising considering the Americans' perception that the major impediment to proper democratic governance in the Philippines were the political practices of the Filipino elites. The elites, the Americans believed, had historically abused their political powers for their own ends. Under the Spanish regime and during their temporary Filipino revolutionary government, they had used theirpolitical privileges in neglect ofthe "public interest," creating a self-serving "oligarchy" (SRT 1908: 23-24; Taylor 1903). Such exercises of power on the part the Filipino elite figured to the Americans as the mark of undemocratic identities (Cullinane 1971: 15-18; Special Report of the U.S. Secretary of War 1910). Thus equating liberal-democratic government with legal-rationality, the administrators' endeavoured to teach the Filipino political elite the discipline of rule-oriented action. Legal-rationality made up the very terms of the Americans' envisioned liberal governmentality in the Philippines.
