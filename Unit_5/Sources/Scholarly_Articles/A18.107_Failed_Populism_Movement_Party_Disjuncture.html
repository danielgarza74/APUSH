FAILED POPULISM: MOVEMENT-PARTY DISJUNCTURE IN NORTH CAROLINA, 1890 TO 1900* KENT REDDING University of North Carolina, Chapel Hill I examine the relationship between social movements and political parties through the study of late-nineteenth-centuiy Populism in North Carolina. I use county-level data on North Carolina Farmers' Alliance membership and People's Party votes to test theories on the social-structural bases of the two organizations and gauge the relationship between them. The results indicate a striking disjuncture between Alliance membership and People's Party support and suggest a new interpretation of the defeat of Populism. The results also provide the basis for analysis of the different organizational and mobilizational processes of move- ments and parties. Additional study of movement-party relationships should improve our understanding of Populism and other insurgent political movements as well. T hroughout electoral history social move- ments in search of political power have trans- formed themselves into political parties, often with quite limited success. Important examples include the temperance and agrarian movements, as well as efforts by leftist and labor groups and the Progressives of the 1940s. The National Or- ganization for Women is one of the latest move- ments to make third party rumblings. The constitutional impediments to third party formation in the United States are well known. What remains unclear, however, is the impact of movement-party dynamics on the viability of these efforts. By and large, studies of such move- ments have left movement-party relationships unanalyzed (see, for example, Lipset [1950] 1968; Blocker 1976; Gusfield 1963; Laslett 1970; Un- ger 1964; Markowitz 1973). Most analysts of Populism have followed this tendency, suggesting that the Farmers' Alliance was simply taken over by the People's Party and that movement members served as the crucial electoral base of the party (Hicks [1931] 1961; Goodwyn 1978). Such assumptions seem intu- itively plausible; after all, one would expect that people who devote time, effort, and money to a movement will support the political party that embodies the movement's ideals. Still, other works cast doubt on the equation of movements and parties. Schwartz (1976), for ex- ample, argued that the Farmers' Alliance's move to politics involved not merely a change in strat- egy, but also a shift in organizational structure. Other analysts noted the different mobilization tactics involved in forming and maintaining iden- tity in movements and in aggregating interests in parties (McNall 1988; Przeworski and Sprague 1986). Finally, movements and parties operate in substantially different organizational settings. Clearly, significant structural differences exist between the two types of organizations. None- theless, the literature is oddly silent on the rela- tionship between movements and parties. Impor- tant questions remain unanswered or, indeed, unasked: What, for example, is the general na- ture of this relationship? How does the transition from movement to party work out in practice? How important is it? How do these structural differences and historical contingencies interact? What are the consequences of the failure or suc- cess of such transitions?' I analyze previously over- looked county-level data on the state Farmers' Alliance and votes for the People's Party. I use a two-equation multiple regression model to test competing theories of the social-structural bases of the movement and party. The results show that the structural factors un- derlying the two organizations were substantial- ly different. I argue that divergent organizational and mobilizational processes underlie these dif- ferences and that they suggest the broader diffi- culties that mass movements face when they turn explicitly to electoral strategies. Finally, I find that linkage problems have a crucial bearing upon the possibility of success for the overall move- ment, as well as for the continued viability of its constituent organizations. HISTORICAL CONTEXT All U.S. farmers faced a hostile economic climate in the latter decades of the nineteenth century. Deflationary federal monetary policies pushed up the price of credit, while forcing down farm com- modity prices. The South Southern farmers faced additional onerous bur- dens. War brought the wholesale destruction of fields, livestock, local exchange networks, farm equipment, and land values. Emancipation wiped out nearly half of the real capital of the major cotton-producing states (Ransom and Sutch 1977, pp. 52-53), while creating the need for an entire- ly new labor system. In addition, the South did not receive a portion of the nation's money sup- ply commensurate with its economic activity (Laird and Rinehart 1968). Low monetary sup- ply, depleted assets, and declining land values prevented most southern farm owners from mort- gaging their land to buy the supplies and equip- ment they needed. The eventual solution to southern labor, capi- tal, and credit problems was the notorious crop lien system, which forced farmers to grow a cash crop, usually cotton. In this system, local land- lords and merchants advanced goods in return for liens on crops. The credit prices charged to the southern farmers were exorbitant, sometimes more than 100 percent. Merchants also charged higher prices for goods bought with credit, with an average markup of more than 25 percent (Schwartz 1976, pp. 35-39; Laird and Rinehart 1968). Though this system kept blacks growing cot- ton, it also brought many white subsistence farm- ers into steady commercial farming for the first time. With rising credit and declining cotton prices many yeomen, tenants, and sharecroppers were forced into an endless cycle of peonage, while others hovered near the brink of indebtedness. Thus, the crop lien impoverished many southern farmers as it forced them into steady commercial farming. Census reports show that the produc- tion of corn and swine (two main subsistence products) declined substantially from 1860 to 1890. Meanwhile, cotton production more than doubled over the same period, even as cotton prices declined by nearly 70 percent from 1870 to 1890 (U.S. Department of Interior 1897). Coincident with these economic problems was the emergence of social and political movements that attempted to alleviate farmer grievances. Farmer movements tried three basic strategies for dealing with the agricultural crisis: better farm- ing methods, alternative economic institutions, and political movements. The failure of "scien- tific farming" to address the structural problems they faced in commodity and credit markets in- cited a group of Texas farmers to form the Farm- ers' Alliance in the late 1870s. By the mid-1880s, the Alliance had adopted the strategy of local- and state-level economic cooperatives as the salvation for farmers. Gener- ally, the local strategy took one of two forms: Farmers organized into local Alliances could ei- ther band together and bargain with the merchant for lower prices or form their own cooperative store so as to bypass the "middlemen" (Schwartz 1976). The Alliance thus put the provision of real services for its members at the forefront of its program, in contrast to its predecessor organ- izations and many contemporary social move- ments. The Alliance sent lecturer-organizers across the South, who called on farmers to organize themselves behind the banner of cooperation and farmer independence. In the process, the Alli- ance developed a federated organizational struc- ture, the basic units of which were the local sub- alliance, the county Alliance, and the state Alli- ance, all operating under the umbrella of the na- tional organization. Economic cooperation initially appeared to be a successful challenge to the status quo.
