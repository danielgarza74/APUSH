"We would say 'The Cattle Towns' is quite trustworthy history," conceded the writer, "[but] the book is not something we are likely to give away to tourists as reading material."6 The subtext here was that after some eighty years Dodge still had a media image to protect. Back in the 1870s and 1880s, its editors, like those of the other cattle towns, hoped the outside world would conceive of their community as filled with imposing public build- ings, thriving businesses, lively schools, well-attended churches, and industrious citizens. But by the 1960s the Dodge City Globe fretted that potential sightseers might lear that the frontier town had not been a lawless hellhole. The writer needn't have worried. In the 1990s Dodge City retains its status as a universal metaphor for violence and civic anarchy. A rash of killings puts New York's mayor on the defensive. "There is law and order," he insists. "Things are not out of control and this is not Dodge City." In Israel a Jewish militant empties his weapon into a crowd of Muslims. A report is headlined "Hebron: Dodge City on the West Bank." The latest crime fad, says Newsweek, is sticking up tollway booths. Toll collectors are arming, and officials worry about "turning the nation's highways into something out of Dodge City."7 Disillusioning tourists was not in mind when I began researching the five major Kansas cattle-trading centers.
