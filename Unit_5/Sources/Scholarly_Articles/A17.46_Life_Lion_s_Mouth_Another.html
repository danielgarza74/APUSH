LIFE "IN THE LION'S MOUTH": ANOTHER LOOK AT BOOKER T. WASHINGTON LAWRENCE J. FRIEDMAN By the end of the nineteenth century, the Southern racial climate was becoming increasingly inhospitable to the hopes and aspirations of most blacks. An unprecedented number of lynchings and widespread disfranchisement made this glaringly apparent. While Southern racial atrocities increased, the white North refused to intercede. There, writ- ers like Thomas Nelson Page and Thomas Dixon, Jr., were in vogue-men who justified the pervasive Southern solutions to the "Negro problem." During the first World War, millions of white Americans, North and South, attended D. W. Griffith's film spectacu- lar "The Birth of a Nation." They watched allegedly heroic Ku Klux Klansmen on the screen brutally assault "wild" Negroes to preserve white civilization. Some subsequently gathered together, visited "nigger-towns" throughout the nation, and slaughtered blacks in a horrendous wave of racist violence.' One consequence of this wave of white terror was an intensifying separatist feeling among Southern blacks. There were new and vigor- ous efforts to make black life independent of white attitudes and ac- tions. The overwhelming number of Southern blacks re- mained poor and unskilled agricultural laborers, and there were pre- cious few black professionals.5 Whites continued to dominate most aspects of Negro education and determined the success or failure of most all-black towns.6 Although Booker T. Washington was a per- petual optimist on black economic development, he calculated that no fewer than 30,000 additional businesses would have to be established before blacks could even begin to consider themselves "a nation within a nation. . ..7 Despite certain separatist ventures, therefore, black Southerners had little chance of successfully resisting the effects of intensifying white racism. When, for example, they tried to circumvent segregated white streetcar lines by organizing lines of their own, deficient re- sources told the story. White bondholders foreclosed, and electric companies refused to supply power.8 When white-owned women's shops in Louisville refused to let blacks try on garments before making purchases, it was very difficult to stage an effective boycott.
