AUGUST MEIER Kent State University & ELLIOTT RUDWICK Kent State University Negro Boycotts of Jim Crow Streetcars in Tennessee* THE PRELUDE TO THE CIVIL RIGHTS REVOLUTION OF THE MID-20TH CENTURY was the Montgomery bus boycott of 1955-56. It was unknown at the time that the transit boycotts of the 1950s had their predecessors in the late 19th and early 20th centuries when segregation was instituted on the streetcars in southern cities. The protests took place in all the states of the former Confederacy. In Tennessee, as the southern streetcar segregation movement reached its crest in 1905, such boycotts occurred in every one of the state's major cities. Actually, the first attempt to require segregation on the state's trolley cars took place in 1903. On February 9, the Tennessee legislature passed an act requiring racial segregation on street railways in any county having 150,000 or more inhabitants.' Only metropolitan Memphis in Shelby County met this population requirement. *We wish to express appreciation to the Kent State University Center for Urban Regionalism for financial assistance in the preparation of this article. Due to a lack of manuscript and archival materials in the field of Negro history for the period covered by this article, and because such collections as are available to scholars (most notably the Booker T. Washington Papers) contain almost no references to the streetcar boycotts, it has been necessary to rely mainly on newspaper sources. In the footnotes which follow all of the Tennessee papers cited are white dailies except for the Nashville Clarion. The Clarion and all newspapers from other states which are referred to are Negro weeklies. Un- fortunately extant copies of the Tennessee Negro journals of 1905-6 are not available. 756 American Quarterly The bill had been the subject of an unusual amount of debate in the legis- lature. Other cities of the state had first been included and then excluded from its provisions,2 and there had been considerable sentiment for requir- ing completely separate cars or trailers for Negroes, rather than a compart- ment within the trolley cars.3 The opposition to segregation by the street- car company4 and the Negroes5 foundered on the rising wave of popular racism and discrimination. The Commercial-Appeal reflected white popu- lar opinion on the question well enough when it said: There can be no doubt that many of the negroes who travel on streetcars are very offensive to the white passengers, and as the latter are the mainstay of the sys- tem, they feel that they are entitled to some protection. Those negroes who are offensive are very offensive indeed. Negro women of this kind will crowd into seats where there is no room for them, sometimes almost sitting on the laps of white women; and it is the conduct of these creatures that has aroused such a sentiment against the negro generally.6 Yet the 1903 law never went into operation. First the Memphis Street Railway Company dragged its feet, and kept postponing the effective date.7 In the end the company took the matter into the courts, by refusing to obey the law, in order to test its constitutionality. On May 29 the Shelby County grand jury indicted the Memphis Street Railway for failing to separate the races. The company was promptly convicted and fined $200 in the criminal court of Shelby County, a decision just as promptly reversed on June 7 by the Tennessee Supreme Court, which held the streetcar segregation law unconstitutional. Its decision, however, was based on a legal technicality, rather than on the constitutional issue of racial separa- tion.8 At the next session of the legislature in 1905, another streetcar segrega- tion law was enacted, this time for the whole state.
