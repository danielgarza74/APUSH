A Note on the >'Aercantilistic Imperialism" of Alfred Thayer Mahan BY WALTER LAFEBER The decade of the 1890's, marked by the Spanish-American War, annexation of the Philippines, and the Open Door Notes, was a crucial period in American diplomatic history. A key to under- standing the decade can be found in the writings of an austere, scholarly, armchair captain in the United States Navy, Alfred Thayer Mahan. This sailor-turned-prophet constructed a tightly knit historical justification of why and how the United States could ex- pand beyond its continental limits. His writings greatly influenced such expansionists as Theodore Roosevelt, Henry Cabot Lodge, John Hay, Benjamin F. Tracy (Secretary of the Navy, 1889-1893), and Hilary A. Herbert (Secretary of the Navy, 1893-1897). American historians have defined Mahan's philosophy as "mer- cantilistic imperialism."' One cannot deny that an intense study of seventeenth and eighteenth century mercantile empires heavily influenced Mahan's thinking. Neither can it be denied that his comments on the merchant marine and a carrying trade strikingly resembled the ideas of former mercantilist authors. But it may be suggested that by placing Mahan inside this mercantilist con- text, and then using this context in analyzing Mahan and American expansion in the 1890's, it is possible to misunderstand both his contributions and American policy. A characterization of Mahan as a mercantilist tends to taint American expansion in this important decade with strong mer- cantilist colors. Like- wise, Mahan's thinking had few similarities with the conclusions of Thomas Mun, George Berkeley, and Daniel Defoe two centuries before. The motivations of American empire in the 1890's ema- nated from the peculiar economic and social characteristics of the day. The value of Mahan's writings is due to his understanding of these characteristics. Some of his tenets meshed perfectly with mercantilist princi- ples.2 Both philosophies agreed upon the necessity of expansion. The Captain, like the mercantilists, desired a favorable balance of trade. But on most points, Mahan differed. By doing so, he dis- played a keen awareness of the United Sates in which he lived. The mercantilists, for example, believed that tariffs were neces- sary in order to enjoy favorable balances of trade; but Mahan stressed that such a policy hindered the entry of American goods into foreign markets. Rather, he praised James G. Blaine's and Wil- liam McKinley's policy of reciprocity and their efforts to lower tariff walls. Viewing protection as "essentially a defensive meas- ure," Mahan (always on the offensive) concluded, "Reciprocity, increased freedom of movement, is the logical corollary of expan- sion."3 Nor did Mahan advocate the mercantilist view that the state was an economic unit, not a moral or religious one, and that the welfare of the state rated a higher priority than the welfare of the individual.
