Selection Overview

Welch notes that historians have studied the press and the Spanish-American War far more than its response to the costlier Philippine-American War, which began when the McKinley administration sought to impose sovereignty and Aguinaldo's government resisted. Classifying one hundred and eighty newspapers as for or against that policy as of January 1900, he cautions that these stances only roughly matched earlier treaty views.

"The Philippine Insurrection" and the American Press Richard Ε. Welch, Jr.* Agréât deal of attention has been lavished by American historians on the role of the press in explaining the Spanish-American War. Articles have analyzed the press reactions of particular regions as well as the incitement furnished by the papers of Hearst and Pulitzer, and several books have been devoted to the words and activities of American war correspondents in Cuba. It is then rather surprising that so little attention has been paid to the American press and its response to a far more costly and bloody war, the Philippine-American War of 1899-1902, usually and erroneously called "the Philippine Insurrection." This war which cost over seven thousand American casualties and six hundred million American dollars began on February 4, 1899, nearly six months after the armistice ending the Spanish-American War and some eight weeks after the signing of the Treaty of Paris by which the United States demanded and received the cession of the Philippine Islands from Spain. The specific occasion for the outbreak of war between the forces under General Emilio Aguinaldo and those of General Elwell S. Otis is the subject of some mystery and more confusion but the basic cause of the war is clear enough: the administration of William McKinley was determined to impose the sovereignty of the United States over the Philippine Islands while Aguinaldo and the revolutionary government at Malolos, having proclaimed the independence of the Republic of the Philippines, were determined to resist American sovereignty. Before analyzing the response of the American press to the Philippine-American War it is necessary to provide a few definitions. Papers will be referred to as PRO or ΑΝΤΙ and, like any blithe terms of classification, these are subject to confusion. It should be obvious that these terms will not distinguish those papers advocating support of the American army in the Philippines from those who sought American military defeat. Neither do these terms imply a distinction based upon an advocacy of commercial expansion as contrasted with diplomatic isolation. Many an ΑΝΤΙ paper carefully distinguished between the virtues of commercial expansion and the evils of colonialism. What is more, and what is more com plicated, these terms can not be considered synonymous with the positions of the Expansionists and Anti-Imperialists in the debate during the fall and winter of 1898-99 respecting the terms of the peace settlement following the Spanish-American War. There is a rough parallelism between the attitude of the press in January 1899 towards the Treaty of Paris and the attitude of the press a year later respecting "the Philippine Insurrection," but that correspondence is marked by many exceptions. By no means were all papers who supported confirmation of the Treaty of Paris advocates of the military and political aims of the McKinley administration twelve months later, and, conversely, certain papers to be labeled anti-imperialist in January 1899 were quite reconciled to the necessity of subjugating Filipino "rebels" by January 1900. In this essay a newspaper has been classified as PRO or ΑΝΤΙ in its response to "the Philippine Insurrection" on the basis of a single criterion: Did it, on balance, favor or oppose the diplomatic military policy of the McKinley administration to subjugate the Filipinos by force of arms and retain United States sovereignty over the Philippines for the forseeable future? January 1900 has been selected as the chronological check point for summarizing the response of the American press on the assumptions that by that point the war in Luzon had been pursued for over ten months and the initial confusion of many newspapers had settled into more identifiable varieties of response; the McKinley administration had by that date made explicit its determination to hold the Philippines for an indefinite period of time as well as to "assimilate" the Filipinos; and the presidential campaign of 1900 had not yet gained sufficient momentum to skew the response of the press by the influence of partisan loyalty and animosity. One hundred and eighty American newspapers have been categorized — in some cases, possibly coerced — as PRO or ΑΝΤΙ. The author has not systematically curried the files of each of these one hundred and eighty papers; he has personally consulted only one-quarter of them, some forty-seven.
