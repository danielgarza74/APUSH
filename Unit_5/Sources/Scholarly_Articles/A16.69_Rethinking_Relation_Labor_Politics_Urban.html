ARTICLES Rethinking the Relation of Labor to the Politics of Urban Social Reform in Late Nineteenth Century America: The Case of Chicago Richard Schneirov Indiana State University In the past decade many "new ?abor historians" have shifted their emphasis away from a narrowly focused social and cultural history and, without abandoning social historical insights, have reengaged vigorously with polit ical history. In addition to their earlier interest in defining a "working-class politics" and tracing how it fared within the electoral system, labor histo rians recently have begun to reassess the larger periodizing concepts in political history, including those relating to party formation, political mo bilization, political language and ideology, and political culture.1 The result has been significant progress toward the goal first articulated by Herbert Gutman: to rethink the basic building blocks of American history in such a way as to take full account of the experiences, aspirations, and movements of working people and other subordinate groups studied by social histo rians.2 Historians of workers and politics of the late nineteenth century who pursue Gutman's task must contend with at least two important articles of conventional historiographical wisdom that impede fresh thought. One of these is the belief, widely held by labor historians, that a radical and class conscious brand of politics, centered around the Knights of Labor, gave way in the late 1880s and early 1890s to a philosophy of business or pure and-simple trade unionism grounded in the practices of the American Fed eration of Labor (AFL). The corollary of this belief is that the radical mass politics of the Knights of Labor were grounded in a class-wide constituency mobilized during labor's "great upheaval" of the mid-1880s, as against the trade union politics practiced by the AFL, grounded in a skilled craft worker constituency mobilized by narrowly based business unions. With regard to these two contrasts, the "new" labor historians have not differed substantially from the "old" labor historians, except for different valua tions of the Knights of Labor and the AFL.3 A second obstacle to a creative rethinking of the role of labor in late ninteenth-century U.S. politics is inherent in the conclusions reached by urban political historians. According to the influential definition provided by Thelen, mugwump reform politics of the 1880s re lied on a social base of elite citizens, comprised of respectable businessmen and professionals known as the "better sort"; manifested itself in nonpar tisan crusades around the issues of temperance, vice, patronage, and gov ernment retrenchment; and was directed at party bosses and local politi cians who served them. The protoprogressive movement of the 1890s, on the other hand, relied on a socially diverse, cross-class base of citizens who viewed themselves as consumers of urban services and were united by the belief that business, not bad men, corrupted politics. Progressive reformers of this decade proposed to remove the givers of bribes from politics through direct democracy and the municipal ownership of local utilities, Nord has amended this definition with the argument that the press played an agenda-setting and mobilizing role in creating a new issue-oriented, mass politics. In each case, however, the authors of these studies attribute the new politics of the 1890s to an emerging social consciousness among middle- and upper-class reformers and largely ignore the importance of "the social or labor question" and the impact of the labor movement on the creation of a mass urban politics.4 If the work of the old and the new labor historians and the work of urban political historians are viewed together, a strange disjuncture emerges between the mid-1880s and the 1890s. The labor movement seems to be moving in a conservative direction from the Knights to the pure-and simple trade unionism of the AFL at the very time that the middle class is jettisoning elitist reform for mass politics. Historians have the choice of accepting or rejecting this disjuncture, or attempting somehow to rein terpret these apparently opposite-tending political movements. In this es say I propose that we consider discarding or greatly modifying key ele ments of the conclusions of labor and urban historians and consider elements of a reinterpretation based on my research into the relation of the labor movement and urban politics in Chicago between the Civil War and 1895.5 Fundamentally, this reinterpretation involves two propositions. First, rather than narrowing its base to skilled workers and abandoning radical, mass politics after the rapid decline of the Knights of Labor, the labor movement retained a concern with organizing and incorporating unskilled and semi-skilled industrial workers and it developed what may be called a "laboristic politics" that was compatible with an agenda of urban progres sive reform, including government ownership of monopolies. Second, the decisive break with mugwump reform politics leading in the direction of progressive politics occurred in the last half of the decade of the 1880s and early 1890s before the 1893-97 depression, with critical input from the labor movement.
