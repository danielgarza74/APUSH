401 REINFORCING MANLINESS: BLACK STATE MILITIAS, THE SPANISH-AMERICAN WAR, AND THE IMAGE OF THE AFRICAN AMERICAN SOLDIER, 1891–1900 Andrew D. Amron Over the course of four sweltering July days in 1898, United States soldiers assaulted a number of well-defended Spanish positions on the hills surrounding Santiago, Cuba. Describing the carnage and the actions of both the white and African American regiments, the Salt Lake City Broad Ax noted that Theodore Roosevelt’s famed Rough Riders “were ambushed by the Spaniards and would have been utterly annihilated had not these black men gone to the rescue, and driv- en the Spaniards into retreat. On the memorable day when the battle raged . . . and it looked as though our army was doomed to defeat, these Negro soldiers, these heroes of the American army, went bravely up the hill of San Juan, captured the blockhouse before the fire of Spanish bullets as thick as hail in a hail storm and thus won the battle to the honor of our country.”1 This interpretation of the events near Santiago, however, was not the narrative that appeared in the mainstream U.S. press. There, the heroism of the African American regiments was greatly downplayed, ignored, or twisted into tales of cowardice, leaving future-President Roosevelt’s miraculous survival and victory alone in the spotlight. The development of these two competing memories can be explained by the handful of African American newspaper editors, including Julius F. Taylor of the Broad Ax, who felt compelled to address the general lack of recognition of black heroism during the Spanish-American War. In the weeks following the heroic vic- tories at El Caney and Santiago, Harry C. Smith, editor of the Cleveland Gazette, often reminded his readers that the four regiments of African American soldiers performed as well as any white regiment. And these shining beacons of black manhood were certainly needed amid the thickly toxic atmosphere of mob violence, disenfranchisement, and legal or Jim Crow segregation. In the decades following the Civil War, images of “Negro domi- nation,” in both politics and the bedroom—whether real or imagined—struck a raw nerve in many white Americans.4 During the Jim Crow era, countless instances of lynchings, beatings, and threats of violence directed at black men went unpunished. The combination of legal disenfranchisement and extralegal violence proved to be a formidable opponent to the psyche of many African American men. The daily peril African American men experienced was born out of southern white male feelings of insecurity following the era of Reconstruction. The original and most frequently assumed reasons for racist violence revolved around a southern code of honor and the “Lost Cause” ideology.5 This code encompassed two general spheres: personal honor and community honor. At the heart of both lay the protec- tion (by violence if necessary) of womanly virtue. As time passed and the memories of slavery faded, many southern white men perceived a new generation of young, uncontrollable black men who posed a new threat to the presumed sexual purity of white women. “Defending white womanhood” had thus become the excuse and explanation given for white racial violence aimed at African American men. Relating gender construction and violence in the Jim Crow South, historian Timothy Tyson noted that “in a world that lacked healthier conceptions of male identity, violence in the defense of womanhood did much to define both whiteness and manhood.”6 The honor code also provided the southern white population a way to cast African Americans as the “heathens” or “savages.” The antithesis of white female purity was the image of the immoral, uncivilized “black beast rapist.” In constructing these popular images of whiteness and blackness, white men were able to justify their racially motivated violence by concluding that African Americans were not equal, justifying their self-defined white supremacist beliefs. In the early 1890s as she inves- tigated the truth behind white southerners’ violent behavior, journalist Ida B. Wells grimly concluded that “humanity abhors the assailant of womanhood, and this charge upon the Negro at once placed him beyond the pale of human sympathy.”7 Whites could attack unarmed African American men without disturbing their conscience.8 But as Ida B. Wells and others probed more deeply into the causes and excus- es given for racial violence, a more sinister plot became apparent.
