Selection Overview

Montgomery examines what the long history of strike behavior reveals about American workers' changing consciousness. He reviews Griffin's findings that defensive strikes clustered in economic crises and failed while aggressive ones came in good times, and that participation leapt in years such as 1886 and 1894, along with modernization theorists who saw protest declining.

He concluded, among other things, that defensive strikes tended to cluster in periods of economic crisis and meet with defeat, while strikes in times of economic buoyancy usually represented aggressive efforts by workers to improve their conditions and began on dates and in seasons of the workers' choosing (such as a springtime Monday after payday). To this clear evidence of a certain rationality in workers' conduct of strikes, Griffin added the observations that the tendency of workers to unionize grew more rapidly than their participation in strikes, and that the most highly unionized occupations were also the most strike prone. He also noted that, though the moving average of the number of workers involved in strikes each year formed a steadily rising line, there were years of extreme deviation from that trend. Strike participation in 1886, 1894, 1903, 1912, 1913, and every year between 1916 and 1922 leapt high above the trend line. No correlation with business cycles could adequately explain those leaps. More recent efforts to formulate a general theory of strike develop- ment have been informed by modernization theory. Ross (1954: 32) traced "the natural history of the strike" and concluded that in "the hands of the business union the strike has not only been invested with rationality, strategy, and discipline but has also been adjusted to the economic and political mores of the community." Similarly, Kerr et al. (1964: 184) argued that "worker protest in the course of industrialization tends to peak relatively early and to decline in intensity thereafter."
