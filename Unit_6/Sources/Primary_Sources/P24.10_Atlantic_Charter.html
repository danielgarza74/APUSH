Primary Source P24.10
The Atlantic Charter
Franklin D. Roosevelt and Winston S. Churchill · August 14, 1941
 
TOC FIT Preparing America for War: The Atlantic Charter (1941)
WHY HERE Four months before the United States entered the war, its president and Britain’s prime minister jointly announced eight principles for the world they hoped to build after “the final destruction of the Nazi tyranny.”
TREATMENT Public-domain text, complete
Historical Context

In August 1941 President Franklin D. Roosevelt and Prime Minister Winston Churchill met secretly aboard warships anchored in Placentia Bay, off Newfoundland. It was their first wartime meeting. Britain had been at war with Germany for nearly two years, and Germany had invaded the Soviet Union in June. The United States was not at war, but through Lend-Lease it was supplying Britain with weapons and food. At the end of the meeting the two leaders released this joint statement to the press on August 14. It was not a treaty and was never ratified; it was a declaration of shared aims.

The eight points echo Woodrow Wilson’s Fourteen Points and Roosevelt’s own “Four Freedoms” speech of January 1941: no territorial gains, self-government, open trade, economic cooperation, freedom of the seas, and disarmament of aggressors. Notice that a neutral country is announcing war aims alongside a nation at war, and that the sixth point speaks of a peace that would follow “the final destruction of the Nazi tyranny.” Some noninterventionists saw the statement as proof of how far Roosevelt had already committed the country. Notice also what the charter leaves vague, such as whether self-government applied to Britain’s colonies.

Editorial note. The text follows the Avalon Project transcription. The leaders’ names appear at the end as in that transcription; the statement was released as a press communiqué rather than signed as a treaty.
Document Text

The President of the United States of America and the Prime Minister, Mr. Churchill, representing His Majesty’s Government in the United Kingdom, being met together, deem it right to make known certain common principles in the national policies of their respective countries on which they base their hopes for a better future for the world.

First, their countries seek no aggrandizement, territorial or other;

Second, they desire to see no territorial changes that do not accord with the freely expressed wishes of the peoples concerned;

Third, they respect the right of all peoples to choose the form of government under which they will live; and they wish to see sovereign rights and self government restored to those who have been forcibly deprived of them;

Fourth, they will endeavor, with due respect for their existing obligations, to further the enjoyment by all States, great or small, victor or vanquished, of access, on equal terms, to the trade and to the raw materials of the world which are needed for their economic prosperity;

Fifth, they desire to bring about the fullest collaboration between all nations in the economic field with the object of securing, for all, improved labor standards, economic advancement and social security;

Sixth, after the final destruction of the Nazi tyranny, they hope to see established a peace which will afford to all nations the means of dwelling in safety within their own boundaries, and which will afford assurance that all the men in all lands may live out their lives in freedom from fear and want;

Seventh, such a peace should enable all men to traverse the high seas and oceans without hindrance;

Eighth, they believe that all of the nations of the world, for realistic as well as spiritual reasons must come to the abandonment of the use of force. Since no future peace can be maintained if land, sea or air armaments continue to be employed by nations which threaten, or may threaten, aggression outside of their frontiers, they believe, pending the establishment of a wider and permanent system of general security, that the disarmament of such nations is essential. They will likewise aid and encourage all other practicable measure which will lighten for peace-loving peoples the crushing burden of armaments.

Franklin D. Roosevelt

Winston S. Churchill
