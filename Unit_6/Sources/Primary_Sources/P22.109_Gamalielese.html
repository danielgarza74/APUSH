Gamalielese
H. L. Mencken · March 7, 1921
 
TOC FIT Warren Harding and “Normalcy”: Harding’s Personality and Associates
WHY HERE Three days after the inauguration, the country’s best-known newspaper critic mocks Harding’s ideas and, above all, his prose.
TREATMENT Public-domain text, selected excerpts
Historical Context

H. L. Mencken (1880–1956) was a Baltimore newspaperman, magazine editor, and critic, famous in the 1920s for ridiculing politicians, preachers, reformers, and the “booboisie,” his word for the self-satisfied American middle class. He wrote a regular Monday column for the Baltimore Evening Sun, and this one appeared on Monday, March 7, 1921, three days after Warren G. Harding delivered his inaugural address (the “harangue of last Friday”). The title “Gamalielese” is a joke on Harding’s middle name, Gamaliel, coined as if Harding’s way of writing were a language of its own. Mencken’s readers were Baltimore newspaper subscribers, many of whom enjoyed his attacks whether or not they shared his views.

Mencken had disliked Woodrow Wilson too, and the column ends by pretending to miss the “seemly bosh of the late Woodrow” (Wilson was alive; “late” here means “former”). The column was written before any of the Harding administration’s scandals came to light, so it judges the man by his words alone. Notice the three phrases from the inaugural address that Mencken quotes and why he laughs at each, and notice what he thinks Harding’s style reveals about Harding and about the crowds he spoke to.

Editorial note. Excerpts as selected in Norton’s exercise. The ellipses and the bracketed letter in “[H]e” are the editor’s, not Mencken’s. The text could not be checked against the printed newspaper page, and a few phrases (such as “in place of stump speech”) may contain transcription slips.
Document Text

On the question of the logical content of Dr. Harding’s harangue of last Friday, I do not presume to have views. The matter has been debated at great length by the editorial writers of the republic, all of them experts in logic; moreover, I confess to being prejudiced. When a man arises publicly to argue that the United States entered the war because of a “concern for preserved civilization,” I can only snicker in a superior way and wonder why he isn’t holding down the chair of history in some American university.

When he says that the United States has “never sought territorial aggrandizement through force,” the snicker arises to the virulence of a chuckle, and I turn to the first volume of Gen. Grant’s memoirs. And when, gaining momentum, he gravely informs the boobery that “ours is a constitutional freedom where the popular will is supreme, and minorities are sacredly protected,” then I abandon myself to a mirth that transcends, perhaps, the seemly.

But when it comes to the style of a great man’s discourse, I can speak with a great deal less prejudice, and maybe with somewhat more competence, for I have earned most of my livelihood for twenty years past by translating the bad English of a multitude of authors into measurably better English. Thus qualified professionally, I rise to pay my small tribute to Dr. Harding. … [H]e writes the worst English I have ever encountered. It reminds me of a string of wet sponges; … it reminds me of stale bean soup, of college yells, of dogs barking idiotically through endless nights. It is so bad that a sort of grandeur creeps into it. … It is balder and dash.

… More scientifically, what is the matter with it? Why does it seem so … confused and childish, so stupidly at war with sense? … That answer is very simple. When Dr. Harding prepares a speech he does not think of it in terms of an educated reader locked up in jail, but in terms of a great horde of stoneheads gathered around a stand. That is to say, the thing is always a stump speech; it is conceived as a stump speech and written as a stump speech. …

Harding carries his stump manner into everything he writes. He is, perhaps, too old to learn a better way. He is, more likely, too discreet to experiment. … Read the inaugural address, and it will gag you. But hear it recited through a sound magnifier, with grand gestures to ram home its periods, and you will begin to understand it. …

But is such bosh out of place in stump speech? Obviously not. It is precisely and thoroughly in place of stump speech. A tight fabric of ideas would weary and exasperate the audience; what it wants is a simple loud burble of words, a procession of phrases that roar, a series of whoops. This is what it got in the inaugural address of the Hon. Warren Gamaliel Harding. And this is what it will get for four long years—unless God sends a miracle and the corruptible puts on incorruption…Almost I long for the sweeter song, the rubber stamps of more familiar design, the gentler and more seemly bosh of the late Woodrow.
