Minutes of the Biennial Convention of the National Association of Colored Women, Buffalo, July 1901
National Association of Colored Women · July 1901
 
TOC FIT The Varied Sources of Progressivism; Promotion of Social Justice: The Campaign Against Drinking
WHY HERE The record of the 1901 national convention of Black women's clubs shows their leaders reporting on temperance, children's and mothers' work, and club organizing, electing officers, and describing their ties to national women's organizations.
TREATMENT Public-domain text, selected excerpts
Historical Context

The National Association of Colored Women (NACW) was founded in 1896 in Washington, D.C., when two federations of Black women's clubs joined together under the motto “Lifting as We Climb.” Mary Church Terrell, an Oberlin graduate and Washington school-board member, served as its first president. In July 1901 the association met in Buffalo, New York, where the Pan-American Exposition was drawing visitors from across the country. This volume, held by the Buffalo History Museum, contains the convention's minutes in two forms: pages handwritten by the recording secretaries during the meeting, and pasted-in sheets of the printed minutes issued afterward. The excerpts come from both.

The NACW's clubs ran kindergartens, mothers' meetings, homes for the elderly and for working girls, and temperance work, and they set out to answer white writers who attacked the character of Black women. Many members also belonged to the Woman's Christian Temperance Union (W. C. T. U.), whose members wore a white ribbon. In these pages Terrell, the poet and reformer Frances E. W. Harper, and the incoming president, Josephine Silone-Yates of Kansas City, speak about women's duties to the home and the race. Notice what the delegates report doing, and which national organizations Silone-Yates says recognized the association.

Tuesday, July 11, 1901—Evening Session (handwritten minutes)

Meeting called to order. Mrs. Terril in chair. Music by the Buffalo Club Organization. Address of welcome by acting Mayor Kennedy of Buffalo. Music by a trio from the Buffalo Musical Organization.

Dr. Sarah [surname illegible] welcomed the visitors in behalf of the clubs of the city.

Mrs. Booker T. Washington responded briefly to the address of welcome and asked Mrs. B. K. Bruce to come and describe the work at Tuskegee. Mrs. Bruce referred especially to the Mothers’ meetings which are often attended by a thousand women. Mrs. Bruce also spoke of the cordial hospitality of the Buffalo people and the courteous attention displayed by strangers on the streets and by conductors on street cars.

The President, Mrs. Mary Church Terril then delivered an eloquent address. Mrs. Terrel said in part: “Five years ago the National Association of Colored Women was an experiment, tonight it is a magnificent success. The more civilization advances, the more clearly it is demonstrated that women by their influence make or mar the home, and that the responsibility resting upon all women therefore is ‹sectional› serious and great, but no woman does duty call more loudly than Negro women.”

Mrs. Terrel’s address was followed by a selection from the Buffalo Musical Organization.

This number concluded the evening program. Adjourned to meet Wednesday 10:30 A. M.

Mrs. Mary C. Terrel, Pres. Mrs. S. Lillian Coleman, Sec.

[. . .]

Thursday Afternoon Session (printed minutes)

Mrs Lucy Thurman then gave a most eloquent address, in which she gave a short reveiew of the work, the wonderful success and great amount of good which is being done by the W. C. T. U. Mrs. Thurman spoke also of the rapidity of its growth, and when she had finished not only those who wore white ribbon were impressed, but it was also plainly shown that the words, the earnestness of the speaker, her great devotion to the cause she has so long represented, had impressed many others.

Mrs. Francis E W. Harper of Philadelphia, was introduced, and amid rousing applause, the Chautauqua applause was given her. Mrs Harper talked as only she can talk; her greatest effort being to impress the young of the race the importance of espousing the cause which alone can give health, prosperity, and long life. Mrs Harper closed by reciting the story of the woman who came unto the Lord.

Mrs Judge Gray of California was presented to the audience, and she expressed herself as not having words in which to clothe her feelings.

Mrs. J Silone-Yates of Kansas City, spoke for the west, giving a historical sketch of the rise and progress of the work in that section, one case of rescue work which she mentioned was of special interest.

Miss Durgan of Tuskegee spoke of the temperance work in that section, which work she represented.

Miss Katherine Tillman of Chicago spoke of the work needed, not only in the halls of vice, but also in so-called society. The address was short but to the point.

Mrs Chapman of Buffalo, who is also engaged in the temperance work, as Secretary for Erie County, spoke chiefly of the work among children. One minute was then given to each one upon the platform who had not spoken, and nearly every one responded.

Mrs Scott of Lathrop, Missouri, sang “Down in the Licensed Saloon.” The applause was so continuous that Mrs Scott came forward and sang one of her own compositions, “Slavery is Dead.” Mrs Wilson, President of the Loyal Union of Buffalo, N. Y., read a paper on “Temperance,” which was well received.

In well chosen words, the Chairman then introduced Mrs Rosetta Lawson of Washington, D. C., a National Organizer in the work of the W. C. T. U. Mrs Lawson gave a very eloquent address, after which Mrs. Sterrett of Charleston, was introduced. Mrs. Sterrett made some very interesting remarks.

The Chairman introduced Mrs. Scott of Lathrop, Mo., who represented the work of the Baptist Home Missionary Association, and Mrs Scott gave a very interesting history of her work and success, after which, by special request, one verse of “Must Jesus Bear the Cross Alone” was sung.

Mrs B. K. Bruce was introduced, and in a most touching manner expressed her gratitude for having had the white ribbon pinned on her several years ago.

The program was then varied a little, and Mrs Fannie Hall Clint, “The Pride of Illinois,” recited “Discharged Again.” Mrs. Clint was heartily applauded.

The meeting was then turned over to the President, Mrs Terrell, and the regular routine of business conducted.

The National Organizer, Mrs Jerome Jeffrey, read a paper on “Organization.” The paper was very interesting, and many good ideas were expressed therein. Mrs L. A Davis, also read an excellent paper on the same subject. Mrs. Davis stated, among other things, that it was largely in the power of any organizer to make the clubs what they should be.

Both of these papers were well received.

The hour for adjournment closed the afternoon session.

MRS MARY CHURCH TERRELL, President. MISS E C CARTER, Secretary.

Friday, July 12, 1901—Morning Session

The Convention was called to order by the President, Mrs M. C. Terrell. After singing a hymn the minutes of the morning session were read, and on motion of Mrs West, approved. Minutes of Thursday afternoon were read, and on motion of Mrs. Thurman, approved.

The reports of the following officers were given, and on motion, approved:

Report of National Organizer, Mrs R. Jerome Jeffrey.

Report of Mrs Tate, State Organizer for Michigan.

Report of Mrs Jackson, State Organizer for Illinois.

Report of Mrs C A Curl, Recording Secretary, approved on motion of Mrs West.

Report of Mrs Josephine Silone-Yates, Treasurer, was read and, on motion of Mrs Anna Douglass, approved.

Report of Ways and Means Committee, Mrs Libbie C Anthony, Chairman, was read, and on motion of Mrs Thurman, approved.

Miss Terry, President of the Phillis Wheatley Club of Nashville Tenn., who wore the Ways and Means Committee medal, turned it over to Mrs Agnes Moody, President of the Woman’s Civic League. This Club paid over to the Ways and Means Committee fifteen dollars, thus winning the Committee’s medal for the ensuing term.

The election of officers was the next order of business.

The following officers were elected:

President, Mrs Josephine Silone-Yates.

First Vice-President, Mrs Booker T Washington.

Second Vice-President, Mrs. Agnes L Moody.

Corresponding Secretary, Miss Ionia Smallwood.

First Recording Secretary, Miss Elizabeth C Carter.

Second Recording Secretary, Mrs Susie J. Evans.

Third Recording Secretary, Miss Josephine Holmes.

Treasurer, Mrs. Libbie C Anthony.

National Organizer, Mrs L A Davis.

Chairman of Executive Board, Mrs Josephine B Bruce.

Mrs Thurman moved that Mrs M C Terrell be made Honorary President of the National Association of Colored Women with full powers and privileges. The motion was seconded by Mrs Bruce and carried. The following resolution was read and adopted:

Whereas, The National Association of Colored Women cannot afford to lose from its Executive Board a woman who for five years has had the experience, as president, of directing its policy; therefore, be it

Resolved, Mrs. Mary Church Terrell, first president of the National Association of Colored Women, upon retiring from office shall be made Honorary President of the National Association of Colored Women, with all powers and privileges, including that of the vote in the Executive Committee.

As the evening meeting was not a business meeting the session was then practically at an end, and closed to meet in St. Louis with the understanding that in the future no city would be chosen where the delegates could not be entertained at one dollar, or less, per day.

MRS MARY CHURCH TERRELL, President. MRS. C A CURL, Recording Secretary.

Friday, July 12, 1901—Evening Session

The regular program was carried out, and included several fine addresses and papers, the installation of the new officers and the presentation of a loving cup to the retiring or honorary president, Mrs Terrell.

The principal address of the evening was made by Mrs Josephine Silone-Yates, the newly elected president, on “Woman as a Factor in the Solution of Race Problems.”

Mrs Yates presented her subject in a most forcible manner, and showed that from early colonial days woman had played an important part in the growth and development of the American nation, that the colored woman is rapidly learning to shoulder and to bear her share of the responsibility that devolves upon every citizen of a great Republic; that she is gradually winning the respect and co-operation of the women of other races in this matter of the solution of race difficulties. Mrs. Yates spoke feelingly of the recognition accorded the National Association of Colored Women by the press of the country, by the National Council of Women of which the N. A. C. W. is a member, of the National Congress of Mothers in which it also had membership, of the National W. C. T. U., in which several of the national officers held high and honorable positions. She predicted that this co-operation of the women of different races on matters of universal importance in the elevation of humanity, would materially lessen prejudice and hasten the coming of the day when all shall acknowledge “The brotherhood of man and the fatherhood of God.”

Excellent papers were read by Miss Terry of Nashville, and others; and the session closed with the presentation speech, made in well chosen words by Mrs. Hester Jeffreys in behalf of the donors of the loving cup, and with a touching response from the recipient of the gift, Mrs M C Terrell.
