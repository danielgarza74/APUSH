Primary Source P24.50
Yasui v. United States
U.S. Supreme Court (Chief Justice Harlan F. Stone) · June 21, 1943
 
TOC FIT The Transformative Effects of World War: Japanese Americans during the War
WHY HERE The Supreme Court’s short opinion upholding Minoru Yasui’s conviction records how a young Oregon lawyer deliberately broke the military curfew so that he could challenge it in court.
TREATMENT Public-domain text, complete opinion of the Court
Historical Context

Minoru Yasui was born in Hood River, Oregon, in 1916 to Japanese immigrant parents. By early 1942 he was a lawyer and a second lieutenant in the Army reserve. When General John L. DeWitt imposed a nighttime curfew on people of Japanese ancestry in Portland, Yasui deliberately stayed out past the 8:00 p.m. curfew on March 28, 1942, and asked to be arrested so that the order could be tested in court. A federal district judge ruled that the law behind the curfew was unconstitutional as applied to American citizens but then held that Yasui had given up his citizenship by working for the Japanese consulate, and sentenced him to a year in prison. The Supreme Court decided his appeal on June 21, 1943, the same day as Hirabayashi v. United States.

Chief Justice Harlan F. Stone wrote this brief, unanimous opinion, which rests entirely on the reasoning in Hirabayashi. Notice what the Court records about Yasui himself: his schooling, his law degree, his Army commission, and his offer of service to the military right after Pearl Harbor. Notice also what the Court does and does not decide. It upholds the curfew conviction, sets aside the lower court’s finding that Yasui had lost his citizenship, and sends the case back only for a new sentence. Decades later, in the 1980s, Yasui, Gordon Hirabayashi, and Fred Korematsu reopened their cases after researchers found that government lawyers had withheld evidence that undercut the claim of military necessity.

Document Text
Mr. Chief Justice Stone delivered the opinion of the Court.

This is a companion case to Hirabayashi v. United States, ante, p. 81.

The case comes here on certificate of the Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, certifying to us questions of law upon which it desires instructions for the decision of the case. § 239 of the Judicial Code as amended, 28 U. S. C. § 346. Acting under that section we ordered the entire record to be certified to this Court so that we might proceed to a decision, as if the case had been brought here by appeal.

Appellant, an American-born person of Japanese ancestry, was convicted in the district court of an offense defined by the Act of March 21, 1942. 56 Stat. 173. The indictment charged him with violation, on March 28, 1942, of a curfew order made applicable to Portland, Oregon, by Public Proclamation No. 3, issued by Lt. General J. L. DeWitt on March 24, 1942. 7 Federal Register 2543. The validity of the curfew was considered in the Hirabayashi case, and this case presents the same issues as the conviction on Count 2 of the indictment in that case. From the evidence it appeared that appellant was born in Oregon in 1916 of alien parents; that when he was eight years old he spent a summer in Japan; that he attended the public schools in Oregon, and also, for about three years, a Japanese language school; that he later attended the University of Oregon, from which he received A. B. and LL. B. degrees; that he was a member of the bar of Oregon, and a second lieutenant in the Army of the United States, Infantry Reserve; that he had been employed by the Japanese Consulate in Chicago, but had resigned on December 8, 1941, and immediately offered his services to the military authorities; that he had discussed with an agent of the Federal Bureau of Investigation the advisability of testing the constitutionality of the curfew; and that when he violated the curfew order he requested that he be arrested so that he could test its constitutionality.

The district court ruled that the Act of March 21, 1942, was unconstitutional as applied to American citizens, but held that appellant, by reason of his course of conduct, must be deemed to have renounced his American citizenship. 48 F. Supp. 40. The Government does not undertake to support the conviction on that ground, since no such issue was tendered by the Government, although appellant testified at the trial that he had not renounced his citizenship. Since we hold, as in the Hirabayashi case, that the curfew order was valid as applied to citizens, it follows that appellant’s citizenship was not relevant to the issue tendered by the Government and the conviction must be sustained for the reasons stated in the Hirabayashi case.

But as the sentence of one year’s imprisonment—the maximum permitted by the statute—was imposed after the finding that appellant was not a citizen, and as the Government states that it has not and does not now controvert his citizenship, the case is an appropriate one for resentence in the light of these circumstances. See Husty v. United States, 282 U. S. 694, 703. The conviction will be sustained but the judgment will be vacated and the cause remanded to the district court for resentence of appellant, and to afford that court opportunity to strike its findings as to appellant’s loss of United States citizenship.

So ordered.
