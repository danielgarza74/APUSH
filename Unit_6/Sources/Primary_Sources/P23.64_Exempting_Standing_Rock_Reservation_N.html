Exempting Standing Rock Reservation, N. and S. Dak., from Wheeler-Howard Act
U.S. Senate, Committee on Indian Affairs (Lynn J. Frazier) · May 31, 1938
 
TOC FIT Continuing Hardships: Native Americans and the Depression
WHY HERE A Senate committee report records what Lakota and Dakota delegates from the Standing Rock Reservation told Congress about how the Indian Reorganization Act was brought to their reservation and why they wanted out of it.
TREATMENT Public-domain text, complete
Historical Context

Senator Lynn J. Frazier of North Dakota, a longtime critic of the Indian Bureau, submitted this short report for the Senate Committee on Indian Affairs in 1938. It recommended passage of S. 3745, a bill to exempt the tribes of the Standing Rock Reservation, home to Lakota and Dakota Sioux bands on the border of North and South Dakota, from the Indian Reorganization Act. Committee reports explain a bill to the full Senate and are printed in the Congressional Serial Set, the official series of congressional documents. This report summarizes the testimony of Standing Rock delegates at the committee’s hearings.

Under Section 18 of the act, a reservation came under the law unless a majority of its eligible voters voted against it, so people who stayed home were counted with the “yes” side. Standing Rock accepted the act in October 1934 on those terms and then twice rejected the constitution the Indian Office proposed. The report repeats the delegates’ charges against Bureau employees; they are testimony, not findings that anyone checked. Notice the names of the three council delegates and the vote totals, and compare this account with what Commissioner Collier said about the referendums.

Editorial note. The report is reproduced complete, from its first paragraph; the heading and the calendar and printing lines are left out (they appear in the citation). The printed date is “April 20 (calendar day, May 31), 1938.”
Document Text

The Committee on Indian Affairs, to whom was referred the bill (S. 3745) providing for the exemption from the provisions of the act of June 18, 1934 (48 Stat. L. 984), commonly known as the Wheeler-Howard Act, any Indian tribe on the Standing Rock Reservation, located in the States of North and South Dakota, having had the same under consideration, report thereon with the recommendation that it do pass.

The testimony of witnesses who appeared before the committee at the hearings held on this bill disclosed that a majority of the Indians on the Standing Rock Reservation were opposed to the Wheeler-Howard Act when the election to accept or reject the act was held but that many did not go to vote because they did not understand the provision that it took a majority voting against the act to reject it. The election was one of the first to be held after the act was passed on June 18, 1934. On October 27, 1934, 508 voted to reject the act, 668 voted for it, and 383 people did not vote, out of a total voting population of 1,559 people. Those who did not vote were recorded as having voted to accept the act. Witnesses testified that Bureau employees of the agency, and from Washington, conducted an intensive campaign to high pressure the Indians into accepting the act, holding meetings and talking with individual Indians, and that at election time all available cars at the agency, school busses, and I. E. C. C. trucks were used to take those who were in favor of the act to the polls, but those who opposed the act had to get to the polls the best way they could.

Since that time two elections have been held at Standing Rock, the first to adopt a constitution and the second to adopt both a constitution and a charter at the same time. Witnesses stated that the Bureau employees again campaigned among the Indians for both of these elections and that during the last campaign rations intended for the relief of old and needy Indians were used to feed those attending the Bureau meetings; that three people from the Rosebud Reservation were paid out of the tribal funds of the Standing Rock Tribe to come upon the reservation and campaign for the act and in all campaigns and elections all Government facilities were freely used to put over the constitution.

In the election held on November 2, 1935, the Indians rejected the constitution by a vote of 605 to 315.

Thereafter on April 16, 1938, another election was held at which the Indians rejected the constitution by a vote of 857 to 590. The charter was at the same time rejected by a vote of 829 to 556.

The Standing Rock Reservation has also voted to reject a so-called law-and-order code promulgated by the Indian Bureau, by an overwhelming majority.

The Indian witnesses were unanimous in the request that the Indian Bureau now refrain from further campaigns in the attempt to get these Indians to accept the provisions of the so-called Wheeler-Howard Act.

The committee are of the opinion that no good will come from any such further campaigns in the attempt to get these Indians to accept the provisions of said act. Therefore your committee earnestly recommend that the Commissioner of Indian Affairs be guided by the decisive vote of these Indians as the provisions of said act provides and its intent implies.

All testimony evidenced that chaotic conditions had resulted from the Wheeler-Howard Act on the reservation; that there is and has been continual trouble between those favoring the act and those opposing it, with feeling running high at all times. Arguments before the committee between the delegates from the Pine Ridge (S. Dak.) and Standing Rock (N. Dak.) Reservations who spoke in support of the present bill (S. 3745) and delegates from the Pine Ridge and Rosebud Reservations of South Dakota who favor the Wheeler-Howard Act and oppose S. 3903 were evidence of the intense feeling about the Wheeler-Howard Act.

Witnesses stated that the Bureau officials and Agency officials favor those who support the Wheeler-Howard Reorganization Act and discriminate against all those who oppose it; that the rights of free speech are denied to those opposing the act; that families have been broken up over it and there is constant and continual strife and turmoil on the reservation and conditions generally are the worst they have ever been on the Standing Rock Reservation.

Willis Mountain, secretary, Standing Rock Tribal Council, Mrs. Mary Many Wounds, and Mrs. Josephine Kelly, duly authorized delegates of the council, appeared and spoke in favor of the bill S. 3745.

Indian delegations from many other reservations have appeared before your committee and given testimony similar to that of the Indians from the Standing Rock Reservation.

The enactment of this proposed legislation will do much to settle the chaotic conditions which now exist among the Indians on the reservation.
