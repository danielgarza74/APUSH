Historical Context

The Suffragist was the weekly newspaper of the Congressional Union for Woman Suffrage, published in Washington, D.C., from November 1913. The Congressional Union began early in 1913 as the congressional committee of the National American Woman Suffrage Association (NAWSA), led by Alice Paul and Lucy Burns, two young Americans who had worked with militant suffragists in Britain. They organized the suffrage parade of March 3, 1913, the day before Woodrow Wilson's inauguration. The paper's first editor was Rheta Childe Dorr, a well-known journalist. This first issue printed Paul's front-page foreword and, on its editorial page, the text of the proposed federal amendment and several unsigned editorials.

In 1913 most suffrage work still went into state campaigns, many of which required a referendum of male voters, and women could vote fully in only nine states, all in the West. The editorials argue for a different strategy: win a constitutional amendment from Congress. Within months the Congressional Union split from NAWSA, and in 1914 it campaigned against Democratic candidates in the suffrage states, holding the party in power responsible. Notice how the paper uses the votes of western women as leverage, and what it says about the liquor interests in Montana.

Foreword

By ALICE PAUL, Chairman Congressional Union for Woman Suffrage

We send into the world today a new paper, the official organ of the Congressional Union for Woman Suffrage.

The purpose of this paper is to aid in securing an amendment to the Constitution of the United States enfranchising the women of the whole country.

Years of struggle for such an amendment now seem nearing success. Already woman suffrage is an assured fact in ten western and middle western States. Women vote on equal terms with men in nine States and one Territory. In Illinois, third State in population, and one of the most important politically, women have the presidential vote. More than one-fifth of the men in the United States Senate, and more than one-seventh of those in the House of Representatives even now represent a voting population of women. More than one-sixth of the electoral vote comes from suffrage States. With the leverage of this political strength in Congress, we can hope for the speedy accomplishment of our end, if suffragists will but stand shoulder to shoulder behind this amendment.

We call upon the administration and upon Congress to do their part towards removing from the Constitution this ancient sex discrimination. We ask consideration of the woman suffrage amendment immediately. There is no other issue comparable in importance to the elementary question of self-government for the women of America.

[. . .]

Salutatory.

AT THE head of this page we have placed the text of the proposed Federal amendment giving full suffrage to the women of the United States. We intend to keep it there until both Houses of Congress have voted favorably on the measure, and until three-fourths of the legislatures of the States have ratified the action of Congress. The work of the Congressional Union for Woman Suffrage will not cease until the Eighteenth Amendment is written into the Constitution. It will be vigorously continued until the present anomalous position of women citizens and tax-payers is changed to a position equal before the law to that of men citizens and tax-payers.

The Suffragist is not a propaganda newspaper. We take the stand that woman suffrage has progressed beyond the propaganda stage. It has been argued and debated and theorized about for sixty-five years. With practically no cessation its devoted advocates have kept up a steady and consistent campaign of education. Two generations of men and women have received the benefits of that education. There is hardly a town, village, or wayside hamlet in the United States that has not been reached by the suffragists; hardly a man or a woman in the United States who has not listened to suffrage speeches or read suffrage literature. There is not one single literate person, now in doubt, who can not easily and cheaply inform himself fully as to the merits of woman suffrage. It is a subject infinitely more familiar to voters than the tariff, the currency, or conservation. Therefore, we declare that woman suffrage has passed beyond propaganda and has reached its political stage.

We expect to convince voters and those who will certainly one day be voters that woman suffrage is purely a political issue. We hope to convince the Democratic Party, now in power, that it is the next great issue to be dealt with in Congress. We call upon the Democratic Party to extend its democracy to us. That does not mean that suffrage is a partisan measure. It is distinctly non-partisan. We call upon the Democratic Party simply because it happens at the present time to be the governing party.

President Wilson has said: “If any part of our people want to have guardians put over them, if they want to be taken care of, if they want to be children, patronized by the Government, why, I am sorry, because it will sap the manhood of America.”

If any part of the American people desire, as the President puts it, to live under trustees, it is distinctly not the enlightened and self-respecting woman part. If any considerable group of women are still content to exist as wards of government, why, we are sorry, because women, as mothers, wives, teachers, and fellow-workers of men, can do more sapping of American manhood than the men themselves. It is one of our objects to stir up a healthy discontent in the minds of all women not now actively connected with the suffrage movement.

We ask the support of all men and women who believe as we do that woman suffrage is a political issue of immediate importance; that it is not a woman question, but a community question, and that the time has come to settle it in the only right or possible way.

Federal Work the Economical Way.

THE Federal Amendment is obviously the most economical means by which we can secure the enfranchisement of women. Once the amendment has passed both Houses of Congress, it becomes the law when ratified by a majority vote in thirty-six State legislatures. It needs in no State a referendum to the people. The simple favorable majority vote in the legislature settles the matter for all time. If we continue to work State by State, we shall have to get a Suffrage bill through thirty-nine State legislatures, and in many States we shall have to get it through two successive legislatures. In all cases but one, the bill will have to be submitted to a referendum of the voters of the State. Which is the simpler task—converting man by man, a majority of the male population of the non-Suffrage States, something like seventeen million voters, or converting a majority of thirty-six legislatures?

Besides, it is not a simple question of converting voters. It is a question of destroying evil forces that control votes. Take the situation in Montana today. By a large majority the State legislature passed a Suffrage bill, which will go to the voters next year. There is practically no opposition to the measure on the part of the voters of the State. Yet an official of one of the strongest of all organized evils sent word to Jeanette Rankin, President of the State Suffrage Association, that his association would not permit the bill to pass “We will spend a million dollars, if necessary,” the message ran, “but we will defeat the Suffrage bill.” Montana is known as the “wettest” State in the Union; that is to say, the liquor traffic has less regulation there than elsewhere. Any organized evil can better afford to spend a million dollars than have women’s votes come into a community where such interests are strongly entrenched.

However, we want to make it quite plain that the Congressional Union hopes that no workers will for a single moment relax their efforts to win Suffrage by a State measure. We hope that every one of the campaign States will meet victory. We regret there is not a campaign on in every State in the Union. Every Suffrage victory increases the chances of the Federal Amendment. Because, with the creation of each new Suffrage State, an important thing happens in Washington, D. C. Two Senators and a group of Representatives make the interesting discovery that they have women as well as men constituents, and that the next time election day rolls round, their attitude towards equal suffrage will be famous and very much talked about in the women’s clubs.

Also, they would do well to remember that nearly all women voters have family breakfast tables.

But Don’t Misunderstand

We do not for a moment intend to convey the impression that Congressmen have to be intimidated into voting for a suffrage amendment. Exactly the contrary. During the discussion in the Senate on July 31st, when petitions from every State were presented, no fewer than twenty-two Senators made eloquent speeches in favor of the amendment. Eleven of these Senators represent non-suffrage States. In the course of his remarks, Senator Crawford of South Dakota said that while a suffrage measure had twice been defeated in his State, it was to go before the voters the third time in 1914, and he predicted its success. Senator Crawford added: “I have always voted for it. . . . I shall support it again in my State, and I have no hesitation in saying frankly that I shall vote for it here.” There is a suffrage campaign on in South Dakota, but there is none in Minnesota, Wisconsin, Michigan, or Louisiana. Yet Senator Clapp spoke at length in favor of the amendment, and so did Senator La Follette, Senator Smith, and Senator Randsdell.

This ought to be sufficient proof that the suffrage amendment will be considered in the Senate strictly on its merits, proof also that the men who are supporting it are sincere in their belief in our cause.

Having Said all This

We desire to call the attention of our friends in Congress to several plain and incontestable facts. We have been assured that Congress will attend to the suffrage amendment as soon as it has time and is not pressed with other business.

Now, it is well known that at the present time both Houses of Congress are meeting and adjourning without the transaction of business of any moment whatsoever. Frequently no quorum is present in either House. No other matter of importance is before Congress. The tariff is finished. The currency is through the House and is not yet in the Senate.

In view of this situation, there is absolutely no excuse for not proceeding at once to the consideration and the passage of the suffrage measure. Congress will never have more leisure than it has right now. Members are drawing large salaries, paid by women as well as men, yet some of them are travelling in Europe, others are campaigning in their constituencies. They are doing everything, in fact, except attending to the work of Congress. We therefore demand that they return to the Capitol and utilize the present unoccupied period to pass the suffrage bill.
