Primary Source P24.38
Oakland, Calif., Mar. 1942. A Large Sign Reading “I Am an American” Placed in the Window of a Store
Dorothea Lange, War Relocation Authority · March 1942
 
TOC FIT The Transformative Effects of World War: Japanese Americans during the War
WHY HERE A Japanese American grocery in Oakland, closed under removal orders, still displaying the “I AM AN AMERICAN” banner its owner put up the day after Pearl Harbor, shows a public claim of loyalty set against the government’s orders.
TREATMENT Public-domain image
Historical Context

Dorothea Lange, famous for her Depression-era photographs of migrant farm families, was hired by the War Relocation Authority (WRA) in 1942 to document the removal of Japanese Americans from the West Coast. She made this photograph in Oakland, California, in March 1942. The store was the Wanto Co. grocery, owned by the Matsuda family; according to the Library of Congress record, Tatsuro Matsuda, a University of California graduate, had the sign made and put it up on December 8, 1941, the day after the attack on Pearl Harbor. The picture shows the storefront with “GROCERY” and “FRUITS AND VEGETABLES” painted on it, the long banner reading “I AM AN AMERICAN” across the front windows, “WANTO CO.” lettered on the glass, and a parked coupe at the curb.

On February 19, 1942, Executive Order 9066 let the Army exclude people from military zones, and in the following weeks Army orders required everyone of Japanese ancestry to leave the West Coast, citizens and noncitizens alike. Families had days to sell or store businesses, homes, and belongings. Lange’s caption records that the store had closed and that its owner would be held in WRA centers “for the duration of the war.” Many of her WRA photographs were seldom seen by the public during the war. Notice how the sign and the caption work together: the owner declared his citizenship in large letters across his windows, and the government photographer recorded that the declaration did not change his fate.

Editorial note. The caption is reproduced as the Library of Congress records it, including its bracketed correction of the store’s address; the original caption gave “13th and Franklin streets.”
