The Deportations Delirium of Nineteen-Twenty: A Personal Narrative of an Historic Official Experience
Louis F. Post · 1923
 
TOC FIT The First Red Scare
WHY HERE The Labor Department official who reviewed the deportation cases after the Palmer Raids describes the 1919 bombings, the January 1920 raids, and why he came to call the Red Scare a national "delirium."
TREATMENT Public-domain text, selected excerpts
Historical Context

Louis F. Post was a Chicago reform journalist who served as assistant secretary of labor under President Woodrow Wilson from 1913 to 1921. Immigration and deportation were then handled by the Department of Labor, so the people arrested in Attorney General A. Mitchell Palmer's raids could be deported only by Labor Department order. In the spring of 1920, while acting as secretary, Post reviewed the cases himself and canceled a large share of the warrants, insisting on evidence and fair hearings. Angry members of Congress tried to impeach him, and he defended himself before the House Rules Committee. He published this account in 1923 with the socialist publisher Charles H. Kerr of Chicago, with an introduction by the Boston lawyer Moorfield Storey.

The book is a memoir written by a participant to defend his own record, three years after the events. Post does not deny that real bombs exploded in 1919: mail bombs in the spring, and attacks on homes in several cities on the night of June 2, including one at Palmer's house. His argument is about what came next. Notice how he describes the press, the Justice Department's detectives, and the public, and how he compares the evidence collected in the January 1920 raids with the fear that justified them.

Chapter IV. The Bombs That Begot the Deportations Delirium

The newspapers of early May, 1919, blazed with accounts of an interception in the mails at New York of sixteen explosive packages. Subsequent investigation showed that these packages had been prepared with deadly and malicious skill. Excitement over this discovery was the more intense because “the first of May” had long been a stock subject for prophecies of evil doings among or in behalf of wage-earning classes. That date, the European Labor-day, had been adopted by European terrorists for hinting at Labor uprisings and for an occasional assassination. In the European imagination, consequently, “May-day” became an anniversary to be anticipated with fear. And this trans-Atlantic nervousness had spread until annual rumors of “May-day” outrages disturbed American imaginations also; though for many years no “May-day” outrages occurred in the United States to verify the annual rumors. In 1919, however, there appeared to be confirmation in the discovery in the mails of those explosive packages just in time for a “May-day” sensation.

[. . .]

With reports of the timely frustration of this death-dealing plot, except in the one sad instance of the maid at Senator Hardwick’s home, the newspapers were filled. Whatever the event in itself lacked for exciting horror and fear, they supplied abundantly in daily reports of detective operations. The entire “bomb squad” of the New York City police, the bomb experts of the Fire Department, postal inspectors, private detectives, detectives of the Department of Justice, all were “sleeplessly” at work on the case. Every day there was a multiplicity of “leads,” hither and yon, each giving promise of “results” that were never realized. Arrests were “imminent,” but none were made except arbitrarily and for “third degree” purposes, the victims being persons of few friends and these without influence. There were “round-ups” that made startling headlines, but at the “showdowns” there was “nothing doing.” One nerve-thrilling discovery pointed to a mythical “Committee of Five” as the “most desperate band of anarchists ever organized in the United States”—according to an “undercover man” in the employment of the Federal Government. One detective who at first was thoughtful enough to suspect that the bombs had been sent by some irresponsible crank, quickly came to the orthodox police-conclusion that “the whole thing was a gigantic conspiracy by an organized gang.” And so the hue and cry clattered on, with every now and again a police assurance to the reporters that “the net tightens” or “the lightning is about to strike.”

[. . .]

Hardly, however, had that “May-day” terror subsided when the public was terrorized anew by the June bombardments. From no less than seven cities there came reports on the 3d of June, 1919, of bombing assaults upon residences the night before. From Philadelphia two explosions were reported, one in the rectory of a Roman Catholic church and the other at the residence of a business man. In Paterson the reported object of attack was the residence of a silk manufacturer. In Boston it was the residence of a judge—that of a Congressman in one of the suburbs being also noted in the list. From Pittsburgh six explosions were reported, one of which damaged the residence of a Federal judge. At Cleveland the reported explosion was at the home of the Mayor. By none of those explosions was anyone injured. But in New York, where the residence of Judge Charles C. Nott was partly wrecked, a watchman in front of the house at the time of the explosion was killed by it.

The most sensational explosion of them all was at Washington. It damaged the front of Attorney General Palmer’s residence. Nobody was physically injured by it, however, except the wretch who is supposed to have been placing the bomb; and of him, if in truth they were his, there were found only unrecognizable bits of flesh and shreds of clothing.

Public excitement revived and detectives kept it boiling. The newspapers were again ablaze with police reports of rapid progress in solving the diabolical mystery. Sleuths were again hot upon the trail. The “ablest” men in their line were working night and day upon the theory that the explosions of June were related to and were a part of the postal bomb-conspiracy of May. The clews to each were shrewdly expected to intertwine, so that the facts ascertained in running down the one plot would be of service in running down the other. Arrests were made quite freely, but no charges were preferred. A New York deputy police commissioner declared that “he would have the culprits in custody in forty-eight hours.” Agitations began for laws to deport all alien “radicals,” to curb American agitators, and to punish persons who were active in “stirring up discontent.”

A noted private detective had been promptly appointed by Attorney General Palmer as Chief of an Information Division in the Department of Justice. This Division became at once a news-distributing center and a secret-police plexus. The police of the whole country were sensitized, and the bomb-throwers were hourly on the verge of capture. A confusing hue and cry which surcharged the air, stirred the public mind with fear and hate.

Chapter VII. The January Raids—A Nation-Wide Glance

Hardly had the year Nineteen-twenty opened when the Department of Justice entered upon the “red” crusade for which its raiding of the preceding November had been a “try-out.” Numerously recruited for the occasion from “rough neck” groups of the “strike-breaking” variety and actively supported by local police authorities, the detective auxiliary of the Department of Justice spent the night of the second day in January at raiding lawful assemblages in more than thirty cities and towns of the United States—thirty-three being the number officially reported. Their object was wholesale arrests in furtherance of the plans already outlined for mass deportations of alien members of the Communist and the Communist Labor Parties. The approximate number of arrests officially reported was 2,500.

Those January raids, which resembled the raids of November, were spread over a vastly greater territory and involved the rights of a much larger number of American residents, both aliens and citizens. With more or less vigor—in some places more, in others less—they were carried on, simultaneously by the calendar and the clock, from Coast to Coast—in Maine, New Hampshire, Massachusetts, Connecticut, New York, New Jersey, Maryland, Pennsylvania, Ohio, Indiana, Missouri, Florida, Michigan, Illinois, Wisconsin, Iowa, Nebraska, Colorado, Minnesota, Kansas, California, Oregon and Washington State. Toward public law and private rights, these raids were if possible more defiantly reckless than those of November. Although the raidings in the farther West and in the farther South and North were hardly more than publicity gestures to give a nation-wide appearance to the crusade, those north of the Ohio River and east of the Mississippi, as far north as New Hampshire in the east and Wisconsin in the west, were serious displays.

In the strategic cities and towns of that vast and populous region, they involved lawless invasions of peaceable assemblies—public and private, political, recreational and educational. Meetings wide open to the general public were roughly broken up. All persons present—citizens and aliens alike without discrimination—were arbitrarily taken into custody and searched as if they had been burglars caught in the criminal act. Without warrants of arrest men were carried off to police stations or other temporary prisons, subjected there to secret police-office inquisitions commonly known as the “third degree,” their statements written categorically into mimeographed question blanks, and they required to swear to them regardless of their accuracy. The sole excuse for these outrages was the mere presence of the victims at those open and lawful meetings. That the meetings were lawful is evident from the fact that there were no prosecutions of citizens for attending them. And as with public meetings, so with private gatherings and social entertainments. Even homes did not escape night invasions and lawless searches, nor their law-abiding inmates unwarranted arrest. The crusade seems to have been conducted in accordance with the notion which the Attorney General afterwards adopted by way of apology for such lawlessness as he was obliged to confess, that when you suppose you are “trying to protect the community against moral rats you sometimes get to thinking more of your trap’s effectiveness than of its lawful construction.”

The day after the January raiding began, accounts of it from all quarters were of course broadcasted by the press over the country. Local news reports were gathered by local reporters for their respective local papers, but the material for the comprehensive news stories that went out from Washington, principally through the Associated Press, was supplied by the Department of Justice.

One of these widely published inspirations, justifying the lawless raids by reference to “the seriousness of the attempt to overthrow this Government,” told of a discovery by the raiders of four bombs in a Communist Party meeting place. Those bombs were the only ones discovered in all the January raids. Nor was there anything to indicate that even they were bombs except the fact that they were iron balls. Taken to the U. S. Attorney’s office those iron balls, deposited for safety in a pail of water, were exhibited suggestively to local reporters. Their seizure was reported, of course, to the Department of Justice at Washington whence exciting news of a bomb discovery was sent over the country. Those four iron balls must have melted in the pail of water, for they were never heard of again.

[. . .]

At no place in all that nation-wide raiding of January, 1920, were any weapons or explosive materials or destructive mechanisms discovered from which an inference of projected crime, private or political, could be reasonably drawn. Even as to criminal thoughts the proof was flimsy—absurdly so in contrast with the severity of the raiding.

Chapter XXXVII. Summary and Conclusion

The most important facts of a portentous fragment of American history are assembled in the foregoing personal narrative of a peculiar official experience. Following immediately upon the close of the World War, a popular delirium spread over the United States. It expressed itself in frenzied demands for deportations of alien residents. It rose to its peak in 1920. This delirium derived its distinctive characteristic from reports of mysterious crimes attributed to aliens. With nerves unstrung, the public easily saw anarchy spooks in every shadow. A postal explosives incident of the year before, closely followed by a hideous bombing of private residences, including the Washington home of the Attorney General, excited terrifying suspicions. Although the origin of those crimes has never been fixed, nor even indicated with reasonable probability, the crimes themselves were outstanding facts and stimulants of fear.

[. . .]

Doubtless, too, this delirium was accelerated by a war frenzy which survived the World War. War gets on the nerves of a people. It breeds popular hysteria as naturally as excessive whiskey-drinking breeds imaginary monsters. The proof of this which the aftermaths of all wars afford, has been overwhelmingly confirmed by the wild behavior of nations in Europe following the World War. Is it then to be wondered at that the people of the United States, though habitually inclined to boast of their poise, also fell victims to one of these after-war deliriums?

In stimulating the American delirium, a conspicuous agency was the Department of Justice, one of the Executive departments of the Federal Government—not, however, in its regular and legitimate organization, nor under the regime of Attorney General Gregory, but through a private-detective adjunct which Attorney General Palmer attached to it. Public money appropriated by Congress for “the detection and prosecution of crimes against the United States” was lavishly used by this detective adjunct of that Department as if the detection and prosecution of crime were legally synonymous with the detection and deportation of unwelcome aliens. The two functions are different in law from the ground up. But that irregularity was trivial in comparison with the anti-American spirit and the lawless procedure with which those detectives carried on the deportations crusade.
