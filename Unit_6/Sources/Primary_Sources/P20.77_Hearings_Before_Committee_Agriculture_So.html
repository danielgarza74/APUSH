Hearings before the Committee on Agriculture on the So-Called “Beveridge Amendment” to the Agricultural Appropriation Bill
U.S. House of Representatives, Committee on Agriculture · June 6–11, 1906
 
TOC FIT Progressive Regulation; Muckraking Journalism
WHY HERE In the House hearings on the 1906 meat-inspection bill, the Chicago packers’ spokesman says which parts of federal inspection the big firms welcomed and which they fought, and the chief federal meat inspector explains what his bureau could and could not do under the old law.
TREATMENT Public-domain text, selected excerpts
Historical Context

These excerpts come from the printed record of hearings held June 6–11, 1906, by the House of Representatives’ Committee on Agriculture, chaired by James W. Wadsworth of New York. The Government Printing Office published the testimony, along with related documents, in a volume whose pages carry the running head “Conditions in Chicago Stock Yards.” The first and longest witness was Thomas E. Wilson, an executive of Nelson Morris & Company, who told the committee he was speaking for “the Chicago packers.” The last excerpt is from Dr. A. D. Melvin, chief of the Department of Agriculture’s Bureau of Animal Industry, the agency that already inspected animals at many large packinghouses and would run the expanded system.

Upton Sinclair’s novel The Jungle (1906) had shocked readers with scenes of filth in Chicago’s packinghouses. President Theodore Roosevelt sent Charles P. Neill and James Bronson Reynolds to investigate, and their report, sent to Congress on June 4, confirmed much of the charge. The Senate had already passed Senator Albert J. Beveridge’s amendment, which required federal inspection of meat sold across state lines, charged the packers a fee to pay for it, and called for dates on canned meat. The House committee wrote its own substitute after these hearings. The final law, signed June 30, 1906, paid for inspection from the federal treasury and did not require dated cans. As you read, notice which parts of the bill Wilson accepts, which he resists, and the reasons he gives for each.

Committee on Agriculture, Wednesday, June 6, 1906. Statement of Mr. Thomas E. Wilson, of Chicago, Ill.

The Chairman. Please give your name to the stenographer.

Mr. Wilson. Thomas E. Wilson.

The Chairman. And also please state what interests you represent.

Mr. Wilson. I am directly connected with Nelson Morris & Co., but in this instance I am representing the Chicago packers.

Mr. Bowie. Are you an attorney?

Mr. Wilson. I am not; no, sir.

Monday, when the report of Messrs. Neill and Reynolds was published, we were, for the first time since this agitation commenced, put in a position where we could defend ourselves. Much comment had been made previous to the report, and, as you can readily see now by comparison, these comments were terribly exaggerated. Knowing this, we refrained from answering until we could learn what the report contained and would have something definite to reply to. That the live stock and packing industries have suffered an irreparable injury by the unjust and unwarranted criticisms of the past few weeks I am sure it is unnecessary for me to repeat to you gentlemen, who are in such close touch with the agricultural interest of the country. Had the original motive been simply the obtaining of legislation to improve the inspection and sanitary conditions, then I say the criticisms have been doubly unjust and unfair, for in the very inception of the investigation the commission and their superiors were given the assurance of the packers’ cooperation, and their personal guaranty was given that any practical recommendations would be welcomed and adopted.

We are now and have always been in favor of the extension of the inspection, also to the adoption of sanitary regulations that will insure the very best possible conditions, and in this connection I most respectfully call your attention to the recommendations of Messrs. Neill and Reynolds, Paragraph VII of their report, all of which except in some minor details has the hearty approval and support of the packers. What we are opposed to and what we appeal to you gentlemen for protection against is a bill that will put our business in the hands of theorists, chemists, sociologists, etc., and the management and control taken away from the men who have devoted their lives to the upbuilding and perfecting of this great American industry; that they have accomplished wonders in the development of the business and improvements, both sanitary and otherwise, surrounding all methods of handling meat-food products is an undisputed fact, the proofs of which (the packing plants) are to-day, and have been for the past fifteen years, wide open to the public.

My denials of Messrs. Neill and Reynolds’s report in detail would of course simply be my opinions against theirs, notwithstanding my twenty years’ experience in the business, and on this account and in justice to the great live-stock, agricultural, and packing interests of this country, I, in behalf of the packers, earnestly beg that you give one week of the time of your entire committee to make a personal inspection of Chicago and possibly other western packing houses. This time spent in this manner will give you a better understanding of the conditions and requirements than can possibly be gained in weeks spent in any other manner, and I feel confident that we can demonstrate to your perfect satisfaction that, contrary to the report of Messrs. Neill and Reynolds, the business and houses of these large packers are conducted in an extremely sanitary, healthful, and cleanly manner, and that the present inspection system conducted by the Department of Agriculture absolutely prohibits the possibility of any condemned meats entering either domestic or foreign trade, notwithstanding the fact that the report practically ignores this feature.

There is no question but that the many necessary operations attending the slaughtering of animals, the sight of blood and other offals, resulting would have a very shocking effect on the nerves and senses of two men with the necessary fine sensibilities that such men as Messrs. Neill and Reynolds must have who have devoted their lives to one line of scientific or sociological work, but men such as this committee is made up of, with broad and more general experiences, will immediately appreciate how the conditions reported have been exaggerated and that the greatest effort is made at all times to handle the product in a clean and sanitary manner. We appreciate that Messrs. Neill and Reynolds’s experiences especially equip them to recommend improvements in social conditions, and many of the suggestions made by them were found to be practical and have already been adopted, but we also feel that their two and one-half weeks’ experience in the packing houses does not equip them sufficiently to justify your honorable body in undertaking to intelligently legislate on their observation alone.

[. . .]

Mr. Adams. I do not find that you object to the conclusions of this report at all.

Mr. Wilson. Not at all, and we never have. It is to our advantage to have it inspected and to have good inspection. We have thought that we had good inspection, and I think it is good as far as it goes; and if they want to continue that further into the products, we do not oppose it.

Mr. Davis. Do you object to continuing that clear into the can?

Mr. Wilson. Not at all, not at all; we are glad to have it if it will improve the conditions.

[. . .]

Mr. Haugen. Then you have no objection to continuing the inspection?

Mr. Wilson. No, sir. The only suggestion they have made concerning that is an extension of it to the products.

The Chairman. That is, from the carcass to the can?

Mr. Wilson. From the hoof to the can.

The Chairman. From the carcass to the can.

Mr. Haugen. Why do you object to the extension?

Mr. Wilson. We do not object; we are glad to have it.

Mr. Davis. But you object to the dating of the label on the can?

Mr. Wilson. It does not accomplish any purpose. It is of no advantage to anybody and is a very great detriment to the industry.

[. . .]

Mr. Wilson. We object to the dating of the cans. I think you all understand our situation on that.

Mr. Brooks. Yes.

Mr. Wilson. We do not object to sanitary regulations and strict regulations, but we want to have it, if it is possible, so that that will be determined by sanitary experts, if necessary. If the Secretary of Agriculture believes, in his opinion, that the sanitary arrangements are not perfect, have it in such shape that he will call in undisputed experts in sanitation. Do not confine it to the rulings and regulations of the Department.

Mr. Brooks. Yes.

Mr. Wilson. But make it so that we would not be put in danger of violating any law here or in the city, or any insurance regulations or any State regulations.

[. . .]

Thursday, June 7, 1906. Statement of Mr. Thomas E. Wilson, of Chicago—Continued.

Mr. Haugen. As I understand it, the Beveridge amendment, the Beveridge bill, is entirely satisfactory to you except the payment of fees for inspection?

Mr. Wilson. Yes; and except as to the other objections that I stated yesterday. My statement this morning was with reference to the balance of the bill.

Mr. Lamb. I understand that perfectly. We would like to hear you on that one point.

Mr. Haskins. Since the adjournment yesterday afternoon, have you been advised by anyone not to specify objections here in detail as you did yesterday?

Mr. Wilson. No, sir. There are no serious objections remaining in the bill, excepting that particular one. There are minor objections, and I had handed to me by Mr. Scott, I believe, last night a copy of the amendment. That was the first copy I had, and I read that over.

[. . .]

The Chairman. Proceed, Mr. Wilson.

Mr. Wilson. The next paragraph is one we do object to, and we object to it very strenuously, and that is the fixing of the expense of this inspection upon the packers. The expense at present is borne by the Government. I do not believe there is another health measure on the statute books where it is different. This would be, to our notion, and as far as we are aware, unprecedented, and therefore unfair. We are standing to-day all the post-mortem condemnations at an expense of in the vicinity of a million dollars a year on these. We stand all that expense. The farmer, the stockraiser, the stock grower, nor the shipper do not stand any of that. The cattle come in and we buy them and pay for healthy animals. The hogs come in there and we buy them for a product that is fit for food, and it is taken into our houses, and if it is condemned, we lose it, and the aggregate of that loss is in the vicinity of a million dollars a year.

Mr. Brooks. For each plant?

Mr. Wilson. No, sir; covering the plants that I represent. The producer and the consumer get the benefit of that. The consumer is protected in getting wholesome meat, and the producer has the advantage of the inspection in creating foreign markets, and so forth, for his products, and he has no expense; and it hardly would seem reasonable to us that we should have to stand, in addition to the cost of the condemned animals, the expense of the inspection.

[. . .]

Mr. Haskins. It is a great benefit to the packers, is it not, to have their meats inspected and certified to be healthful?

Mr. Wilson. Yes, sir; it is a benefit to the packers, and so it is to the producers and consumers. It is not only a benefit to the packers, and we are paying for our benefit in condemnations to-day.

[. . .]

Mr. Brooks. When the matter of the special inspection was before the Appropriations Committee at that time we were given to understand that the packers were not only willing but anxious to pay the expense of the inspection.

Mr. Wilson. I really do not know who would give you that understanding, because the packers do not feel that way.

[. . .]

Mr. Scott. The general belief is, I think, that the packers whom you represent here sufficiently control the cattle market and the beef market so as to make it easy for them to recoup, if they desire to do so, any additional expense that might be put upon them by this inspection. Would you consider that there would be any doubt about your ability to do that?

Mr. Wilson. Why, yes; I am sure there is very great doubt about the ability to do that; I think it is not possible for the packers to do so. The packers do not kill more than 50 per cent of the cattle killed in this country—that is, the large packers concerned here—and they have to compete with the balance of the 50 per cent killed by the other folks, and they can not regulate prices. They have to take such prices as the market shipped to will justify, according to the supply and demand at that point.

Mr. Henry. You object to the payment of fees. Now, I can readily see why you object to paying ante-mortem inspection fees. But if your objection is a plausible one, how can you object to the payment of fees for sanitary inspection to remove your own delinquencies, and also for the inspection of your canned-food products and other products, sausages, etc., which, on account of your own sins of omission, have been blacklisted until you are out of the market for them and can not recover a market—can not recover your market—until your product is O. K.’d by the Government? How can you ask the Government reasonably to pull you out of the hole that you have digged for yourselves?

Mr. Wilson. Unfortunately it has not been of our doing that we have been put into this position. The present criticism and the present destruction of our trade is not of our doing, and it is unjust and unfair. The packers have gone to an enormous expense. They have sent men into every hamlet on the face of the earth to find a market for their products, and they have done it at an enormous expense, and they have put up their goods in a manner that they think should be satisfactory. If the Government comes along and insists upon different inspection, that we do not object to, but——

Mr. Henry. Beg pardon, it is not the Government, it is the people who purchase your goods that reject them.

Mr. Wilson. I think up to this time the chief objections have emanated from the criticisms on the part of folks attached to the Government. The effective objections, and the ones that have done harm in the foreign countries, are those. We have not got American or foreign markets in the palm of our hands. We have competition with other countries. Australia and South America to-day are keen competitors, not only on manufactured products, but on fresh meats. They are to-day shipping into England refrigerated meats that are coming into keen and close competition with meats from this country. They are improving their cattle; they are improving their facilities; they have become more and more Americanized in the methods of handling their stuff. Up until a few years ago they landed all their stuff in England frozen. Now they have taken up our methods, and they land it refrigerated and not frozen. We have all this to contend with, and to-day, chiefly on account of this agitation, the result from our English business is a destructive loss.

We can stand burdens, but it is possible to overburden. This fee is part of it. We are standing our share of the burdens of this inspection by standing the loss of condemnation. Now, the proposition to burden us with further losses is unfair. It is something that has never been done with any health measure that has ever been put into operation.

[. . .]

Statement of Dr. A. D. Melvin, Chief Bureau of Animal Industry, Department of Agriculture.

While regulations issued by the Department shortly after I became Chief of the Bureau provided for certain sanitary regulations, I was advised by the solicitor of the Department at the time I was considering the issuance of such a regulation that, while the matter was very necessary, the Department had no legal authority to enforce sanitary regulations.

On April 18, 1906, inspectors in charge of meat inspection were notified that the regulations of the Department were to be changed to require each package of meat prepared in an abattoir having Government inspection to bear a label stating in effect that the meat product in the package was from animals which, at the time of slaughter, were inspected and passed for food by United States Government inspectors, according to the acts of Congress. Shortly afterward a formal order was issued to this effect.

On May 22, 1906, I addressed a letter to the inspectors in charge at the principal stations and instructed them to inform me whether amendment 5, regarding sanitation, was being complied with. I instructed the inspectors to notify the proprietors of the establishments that lavatories must be installed, so that workmen could have no excuse for not keeping their hands clean, and that proprietors must see that employees did keep themselves clean. It was ordered that meat must be raised from the floors, and that the practice of mixing meats upon the floors must be abandoned and suitable boxes or benches provided. Other requirements such as wire netting and other covers for racks, vats, and boxes used for meats and meat products must be provided.

Of course, there being no legal authority for this work, I had to depend more or less upon moral suasion, which is not a very satisfactory way of securing compliance with regulations. The only remedy which has heretofore been in the hands of the Department has been to withdraw the inspection, and this has had little or no weight with establishments, except so far as it referred to that part of their product which was exported; and when it is considered that if inspection is withdrawn from any house, it is still free to put its products in interstate commerce, and no means is provided for detecting diseased carcasses, it will be seen why the Department was very loath to withdraw inspection from any establishment.
