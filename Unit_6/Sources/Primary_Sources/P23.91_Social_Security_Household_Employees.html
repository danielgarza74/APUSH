Social Security for Household Employees
Mary Anderson, Director, Women’s Bureau, U.S. Department of Labor · May 13, 1939
 
TOC FIT Social Security: Limitations and Critics; Working Women; African Americans
WHY HERE The head of the federal Women’s Bureau explains on the radio why the household workers left out of the 1935 Social Security Act, most of them women and nearly half of them Black, should be brought into old-age and unemployment insurance.
TREATMENT Public-domain text, complete
Historical Context

Mary Anderson, an immigrant from Sweden who had worked in a shoe factory and organized for the Women’s Trade Union League, became the first director of the U.S. Women’s Bureau when Congress made it a permanent part of the Department of Labor in 1920. She held the post until 1944, making her one of the longest-serving women in the federal government of her era. On May 13, 1939, she gave this short talk over WJSV, a Washington, D.C., radio station. The Women’s Bureau issued the text as a four-page typed release, and this copy survives in the Boston Public Library.

The Social Security Act of 1935 left out several large groups of workers, among them farm laborers and household employees such as maids, cooks, and laundresses. In 1939 Congress was considering amendments to the act, and an advisory council and the Social Security Board had recommended covering domestic workers. Anderson speaks to listeners who might employ such workers, so notice how she makes her case: she appeals to taxpayers and housewives as well as to fairness. Notice also the census figures she gives on who household workers were, and her explanation of why so many Black women stayed in the occupation. Congress did not extend old-age insurance to most household workers until 1950.

Document Text

In the Social Security Act, passed in August 1935, household employees were exempted from the Federal old-age and unemployment insurance features. This was one of the serious gaps left in the social security program as first established, because it was impossible to achieve in the launching of the program all desirable objectives under the already exceedingly complicated piece of legislation.

Now, after a few years of experiment and experience with the social security system, certain changes are being advocated. Among desirable amendments to the Act that authorities are urging Congress to pass is extension of old-age and unemployment benefits to domestic workers, that is, household employees. This step was recommended by the advisory council which was appointed by the Senate special committee on social security and the Social Security Board to report on any desirable revisions in this Act.

Thus the Social Security Board in its report to President Roosevelt included in its recommendations extension of old-age and unemployment benefits to domestic workers in private homes, provided that sufficient time be allowed for adjustment of certain administrative difficulties. This proposal was endorsed by the President in a report to the Congress.

I am heartily in sympathy with this plan. During my 20 years as Director of the Women’s Bureau I have given considerable attention to the problems of household employees. The need and value of providing them with security measures more or less similar to those given other types of workers is obvious to all who look into the question. Old-age insurance monthly payments for domestic workers after they reach 65 years of age and unemployment compensation for them would prove beneficial to the general taxpayers as well as to both the workers and employers.

With such an insurance system in operation for household employees taxpayers would be relieved of the burden of caring for many domestics who, because they lack jobs and other means of support, become the community’s responsibility. These include former household workers over 65 who are receiving the old-age pensions provided out of Federal and State funds for needy aged persons. Domestic workers on relief also include those who are jobless and in need, irrespective of age. Such persons may be on W.P.A. work projects—sponsored by the Federal Government, or they may be on a straight relief basis—cared for out of State funds. Both of these funds must be provided by the taxpayers, who should welcome a system which will help domestic workers build up a security for themselves with some assistance from their employers.

What would such a system mean to those most concerned? It would bring to both the workers and employers returns of real value, and at a very small cost. The system calls for the payment in the beginning of only a few pennies a week by the average household employee and a somewhat larger, though still very nominal amount, by the employer. Even when the maximum rate of contribution would be in effect some years hence, the average worker and employer would still pay only a small amount each week.

Let us analyze the status of domestic workers and see what they might expect in the way of betterment of conditions in the future if they were covered by these two types of insurance.

When compared with many types of workers domestic employees are today at a serious disadvantage. They are not protected by labor legislation. They are not covered by the Federal wage-hour law or the National Labor Relations Act. They are not protected by State maximum-hour laws except in two cases, and they are not safeguarded by minimum-wage orders except in two. Nor are household employees included under compulsory workmen’s compensation laws in the various States providing benefits to victims of industrial accidents or occupational diseases.

Why is there such universal exception of household employees from these legal safeguards? It is because of administrative problems, despite the great need of these workers for security measures.

Also as an occupational field for women, household employment is the most unstandardized. There are no definite yardsticks as to hours, wages, and working conditions. There are no standard requirements as to training or job performances.

Consequently, domestic workers as a class are low paid, many of them receiving less than $10 a week, and large numbers are paid $5 or less. Most of them have long working hours, with little opportunity to devote to their own interests.

They are a very unstable group as to employment. The Nation-wide unemployment census of 1937 revealed as totally unemployed or on emergency work, 12 percent of the total number of domestic gainful workers reported in the United States in 1930 by the census. An additional 5 percent had only a part-time job and were seeking more work.

A recent study made by the Women’s Bureau of 600 unattached women on relief in Chicago showed the needs of domestic workers to be a challenging problem. The women included in the analysis were picked more or less at random from the relief rolls, though only women under 65 years of age were chosen. Nearly two-fifths of the women reported household employment as their usual occupation. Of these domestics on relief a large number were unemployed because of illness or inability to find a job, others because their age made such heavy work impossible.

Right here we run into a paradoxical situation in regard to domestic workers. Despite the fact that thousands of such workers are unemployed, there still remains a great unmet demand for skilled domestics. A survey by the United States Employment Service shows that in January 1937 at least 500 cities faced a shortage of trained household workers and that possible placements in these cities might reach a total of well over 400,000. Nevertheless, in July of the same year some 400,000 applicants describing themselves as household workers were registered for jobs in the active files of the public employment centers. Obviously large numbers of applicants for domestic service are not qualified for available jobs. Such a situation calls for a carefully worked-out training program.

Because of all these conditions, for which the household workers are not responsible, they are among the least secure in our economic system. They are unable to provide against a rainy day—against illness, unemployment, old age.

The extension of the old-age and unemployment insurance program to domestic workers might help to standardize and stabilize this field in various ways. It would help, I believe, to develop a clear-cut classification of these workers, to include those who belong rightfully in the field. It would exclude those who enter this type of work because they are not qualified for any other even though not trained for domestic service either. In many quarters we encounter the mistaken belief that any woman can make good as a household worker. If domestic service were brought under the social security program, this would, I believe, stimulate better training facilities for household employees. In general, more desirable types of workers would be attracted to this field and be willing to remain in it if given a legal status and a more secure basis along the lines granted other types of labor.

Domestic workers constitute a large group numbering almost two and a quarter million, according to the 1930 census report. Well over 90 percent are women. A larger proportion of women are employed in private homes than in any other one occupational field. The next largest groups of women are the clerical workers who total not quite 2 million and the factory workers numbering about 1½ million.

Not far from one-half of the household employees are Negroes. Thus the inclusion of household employment in the social security program would be an important factor in giving more security to the Negroes and in taking many of them off relief.

Household employment is a field in which are found about one-half of the Negro women in gainful work. It is an occupation for Negro women of all ages, whereas white domestics are concentrated more largely than are Negro women in the very young or relatively old age groups. Negro women are apt to remain in this field for all their working lives because race prejudice bars them, to a great extent, from many other kinds of jobs. White women who start as domestics are likely to desert this work as soon as opportunity is found for some other occupation in which they will have social security coverage and generally better labor standards.

Altogether, many domestic workers feel that inclusion under the old-age and unemployment insurance would be a great gain for them. They would like to be granted the same opportunity as other workers to help build up their own reserves for old age, and then at 65 automatically to receive monthly payments. Such insurance would prevent household employees from falling into the pauper class and of having to prove their poverty in order to receive relief under the public assistance program. The insurance system would guarantee many household employees a higher monthly payment than that received by an old person on relief. Naturally household employees also are eager to have the protection of unemployment benefits more or less similar to those of other workers when out of a job.

We all know that household service is a very important type of work—one on which the health and welfare of the family depend to a great extent. We all know that many housewives are always seeking better workers to meet their needs. If the social security measures would help to guarantee more satisfactory employees, I believe the housewives would be more than willing to pay the small contribution required by the social security insurance measures.

If household employees were covered by such insurance, the problem of securing compliance might not prove so serious as is feared. Authorities claim that though the administrative problems are difficult, they are not insurmountable.

Though domestic workers receive part of their pay in the form of meals, or room and board, this does not present too difficult a problem. Already the social security program has coped with similar problems in other occupations. Nor does the question of high labor turnover in this field close the door necessarily to its inclusion under the social security program. The fact that the number of employers so nearly equals the number of workers makes administration difficult, but not impossible. Employment in private homes complicates the procedure in law enforcement, but experience in foreign countries proves the feasibility of protecting household workers by social legislation of this sort.

Eighteen countries, including the principal ones in Europe, provide for domestic workers under compulsory old-age insurance. No country has found it necessary to drop such workers from the system after they have been brought into it. Nor has a separate system been necessary for domestic workers in any country, though the method of assessing contributions has been slightly different from the procedures used for other workers.

Available information has revealed no notable difficulty in securing reasonable compliance from employers of domestic workers in other countries. In countries where Government inspectors are unable to enter private homes to enforce the law, the interests of the workers in their own security and the penalties imposed upon employers who fail to comply have served to effect adequate enforcement.

In this country, can we hope to enact the necessary legislation? Already we can point to a slight entering wedge in regard to including household workers under unemployment compensation. Though the Social Security Act exempts employers of household workers from payment of the unemployment insurance tax it does not prevent the States in their legislation on the subject from covering these workers. Two States have already taken such action. The New York law applies to households with four or more domestics, and is thus relatively limited in scope. Idaho has recently amended its law to include domestics even if only one is employed in a home. A similar proposal is found in a bill sponsored by Congressman Randolph for revising the District of Columbia unemployment compensation law. Other States can follow suit.

Then as soon as the time seems ripe for the necessary Federal action of extending the old-age and unemployment insurance provisions to household employees I believe we can attain the objective. We realize the need, we have the progressive spirit to go ahead, and we have the ingenuity to solve the problems.
