Memorandum on the British Order in Council of March 11, 1915
Robert Lansing · March 20, 1915
 
TOC FIT Initial American Reactions: Neutral Rights and Submarine Attacks
WHY HERE The State Department’s legal adviser explains to his superiors how Britain shaped its 1915 order cutting off trade with Germany so that it would not have to follow the accepted rules of blockade.
TREATMENT Public-domain text, complete
Historical Context

Robert Lansing was Counselor of the State Department in March 1915, the department’s chief legal officer, serving under Secretary of State William Jennings Bryan. He wrote this short memorandum as advice inside the government, not for publication. Two days later Bryan sent it to President Woodrow Wilson with the covering note printed first here, explaining that Lansing had written it before seeing Wilson’s own notes for a reply to Britain. The memorandum and Bryan’s note were printed in 1939 in a State Department volume of Lansing’s papers in the series Papers Relating to the Foreign Relations of the United States.

In February 1915 Germany declared the waters around the British Isles a war zone for its submarines. Britain answered with an Order in Council, dated March 11 and made public March 15, that allowed its navy to stop goods going to or from Germany, even when ships were bound for neutral ports such as those of the Netherlands or Scandinavia. Lansing compares the order with the Declaration of London of 1909, a code of naval rules that Britain had said its courts would apply. Notice the advice he gives at the end: the United States should not accept the British claim that this was a lawful blockade unless Britain obeyed the rules of blockade.

Editorial note. The bracketed “[difficult?]” is in the printed edition, where the editors suggest a correction to Lansing’s word. The edition’s two footnotes (references to other volumes of Foreign Relations) are omitted; one of them explains that the order dated March 11, 1915, was often called the Order in Council of March 15, the day it was made public.
Document Text
The Secretary of State to President Wilson

Washington, March 22, 1915.

My Dear Mr. President: I am sending you a memorandum on the Order in Council which Mr. Lansing prepared before he received your memorandum covering the details of the reply. Mr. Lansing has carefully examined the memorandum which you sent over and is preparing a suggestion which I will send you this evening, but I thought you might be interested in reading the enclosed.

With assurances [etc.]

W. J. Bryan

[Enclosure] Memorandum by the Counselor for the Department of State (Lansing) on the British Order in Council of March 11, 1915

[Washington,] March 20, 1915.

Query. Why did not the British Government proclaim a blockade of German ports and notify neutral governments of that action?

I think the answer to this query is to be found in the fact that the British Government by their Order in Council of October 29, 1914, put in operation during the present war the Declaration of London with certain modifications and directed the British Courts to apply its provisions thus modified.

The modifications made by the Order in Council in no way affected the rules laid down in the Declaration covering the subject of blockade.

Articles 1, 18 and 19 of the Declaration read as follows:

Article 1

A blockade must not extend beyond the ports and coasts belonging to or occupied by the enemy.

Article 18

The blockading forces must not bar access to neutral ports or coasts.

Article 19

Whatever may be the ulterior destination of a vessel or of her cargo, she cannot be captured for breach of blockade, if, at the moment, she is on her way to a non-blockaded port.

Manifestly these provisions of the Declaration are directly at variance with the procedure authorized by the Order in Council of March 15, 1915.

To have announced a blockade as extensive in effect as the operations contemplated in the Order in Council of March 15th would have compelled the further modification of the Declaration of London by striking out the three articles quoted. So radical a change in the accepted theory of a blockade would undoubtedly have aroused general criticism by neutral governments and would have been embarrassing for the British Government to defend.

To avoid placing themselves in so different [difficult?] a position diplomatically the British Government issued the Order in Council of March 15th carefully avoiding the terms “blockade” in the Order, but in the note of transmittal they not only use the word “blockade” but seek apparently to have this Government adopt the idea that it is a blockade and to discuss it from that standpoint.

It seems to me that the course pursued by the British Government is very adroit. This Government, however, should not be led into the trap of admitting that a blockade has been established by the Order in Council, and should insist that, if it is to be considered a blockade with its attendant belligerent rights, the British Government should conform strictly to the rules of blockade promulgated in their Order in Council of October 29, 1914, namely, the rules laid down in the first twenty-one articles of the Declaration of London.

Robert Lansing
