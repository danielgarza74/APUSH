Phoenix Tribune · August 3, 1918
 
TOC FIT Managing the Home Front: Race, Ethnicity, and the War
WHY HERE The front page of a Black weekly in Arizona reports that a War Department conference of Black editors helped win President Wilson’s statement against lynching, and in another column counts the year’s lynchings and asks what they say about a war for democracy.
TREATMENT Public-domain text, selected excerpts
Historical Context

The Phoenix Tribune was a weekly newspaper published for Black readers in Phoenix, Arizona, which called itself “Arizona’s Greatest Weekly.” This issue, number 20, appeared on Saturday, August 3, 1918. Its front page mixed war news, local items, and opinion. The lead story told of the city’s farewell to Black draftees; other columns reported on Black soldiers in France and on new War Department orders. The three items here are the opening of a Washington news report on the editors’ conference, an unsigned column headed “Yet the Negro Is Loyal,” and a short item from Miami, Arizona. The scan comes from the Library of Congress’s Chronicling America collection.

In June 1918 Emmett J. Scott, a former secretary to Booker T. Washington who served as special assistant to the secretary of war for Black affairs, brought Black newspaper editors and leaders to Washington. The government worried about Black morale and the Black press while lynching, segregation, and discrimination continued. On July 26, 1918, Wilson publicly condemned mob violence and lynching. The figures in “Yet the Negro Is Loyal” come from Tuskegee Institute, which kept a national count of lynchings. Notice who called the conference and why, how the paper describes Wilson’s statement, and how the column sets the lynching figures beside the nation’s claim to be fighting for democracy and beside Black Americans’ loyalty.

Editorial note. The news report on the editors’ conference continues on page 6 of the issue, which is not in the saved file; the excerpt ends at the last complete sentence on the front page. Spellings are reproduced as printed, including “positiv,” “isx months,” and “dealth with.” The word “Negro” is the newspaper’s own usage.

Editors’ Conference Yields Big Results

WASHINGTON, D. C., July 29.—Already there are numerous evidences that the recent conference of colored editors and leaders held in this city in June, which was called by Emmett J. Scott, special assistant to the Secretary of War, at the suggestion of the War Department and the Committee on Public Information, to consider the relations of the 12,000,000 Negroes in this country with the general government itself, and to discuss ways and means how each might co-operate most effectively in the work of winning the present world-wide war, is bearing fruit. Three results of the most significant character have been worked out within the past fortnight that fully justify the holding of the conference and other issues not less far-reaching are well on their way toward a satisfactory conclusion.

President Wilson’s Clean-Cut Pronouncement Against Lynching

The first in importance and human interest of the helpful results that may be said to have grown directly out of the recent conference of editors, and the co-operation of Negro leaders and friends of the Negro people, is the frank, positiv and unequivocal declaration of the President of the United States against the mob spirit, which has been sent to the four corners of the earth by cable and through the newspapers of this country.

[. . .]

Yet the Negro Is Loyal

According to reports handed out by the Division of Records and Research of Tuskegee Institute thirty-five lynchings have occurred during the first six months of this year. This, the report says, is twenty-one more than were lynched during the first six months of 1917, and ten more than for the same period in 1916. Of the thirty-five lynchings during the first isx months of 1918 all but one were Negroes, and eight were charged with the crime of rape. Georgia and Louisiana contend for first place with eight lynchings each to their credit; then follows Tennessee with four, Mississippi 2, and Alabama, Arkansas, Florida, Illinois, North Carolina and South Carolina, one each.

These figures tell their own story and register testimony that does not add power to this nation’s boast of civilization and love for democracy. This kind of treatment of the nation towards its own citizens at home is compelled to embarrass its protested love and interest in the rights and liberties of oppressed peoples across the seas. Centralization of power in the various departments of our National Government has added immensely to the efficiency with which we are waging war for democracy. That this centralization and peremptory use of power to meet emergencies and to cope with the world situation cannot be used to suppress lawlessness and the brutal taking of human lives right here at home, seems anomalous. And yet the race that furnishes the victims for such barbarous crimes against law and justice is exhibiting a type of loyalty and patriotism that is not equalled by any class of the nation’s citizenship, and which shall be the wonder of coming generations.

Negro Soldiers Must Be Served Say Officers

Miami, Ariz.—Army officers have passed the word to keepers of restaurants and refreshment places in this vicinity that if they refuse to serve uniformed men they will be severely dealth with. The case of a refreshment parlor proprietor who refused to serve a Negro soldier caused the action by military authorities.
