Selection Overview

Hamilton accepts that the 1930 panic mattered but argues it grew out of weaknesses in an overextended, undercapitalized unit-banking system, the Caldwell failure and falling farm prices and drought, rather than autonomous contagion or Federal Reserve inaction alone.

The Causes of the Banking Panic of 1930: Another View By DAVID E. HAMILTON IN NOVEMBER AND DECEMBER I930 THE SOUTH AND THE MIDWEST were rocked by the closing of more than six hundred banks. The wave of failures was the first of the Depression era's three great banking panics, and in recent years the debate over the causes of the panic of 1930 has emerged as an important issue in the general debate over the causes of the Great Depression The controversy centers on the interpretation of the panic put forth by Milton Friedman and Anna Jacobson Schwartz in A Monetary History of the United States, 1867-1960. Prior to the appearance of their study, most historical accounts of the economic contraction of 1929-1932 had portrayed the rash of failures as but one of several reasons for the rapid deterioration of conditions during the second half of 1930.1 Friedman and Schwartz, however, attached far greater significance to the panic. In their view it caused a sharp decline in the money supply, and, in the absence of an expansionary monetary pol- icy by the Federal Reserve Board, this dramatic change in the "mone- tary character of the contraction" turned a serious recession into a disastrous depression. The "crop of bank failures," they argued, sparked a "contagion of fear . . . among depositors" and "led to wide- spread attempts to convert demand and time deposits into currency ...." It was a "contagion" that started in the "agricultural areas, which had experienced the heaviest impact of bank failures in the twenties." Surprisingly, since they placed so great an emphasis on the panic, Friedman and Schwartz's book is confusing and vague about its causes. Although earlier studies had attributed the increase in fail- ures to falling agricultural incomes, these authors did not consider this possibility. They conjectured instead that the "failures that char- acterized the first banking crisis after October 1930 may possibly have resulted from poor loans and investments made in the twenties," particularly late in the twenties when prosperity may have led bank- ers to be overly optimistic. Yet while making this conjecture, they conceded that the evidence for it pertains only to foreign and not domestic assets, and they went on to argue that, because of increasing pressure on bank reserves in 1928 and 1929, bankers were probably taking greater care in making loans.4 Friedman and Schwartz also seem to have contradicted themselves when discussing the relationship between bank failures of the twen- ties and those of 1930. If, as they asserted, the "contagion of fear" started in the agricultural regions that experienced the most bank Friedman and Schwartz believed that much of the importance of the banking panic stemmed from the failure of the Bank of the United States. They argued that, because of its size and because it was widely believed that the bank was a government institution, its failure consti- tuted a disastrous blow to depositor confidence, one which intensified demands by depositors for currency and attempts by bankers to improve on their liquidity. Elmus Wicker has recently argued that the failure of the Bank of the United States was an event coincidental to the banking panic but unrelated to it. I take the same position in this paper, and therefore the paper does not examine the causes of the bank's suspension. Friedman and Schwartz implied that its failure was related to the "contagion of fear" that arose in agricultural states, but they pro- vided no evidence to support such a conclusion. Furthermore, the bank's closing did not cause the failure of any other bank in New York City or in surrounding cities and states. Nor, as Wicker argued, is it possible to discern any relationship between its failure and the failures in the South and the Midwest after December 11. In his study, Peter Temin said that Friedman and Schwartz offered two possible interpretations of the causes of the panic. The first - bad loans and investments - was explicitly stated; the second - a decline in agricultural prices-was implied, Temin believed, in their description of the panic. However, since no explicit reference was made to the role of commodity prices in their account of the panic, I prefer to argue that Friedman and Schwartz saw only bad loans and investments as its cause.
