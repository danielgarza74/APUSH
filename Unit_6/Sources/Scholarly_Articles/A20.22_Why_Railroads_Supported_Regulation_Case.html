Selection Overview

Caine shows that Wisconsin's railroads fought reform bitterly, then embraced the commission because its conservative administration shielded them from politics. He presents this as an escape from reform by unexpected means, not as business using reform for its own ends, which tempers Kolko's argument.

"Progressivism in operational reality," asserts Kolko, "becomes a political capitalism whereby important economic interests utilize the power of the federal government to solve in- ternal economic problems which could not otherwise be solved by voluntary or non-political means." Kolko's case is based upon the overriding assumption that the "business philosophy" ruled the age, molding the thinking of poli- tician and businessman alike:4 Business held the ultimate reins of accepted ideology and defined the outer limits of potential reforms; all major parties paid tribute to the basic institutional rights and interests of business, and to the mythology of social values which allowed it to survive all onslaughts. Caught up in the pro-business climate of the times, the "well-in- tentioned reformers" often worked with business interests and sel- dom sought to thwart their aims.5 A "triumph of conservatism" was thus achieved in the very era when talk of anti-business reforms filled the air. In a specific extension of his theory to the area of railroad regula- tion, Kolko, in Railroads and Regulation, examines the manner in which federal regulatory statutes were enacted from 1877 to 1916 and concludes that the "railroads... were the most important single advocates of federal regulation" during this period." He sees the rationalization of the industry and the reduction of "cut- throat competition" as major elements in encouraging this drive for new regulation. In the case of railroads Kolko also names as a contributing cause the desire to escape state regulation, which was "beginning to grow more cumbersome, and was less controllable and more unpredictable than federal regulation."' While Kolko's analysis raises numerous questions, three are es- pecially relevant to a study of state railroad regulation. To what degree did the sort of consensus between businessmen and re- formers upon which Kolko bases his theory exist in the various phases of the progressive movement? Did the railroads favor regulation at other levels of government, and, if so, was this sup- port based on their control of the legislative process?
