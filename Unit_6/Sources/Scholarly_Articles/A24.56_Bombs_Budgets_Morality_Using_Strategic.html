A-Bombs, Budgets, and Morality: Using the Strategic Bombing Survey
Gian P. Gentile
 
ARTICLE A-Bombs, Budgets, and Morality: Using the Strategic Bombing Survey
JOURNAL Air Power History 44, no. 1 (1997)
EXCERPT Original journal pages 20-21
JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/26287785
Selection Overview

Gentile argues that the Strategic Bombing Survey's reports were a loose, sometimes contradictory amalgam, so Navy and Air Force advocates could use them in postwar budget fights; he contends its own narrative undercuts its claim that Japan would have surrendered without the bomb.

(Overleaf) From close up, the city of Hiroshima. THE SURVEY WAS MUCH LIKE THE BIBLE IN ONE RESPECT. IF YOU REACH DEEPLY ENOUGH YOU CAN FIND SUBSTANTIATION FOR ALMOST ANY PRECONCEIVED NOTION OR PREJUDICE THE SURVEY'S REPORTS WERE NOT A SINGLE SET OF UNIFIED ANALYSES AND ARGUMENTS, BUT, RATHER, A LOOSE AMALGAM OF STUDIES Some historians have argued that the authorsof The United States Strategic Bombing Survey (USSBS) reached a consensus in the publication of the chairman's reports and that the numerous other reports and studies should be viewed only as supporting documents.1 That genial approach, however, brushes aside the questions of whether and how the chairman's reports followed logically and evidentially from the published division studies. The Survey's competing, ambiguous, and even contradictory conclusions made it a very malleable source document for Navy and Air Force leaders during the intense and often bitter disputes over budgets, roles and missions, and conceptions of strategic air power during postwar unification hearings. General Haywood Hansell once said that "the Survey was much like the Bible in one respect. If you reach deeply enough you can find substantiation for almost any preconceived notion or prejudice. "2The Survey, much like the Bible, is rooted in the past, but has strongly influenced the present and the future. Secretary of War Henry L. Stimson officially established the USSBS in November 1944 to analyzethe effects of strategic air power in the European theater. Later, President Harry S. Truman expanded the Survey's scope to study all types of aerial war against Japan, including the effects of the atomic bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. In an attempt to keep the Survey's findings impartial, prominent civilians, instead of military officers, were appointed as directors of the Survey's divisions. The Survey was made up of over 1,000 civilian and military specialists and support personnel. Specialists were placed, according to their expertise, into analytical divisions, including Military Analysis, Morale, Physical Damage, Transportation, and Economic Effects. Each division sent out small field teams to collect data on strategic bombing attacks against targets in France and Germany, and, later, Japan. The Survey also conducted extensive interrogations of key Japanese and German war leaders. The divisions used the data collected by field teams to write reports reflecting each division's overall analysis. The chairman's office then, theoretically, based its Summary Reports on the analysis provided by the individual division reports. The Survey's key directors were Franklin D'Olier (chairman), Henry Alexander (vice chairman), George Ball, Paul Nitze, Theodore Wright, Fred Searls, and John Kenneth Galbraith.3 With such a wide range of personalities and professional expertise, Survey members undoubtedly came to disagree over the evaluation of data. The official historian for the Survey, Maj. James Beveridge, pointed out that many of the Survey divisions were collecting evidence of a nature that was "considerably controversial."4 Survey authors also brought to their work assumptions affecting their analyses. In July 1945, weeks before Hiroshima and about three months before he departed for the Pacific to conduct that portion of the Survey, Paul Nitze, a proponent of strategic bombing, concluded that "even without the atomic bomb, Japan was likely to surrender in a matter of months—Japan would capitulate by November 1945." Nitze's pre-Hiroshima predictions were manifested in the well-known Pacific Survey counterfactual (of which Nitze was the primary author)5 that argued Japan would have capitulated without the use of the atomic bombs or Soviet declaration of war. The final reports were completed and released for publication between September 1945 and June 1946. As political and military leaders argued over how to organize the defense establishment for the future during congressional hearings on armed forces unification, Survey authors debated over how to interpret the recent past. George Ball, a director of the European Survey, acknowledged in his memoirs that the evidence collected concerning strategic bombing caused "wide differences of view as to the interpretationof the data."6 The Survey's reports were not a single set of unified analyses and arguments, systematically grounded in data, but, rather, a loose amalgam of studies, sometimes using different standards, and sometimes prepared more to shape the future than to interpret the past. Thus, the Survey's reports and conclusions should not be treated as unimpeachable authority but as interpretations, subject to close reading and thoughtful challenge. One way to illustrate the diverse nature of the Survey's reports is to analyze some of its stated conclusions and then show how the evidence used by Survey authors to support their conclusions can lead to alternative findings.7 A well-known counterfactual conclusion from the Survey is that "Japan would have surrendered even if the atomic bombs had not been dropped, even if Russia had not entered the war, and even if no invasion had been planned or contemplated." The Agricultural Exposition Hall and surroundings In Hiroshima were directly under the atomic bomb blast and totally devastated. A COMMON THEME THAT RUNS THROUGHOUT IS THE DECISIVENESS OF AIR POWER IN THE WAR AGAINST GERMANY BOTH THE AIR AND GROUND EFFORTS WERE INEXTRICABLY MIXED IN THE ASSAULT ON THE GERMAN WAR ECONOMY would have occurred "certainly prior to 31 December 1945, and in all probability prior to 1 November 1945."8 This conclusion appears in a report from the Pacific Survey, issued by the chairman's office, entitled Japan's Struggle to End the War. The report provides a detailed narrative of the events leading up to Japan's capitulation on August 14, 1945. According to the report, by July 1945 American aerial bombardment had created enough urgency for three membersof the Japanese inner cabinet to pursue peace aggressively at virtually any cost, though conventional bombing had not convinced the cabinet's military faction to accept the Potsdam terms for surrender, even up to the time when the United States dropped the first atomic bomb on Hiroshima on August 6, 1945. The report argued that the atomic bomb and the subsequent Soviet declaration of war on August 8, 1945, only "provided further lubrication" for the Japanese to accept unconditional surrender;9 the decisive factor was the conventional bombing of the Japanese home islands. This analysis led Survey authors to their counterfactual (what-if) speculation that even without the bomb and Soviet war declaration Japan would "in all probability" have surrendered by November 1, 1945 and definitely prior to December 31, 1945.10 If, however, one disregards the Survey's speculativeconcluding remarks in the report, the Survey's narrative, by itself, suggests another interpretation: that the atom bomb might have been more critical in forcing Japan to surrender in mid-August 1945. Former Secretary of War Henry L. Stimson, in an influential 1947 article for Harpers Magazine entitled 'The Decision to Use the Bomb," explicitly cites the Survey's report, Japan's Struggle to End the War, as proof that the atom bomb was decisive in ending the war with Japan.11 Stimson's statement is an example of how conclusions drawn from a Survey report could be much different from those stated by its authors. A common theme that runs throughout the European Summary Report and Overall Report is the decisiveness of air power in the war against Germany. While these two reports acknowledge that "air power might have been employed better in some respects," they both contend that air power was decisive.12 In partial dissent was another report from the European Survey, also published under the auspices of the chairman's office, entitled The Effects of Strategic Bombing on the German War Economy and authored primarily by economist John Kenneth Galbraith. His Economic Division's analysis offers conclusionsabout the effects of strategic bombing that were different in degree from the Summary Report and Overall Report. The Economic Division's report focuses on the inability of the Army Air Forces (AAF), especially prior to the fall of 1944, to substantially affect Germany's war production. Not until late in the war, according to Galbraith's division, when the AAF began to concentrate heavily on oil and transportation, did Germany begin to feel the full weight of the air attack. By this time, however, as the report points out, the ground offensive had also become a powerful factor in crippling Germany's fighting strength; both the air and ground efforts were inextricably mixed in the assault on the German war economy.13 Pacific Survey reports contain even more diversity and competing conclusions than those from its European counterpart. The chairman's Summary Report, authored primarily by Paul Nitze,14 acknowledged the importance of the war against Japanese shipping in destroying Japan's war economy, but concluded that the decisive factorin persuading the Japanese leaders to surrender was the conventional strategic bombing of the Japanese home islands.15 Two other Pacific Survey reports, entitled The Effects of Strategic Bombing on Japan's War Economy and The War Against Japanese Transportation, subtly differed from the Summary Report's argument. In the view of these two division studies, the anti-shipping campaign conducted primarily by the Navy had virtually destroyed Japan's economy prior to the main weight of the AAF's bombing campaign, and therefore these two publications, unlike the Summary Report, stress the decisive role of anti\x=req-\ shipping in Japan's capitulation.16 The connection between breaking Japan's will to resist, measured to a large extent by the morale of the Japanese people and its leadership, and the political acceptance of surrender is a murky one in the Pacific Survey reports. The Summary Report contends that the main weight of the AAF's campaign against Japanese cities lowered the morale and will to resist of the Japanese leaders, thereby forcing them to surrender.
