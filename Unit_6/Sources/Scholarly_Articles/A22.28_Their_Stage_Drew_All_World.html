Selection Overview

Their Stage Drew All the World: A New Look at the Scopes Evolution Trial By R. M. Cornelius The Scopes Trial of 1925 has again come into the limelight of historical review since 1980-81 witnessed the death of Sue K. Hicks, the last surviving lawyer involved in the trial, as well as marked the fiftieth anniversary of William Jennings Bryan College in Dayton. Defended by some and denounced by others, the trial not only was a major event covered by some two hundred newsmen, whose stories totaled about two million words, but also became a reference point for a series of similar legal controversies in at least seven states. In addition, it served as a major influence on the de- emphasizing of evolution in high school biology textbooks up until I960.1 The significance of the trial was further recognized first in 1977, when the trial courthouse was designated by the National Park Service as a National Historic Landmark, and then in 1979, when a one-million-dollar courthouse restoration and trial museum project was completed. Although the renovation of the ninety-year- old courthouse was long overdue, no less overdue is the revelation of the real story behind the trial. Since 1925, the Rhea County Courthouse has been famous as the stage for the "Monkey Trial" of John Thomas Scopes, accused of teaching evolution in Dayton, Tennessee, a small town nestled among the Cumberland hills forty miles north of Chattanooga. Many actors and action lines played a part in this legal drama, described by the title of one book as The World's Most Famous Court TriaP : a three-time presidential candidate, America's foremost criminal lawyer, the twentieth century's most cynical journalist, evolution vs. religion, academic freedom, and government authority vs. parental rights. Seeking juicy morsels for their omnivorous readers, journalists at home and abroad have periodically descended upon Dayton like the wolf on the fold. But the pressures of deadlines or of editorial bias have tended to keep many of these analysts from getting to the heart of the trial. In their willingness to serve the public a superficial sop, the sunshine reporters and summer re- searchers rarely discovered or acknowledged that for the citizens of Dayton the real reason for the trial was not evolution, religion, or academic freedom: it was economics. As explained by Warren Allem in a University of Tennessee history thesis, the trial began as a public relations scheme to interest a second generation of developers in the industrial potential of the Dayton area.3 When George W. Rappleyea, a young metallurgical engineer with the ailing Cumberland Coal and Iron Company, picked up his May 4, 1925, copy of the Chattanooga Times and read an an- nouncement of an American Civil Liberties Union offer to pay the expenses of a teacher willing to make a test case of the recently passed Tennessee anti-evolution law, he knew that a powerful remedy for the limping local economy had been discovered. Rappleyea hurried over to discuss the article with F. E. Robinson, "The Hustling Druggist." Teacher John T. Scopes and super- intendent of schools Walter White were brought in for consulta- tion. The next day, May 5th- the day the case supposedly began as a result of a "chance" meeting at Robinson's Drug Store- lawyers and town officials were drawn into the plot. Scopes agreed to act as the law-breaking villain and was promptly served with a warrant.4 The casting of the principals was completed when three-time presidential aspirant William Jennings Bryan accepted the invita- tion to help with the prosecution and when criminal lawyer Clarence Darrow and New York divorce lawyer Dudley Field Malone volunteered their services to manage the defense. Bryan was invited partly because the Dayton promoters had an eye for the spectacular and partly because some people wanted to acknowledge Bryan's influence in getting the anti-evolution law passed.
