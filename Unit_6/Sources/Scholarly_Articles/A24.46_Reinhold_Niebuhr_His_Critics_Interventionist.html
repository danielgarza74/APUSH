Reinhold Niebuhr and His Critics: The Interventionist Controversy in World War II
Justus D. Doenecke

JOURNAL Anglican and Episcopal History 64, no. 4 (1995)
EXCERPT Original journal pages 459-460
JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/42611736
Selection Overview

Doenecke recounts the bitter religious dispute between interventionist Reinhold Niebuhr and his pacifist and noninterventionist critics, and argues that those critics deserve a fairer second look, since Niebuhr's polemics misrepresented them even though he won the immediate case.

Foes of intervention, labeled "isolationists" by their opponents and subsequently by historians, ardently fought the interventionist proposals of President Franklin D. Roosevelt at every turn. From Hider's invasion of Poland, which commenced on 1 September 1939, to Japan's strike at Hawaii on 7 December 1941, the nation experi- enced a climate of uncertainty and recrimination seldom matched in its history. Such phrases as "warmonger," "appeaser," and "fifth- columnist" were very much in the air. Indeed few controversies have ever so polarized the nation. The churches were far from immune from such polemics. Many Protestant clergy had been so disillusioned by the consequences of World War I that they became pacifists. Yet the interventionist cause did not lack able and influential advocates, the foremost being Reinhold Niebuhr. Professor at Union Theological Seminary from 1928 until his retirement in 1960, Niebuhr s books, sermons, and journalism gave him an unmatched influence not only among Ameri- can clergy, but in the wider intellectual world as well.
