Who Was Warren G. Harding?
Louis W. Potts
 
ARTICLE Who Was Warren G. Harding?
JOURNAL The Historian 36, no. 4 (1974)
EXCERPT Original journal pages 621-622
JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/24443808
Selection Overview

Potts surveys how historians and biographers have judged Harding, from Schlesinger's polls and textbook condemnation to a recent revisionist wave (Bailey, Murray, Downes) that stresses his political skill and constructive achievements. He concludes that the low image persists despite revisionism and that the central disagreement is over the president's role: leading the people or reflecting them (historiography).

Who Was Warren G. Harding: ? Louis W. Potts* Historians in the past, perhaps following rather than informing the public, have generally declared that Warren Gamaliel Harding was the worst man to sit in the White House. Arthur M. Schlesinger's polls of fellow historians in 1948 and 1962 rated Harding a failure. Schlesinger cast the twenty-ninth President as an "amiable, easy-going man who had been pushed into office by machine politics and the ambitions of his wife" and who died knowing that he had betrayed the national trust.1 A recent effort at assessing American chief executives, one utilizing an enlarged sample and social-psycho logical scaling methods, shows that historians continue to rate Harding as lowest in prestige, weakest, most passive, and least productive.2 This low estimate of Harding dominates college textbooks. The National Experience depicts Harding as "a handsome, semi educated political hack with a modest taste for golf; a larger taste for women, liquor and poker; a complaisant disposition; an utterly empty mind; and an enduring loyalty to the Republican creed of 1890." The Historian sophisticated text on modern America which contrasts Harding to his predecessor, Woodrow Wilson. According to Arthur S. Link and William B. Catton, the Ohioan's vices and "a striking inability to discriminate between right and wrong" cast America adrift without moral leadership in the 1920s.4 Within the last decade, however, a chorus of revisionism has appeared. Thomas A. Bailey, for example, has challenged the Schlesinger polls and declared that Harding and his successor, Coolidge, "chimed in admirably with the times." Though depict ing the man from Marion, Ohio as "a glad-hander and philanderer," "the finest imitation of a President we ever had," and "a third-rater," Bailey did revise the usual portrait of Harding. He pointed out that Harding was not the inventor of the term "normalcy," nor the creature of crony Harry Daugherty, nor the compliant tool of party bosses meeting in the notorious "smoke filled room" during the Republican convention of 1920. Overall, Bailey judged Harding a President superior to Andrew Johnson, John Quincy Adams, and James Madison.5 This re-evaluation has surfaced in a few texts. One work observes, "He has often been characterized as lazy and incompetent, but he was actually both hard-working and politically shrewd; his true difficulties were indecisiveness and an unwillingness to offend."6 Another publication relates, "Harding occasionally showed courage and statesmanship," as in pardoning Eugene Debs, campaigning for an eight-hour day for steelworkers, and establishing the national budget.7 At the center of this wave of revisionism is an effort to re capture Harding's true personality, an effort which has produced four major biographical studies in the last nine years. These works, along with the estimations of Harding recorded by his contemporaries and analyses offered by a generation of students of the Harding epoch, yield a conflicting interpretation of the man from Marion.
