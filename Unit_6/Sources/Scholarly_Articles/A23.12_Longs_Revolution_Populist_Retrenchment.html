Selection Overview

Dethloff holds that Huey Long drew on Populist and Progressive precedents but was neither a resurgent Populist nor a liberal Progressive. Long's programs went beyond both traditions, and if anything was revolutionary it was his style, his total politicization, and his use of personal power.

The Longs: Revolution or Populist Retrenchment? By HENRY C. DETHLOFF Department of History Texas A&M University College Station, Texas There have been no two more debated and contested historical phenomena in Louisiana than the Populist movement of the 1890s and the Longism that has dominated much of the state's modern history. Both Longism and Populism received extreme praise and bitter condemnation by their contemporaries and by historians. Both have been styled "revolutionary." Populism, it is usually writ- ten, was effectively suppressed by the Louisiana Bourbon oli- garchy. Longism, it is often said, succeeded where Populism failed. Did Longism represent a final triumph of the Populist persuasion, or did it constitute a more radical revolution exceeding the defini- tions of Populism? Or did Huey Long bring no revolution at all, but dictatorship, demagoguery, and denigration? An understand- ing of Longism requires, in part, an evaluation of its intellectual sources, Populism and Progressivism. The organization of the Populist party in Louisiana in October, 1891, climaxed the development of a rural reform movement which had similar characteristics throughout the United States, but which in Louisiana had some unique qualities, and which basically passed through four distinct stages. The Grange, or Patrons of Husband- ry, organized in 1867, first came to Louisiana in 1872, and within a decade had passed its peak. Nationally and locally the Grange organization promoted regulation of railroads, opposed land and commodity speculation, encouraged expanded public education, and advocated easier credit. The second stage is marked by the organization of the Louisiana Farmers' Union in 1885, in Lincoln Parish. This organization had less of a social and more of a business orientation, but espoused most of the Grange causes. The third stage is marked by the merger of the Louisiana Farmers' Union with the Texas Farm- ers' Alliance in 1887, to create the National Farmers' Alliance and Co-Operative Union of America, better knowrr as the Southern Alliance. Finally, in 1891, a group of Louisiana Alliancemen orga- nized the Populist party. Between 1894 and 1896 Louisiana Popu- lists fused with New Orleans reformers in a Citizens League, and with New Republicans, to challenge Democratic party control.' The 1890s were full of ferment, stirring with social and economic readjustments, politically explosive, exciting, and full of history. These were the years when Louisiana's modern government and political traditions were being formed. The Progressive movement is less well-defined than the Populist movement, in terms of its organization, goals and time span. The Progressive is usually regarded as an urban, twentieth-century, middle-class reformer who wanted to enlarge governmental powers on the state and national level in order to control and offset the power of private economic groups over the nation's political and economic institutions. On the national level the most prominent Progressive leaders include Republicans Robert M. LaFollette and Theodore Roosevelt, and Democrat Woodrow Wilson. John M. Parker, who was scheduled to run as vice-president with Theodore Roosevelt on a Progressive party ticket in 1916, and who did serve as a reform governor between 1920 and 1924, is Louisiana's most recognized Progressive leader. Generally, historians reject any lineal or intellectual connection between Populism and Pro- gressivism, while proposing that Progressivism, nonetheless, achieved most of the Populist objectives such as railroad regulation suffrage reforms, including the Australian ballot and the initiativ and referendum, the graduated income tax, the eight-hour day for labor, and the direct election of U.S. senators.2 Both Populism and Progressivism have been regarded by most historians as having had little to do with Louisiana's historical development.
