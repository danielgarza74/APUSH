EXCERPT Original journal pages 351-352

Selection Overview

Burk argues that Britain grew steadily dependent on American private and then public finance to feed its people and fight the war, so that by 1917–18 financial leverage had shifted decisively to Washington. She traces this through the British missions to the United States.

The Historical Journal, 22, 2 ('979), pp. 35 1-372 Printed in Great Britain THE DIPLOMACY OF FINANCE: BRITISH FINANCIAL MISSIONS TO THE UNITED STATES 1914-1918* KATHLEEN BURK St Hugh's College, Oxford It is a commonplace among historians of the First World War that during this period Britain and America changed positions as dominant and subordinate financial powers. The great gap in the histories, however, has been any description of how this came about. Historians of the period have generally relied on one book, E. V. Morgan's Studies in British financial policy I9I4-I925 (1952), with the occasional use of Henry F. Grady's British war finance, IqI4-IgI9 (I923). Both are very useful, and within its field Morgan in particular will be difficult to supplant; but within the context of Anglo-American relations they only treat one side of the story. It is clear that during the war Britain was dependent increasingly on American finance, private and later public, both to supply her civilian and military populations and to fight the war; and further, that by late I9I6 decisions taken in America could have a direct bearing on British ability to finance her own operations. Once America entered the war this influence became even more direct. Negotiations over finance largely took place in America, by means of temporary and permanent missions. This article will describe the most important of these missions, and trace the gradual change in the balance of British and American financial power. For fifty years before the war the United States had been the single most important country for British long-term foreign investment, attracting almost 2I % of the new overseas issues in the British capital market.' London was the centre of the international money market, and the United States depended on the stability and convertibility of the pound in order to carry on her export trade. Britain exploited her international financial position in her war propaganda, comparing the stability of the pound with the depreciation of the German mark,2 and her financial and economic power underpinned the whole Entente. 352 KATHLEEN BURK in December 1914 the pound for the first time fell against the dollar in New York it was a matter of some concern. During the first half of 1915, however, the Treasury found it almost impossible to persuade the cabinet to take the problem seriously, possibly because the financing of the war was being accomplished reasonably easily. But purchasing goods with sterling and with Treasury notes in Britain and the empire was one thing; purchasing goods in the United States, where it was necessary to buy ever increasing numbers of dollars first, was quite another. The amount of goods being purchased in the United States by the British government was rising quite substantially, with the growing dependence on America for food, cotton, oil and various kinds of munitions, to name the more important.3 These goods were paid for through the American bankers J. P. Morgan & Co. Morgan's had become the financial agent for the British government soon after the outbreak of war (they also became the purchasing agent in America for the war office and Admiralty in January 1915). Neither the Treasury nor the Bank of England had connexions or machinery for overseas transactions and these were supplied by the various interlocking Morgan banking organizations. E. C. Grenfell of E. C. Grenfell & Co. of London was a partner in J. P. Morgan & Co. of New York, and J. P. Morgan was a partner in E. C. Grenfell & Co., while both men sat on the board of Morgan et Cie in Paris. Further, the companies were among the few banks which had no links with German firms.4 Until the spring of 1917 the procedure was that Grenfell would call in at the Treasury each day to talk over the trend in the exchange rate or problems connected with the payment of British government debts in the United States, and then he would cable the chancellor's instructions to the Morgan firm in New York. Further, Morgan personally kept Grenfell abreast of American financial and business developments, information about which Grenfell then passed on to the Treasury. The development which worried both sides of the Morgan connexion in 1915 was the decline in the rate of the pound in New York.
