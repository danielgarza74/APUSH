Selection Overview

Buenker argues that Illinois's prohibition fight, culminating in ratification of the federal amendment, was above all a cultural clash between old-stock Protestants, mostly rural and Republican, and new-stock, largely non-Protestant urban legislators, rather than a simple partisan or economic contest.

Nowhere were these disagreements more basic than on the use of alcoholic beverages, for the immigrant had built a whole segment of his culture around them, where- as the native had come to regard them as a major source of the evils that threatened the traditional American system of values. This cultural gap was evident even before prohibition became a celebrated legislative issue. Ardent advocates such as the Anti-Saloon League and the Women's Christian Tem- perance Union drew nearly all their membership from Amer- icans of old-stock Protestant antecedents. The former styled itself "the Protestant Church in action," and the atti- tude of the latter was best expressed by one of its founders, Frances Willard, who claimed, "I am first a Christian, then I am a Saxon, then I am an American, and when I get home to heaven I expect to register from Evanston." The Prohibition party, proclaiming that the great influx of recent immigrants was a "menace to our institutions," established itself as the champion of the ancient American culture by calling for the "Americanization of aliens" and the mandatory use of the Bible in public schools.2 The leadership of the Anti-Saloon League in Illinois was simi- larly drawn from the state's old-stock population, featuring A.
