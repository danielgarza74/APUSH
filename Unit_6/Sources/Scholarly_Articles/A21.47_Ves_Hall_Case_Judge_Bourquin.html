Selection Overview

Gutfeld argues that wartime hysteria in Montana, fed by mining-company-controlled newspapers, helped produce the federal Sedition Act after Judge Bourquin's ruling in the Ves Hall case. He adds that business interests skillfully manipulated patriotic fear.

The Ves Hall Case, Judge Bourquin, and the Sedition Act of 1918 ARNON GUTFELD The author is a graduate student in the U of Montana. DURING WORLD WAR I, an extraordinary amount of hysteria prevailed in Montana. Citizens conducted a frenzied campaign to find and punish supposed traitors and spies. One of the men who dared to oppose the "super-patriots" was Federal District Judge George M. Bourquin. His ruling in the case of the United States vs. Ves Hall had a marked effect on subsequent events in Montana and was instrumental in the passage of stringent federal legislation designed to suppress the opponents of war. Several factors contributed to the absence of reason in Montana. During the war, labor, long-subdued, fought management.' In taliation, the Montana press, which was largely controlled by mining companies, waged a relentless campaign in favor of the effort and adamantly opposed to dissent.2 The companies viewe treason any attempt to change the status quo.
