John H., Jr. Bracey, August Meier

EXCERPT Original journal pages 1-2

Selection Overview

Bracey and Meier challenge Garfinkel's view of the NAACP as a reluctant, conservative latecomer, using newly available papers to show it played a leading role in the 1941 March on Washington campaign, while granting that its relations with Randolph mixed cooperation and rivalry.

Indeed the evidence in these files indi- cates that the NAACP played a crucial role, whose very exis- tence has not hitherto been imagined possible. From its founding in 1909, the NAACP had concentrated its efforts on using litigation in the courts and legislative lobby- ing to secure enforcement of the rights of Afro- Americans as guaranteed by the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments. The NAACP was only marginally involved in labor questions because at the time there was no political or constitutional basis upon which to attack economic discrimination. Moreover, the National Urban League, founded in 1911, undertook as its major task the improvement of job opportunities principally by using polite forms of moral suasion to cultivate support among white business elites. There was even a tacit understanding be- tween the two organizations as to this division of labor.3 Then, during and after World War I, the militant socialist A. Philip Randolph emerged as the leading advocate of the cause of black workers and was known chiefly for his role in organizing and leading the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters. While these and other agencies such as the YMC A and YWCA depart- ments for Negro work played complementary and supportive roles, interorganizational tensions and leadership rivalries did exist. Among other things, the history of the 1941 March on Washington offers a superb example of the ambivalent and complex relations that have historically existed among black leadership and advancement organizations.
