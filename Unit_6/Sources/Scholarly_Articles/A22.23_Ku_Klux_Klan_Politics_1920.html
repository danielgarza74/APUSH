The Ku Klux Klan in Politics in the 1920's
David Chalmers
 
ARTICLE The Ku Klux Klan in Politics in the 1920's
JOURNAL The Mississippi Quarterly 18, no. 4 (1965)
EXCERPT Original journal pages 234-235
JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/26473702
Selection Overview

Chalmers surveys the Klan's electoral reach, from Maine to Oregon, in cities as well as the South, calling it a real political force that marched, elected and sometimes terrorized. He concludes it fed nativism and anti-Catholicism but built no durable movement, and may have pushed ethnic urban voters toward Democrats.

DAVID CHALMERS University of Florida The Ku Klux Klan In Politics In The 1920's Speaking for the Social Science Research Council at its 1937 At lantic City meeting, Professor Roy Nichols urged the inclusion of the "growth and decline of the Ku Klux Klan" in any adequate American history.1 One possible obstacle was that no adequate history of the Klan existed. In 1924, when the hooded tide was at its high water mark, John M. Mecklin, a former Mississippi Presbyterian minister turned hard nosed sociologist, had written an excellent analysis of the Klan mind.2 Twelve years later, in 1936, E. H. Loucks had put out his account of the Klan in Pennsylvania, the single best work on the Invisible Empire. The fact that his study was published, in limited edition by the Telegraph Press of Harrisburg, Pennsylvania,3 was not the result of an overwhelm ing interest in the Klan by the historical and publishing communities, but rather to please that once great inspiration to scholars, the publica tion requirement of the Columbia Graduate School. And so, for a good thirty years, this was all there was. There had been some fine contempo rary analysis of the reasons for the Klan,4 textbooks did include under standing account of its rise and decline, but few historians bothered to tell its story. In the 1920's the Klan was a powerful force which marched, elect ed, and sometimes terrorized, literally from Maine to California. KU KLUX KLAN IN 1920'S 235 was a member of a hooded", violence-oriented organization which preach ed the rejection of all but the white, protestant, native-born Americans? To say that the Klan had been reborn, expanded to perhaps four million members, burning through America like a prairie fire, and then declined, does not tell enough. Perhaps the Klan had relatively little lasting effect, but it was a vital force in the 1920s, and the story of politics and of every day life can not be understood without it. Only the historians of Georgia, Alabama, Texas, Oklahoma, Kan sas, and occasionally Indiana and Illinois have chronicled the Klan. A quarter of a century after the Klan touched the peak of its power, the graduate students began to work. They studied Indiana, Ohio, Oregon, and the Great Lakes region, followed by work on Colorado, Oregon, Louisiana, Mississippi, the South, the Southwest, and probably many others.5 And at last, publication began.6 Although the strength of the Klan fluctuated rapidly and was un evenly spread, it was sufficiently concentrated to play an important role in the political life of many states. It defeated Catholics, Jews, and hostile Prostestants, and elected its members, friends, and willing politicians to office. Even where the Klan was not successful, it was often a vital issue, forcing men to battle or to silence. The Klan helped elect sixteen members of the U. S. Senate.
