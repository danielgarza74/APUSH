EXCERPT Original journal pages 3-4

Selection Overview

Beaumont argues that Britain's bomber offensive took shape under heavy Soviet and public pressure for a second front that the Western Allies could not yet supply. He treats bombing as a politically driven stopgap, while granting that the Eastern Front was the war's largest theater.

Roger Beaumont The Bomber Offensive as a Second Front A call for a 'Second Front' - an invasion of Europe from the west came less than a month after the German attack on Russia, when on 18 July 1941 Stalin cabled Churchill: The military position of the Soviet Union as well as that of Great Britain would be significantly improved if a front against Hitler were established in the West and the North ...I It was not the first call nor the last. Demands for a Second Front continued until D-Day in June 1944 and recriminations thereafter. British reluctance to attack in 1942 and Churchill's scepticism regarding the likelihood of success have frequently been censured by American as well as by Soviet and some British critics. Whatever the motives of their leaders, by mid-1941 major British forces had been pushed off the continent by the Germans three times in just over a year - from Norway in April 1940, from France in May 1940, and from Greece in May 1941. Nearly bankrupted by the cost of preparing to fight a war not accepted as likely by the British government until 1938, Britain's stressed economy and military forces nevertheless had held out against the nazis for a full year after France's surrender in June 1940, while bolstered by an increasing flow of American aid and encouragement. Some of Britain's imperial defence problems were critical to Anglo- American and ultimately Allied strategy, e.g. the defence of Suez, the Battle of the Atlantic, and fending off Japanese threats to India. From 1940 to 1942, there, as in Europe, successes were outweighed by failures. The British drove back the Italians in Libya and Ethiopia in late 1940, and then, in the spring of 1941, Rommel and the Afrika Korps came to the aid of Mussolini's battered legions. Losses incurred in the Greek expedition cost the British the initiative in North Africa for some time. Even at the time, the plight of Russia was seen very much as Stalin's doing. He had signed the Non-Aggression Pact that gave Hitler a free hand against Poland, and had provided co-operation and supplies to the nazis as they conquered nine countries in eighteen months. The Soviets' bludgeoning of Finland in the winter of 1939-40 had almost led to direct combat with Anglo-French forces, and Stalin had watched with little evident concern as the Luftwaffe bombed Britain and nazi forces moved into Africa and the Balkans. In the spring of 1941, Churchill repeatedly warned Stalin of a German invasion, but was ignored. After the Germans struck in June 1941, there was little that Britain could do while Russia burned and bled in late 1941 and early 1942; the British bomber offensive, the Battle of the Atlantic, occasional commando raids and the North Africa war were mere pin-pricks in the hide of a German behemoth. The silent naval blockade, mainstay of British strategy in several wars, took its toll, but all too slowly, due to the fact that the nazi war machine was fed substantial Soviet aid and able to withstand that pressure for almost two years. With their backs to the wall in the fall and winter of 1941-42, the Soviets could not evade or retreat, nor did they have the means or breathing space to use methods of war thrifty in lives.2 They did not fully trust Churchill, long an implacable enemy of bolshevism, although he had surprised many by offering support to Russia the day after the German attack. Not only was substantial western military aid, in the form of major attacks or substantial supplies, slow in coming, but differing perceptions of war between a continental and a maritime power were visible in the 'Grand Alliance' from the outset. Stalin's first message to Churchill, ten days after the nazi invasion of the USSR on 22 June 1941, demanded an immediate major landing on the continent, in France or Norway.3 Throughout much of the war, Soviet bitterness toward continual postponement of a major landing increased, especially since it was widely recognized in the US and Britain that the Eastern Front had become the major theatre of war, in terms of scale and ferocity, and offered the greatest potential for Allied military catastrophe. The disasters of 1941-42 transformed the Soviet system. The commissars' authority in the army was substantially weakened as Red Army professionals regained control.
