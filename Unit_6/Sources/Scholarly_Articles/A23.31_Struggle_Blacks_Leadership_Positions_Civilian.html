123 THE STRUGGLE OF BLACKS FOR LEADERSHIP POSITIONS IN THE CIVILIAN CONSERVATION CORPS: 1933-1942 Calvin W. Gower* In February, 1935, Robert Fechner, the national Director of the Civilian Conservation Corps, in correspondence concerning the occasional placement of a few blacks in camps which were for white enrollees, stated that such a breach in the policy of racially segregated camps was permissible and workable "because of the natural adaptability of Negroes to serve as cooks." A few months later Fechner reiterated his views on this matter asserting that "in cases of this kind the small group of Negroes will be assigned to kitchen police or similar camp duties." The attitude reflected in these statements was a dominant feature of the C.C.C. operation, and segregation and/or discrimination against blacks occurred throughout the nine years of the Corps' existence. These circumstances prevailed in spite of the fact that the law establishing the C.C.C. forbade discrimination "on account of race, color, or creed . . . . "1 Discrimination against Negroes in regard to leadership positions in the C.C.C. was one aspect of the operation of the agency which brought forth numerous protests from a number of blacks and their supporters. One of the protestors, Edgar G. Brown, did gain a limited administrative post in the Corps, in large part because of backing from President Franklin D.Roosevelt, but his difficulties in the agency were a reflection of the overall struggle of blacks in their efforts to secure executive spots in the C.C.C. This article concerns first the problems of black Americans in general in the Corps, and particularly the obstacles they encountered as they endeavored to obtain leadership positions in the agency. The analysis of Brown's experiences in the Corps sheds more light on the nature of Roosevelt's so-called "Black Cabinet."2 The Civilian Conservation Corps, began functioning in April, 1933, and continued in existence until July, 1942. The first national Director of the C.C.C. was Robert Fechner, a labor leader who had grown up in the South. One of Fechner's principal tasks as Director was to coordinate the work of thefour major executive departments included in the C.C.C. organization. The Department of Labor enrolled the young men; the Department of War using regular and reserve Army officers supervised the C.C.C. camps; the Departments of the Interior and Agriculture organized and directed the conservation work which the enrollees performed. Fechner, with the aid of an Advisory Council, kept this rather complex arrangement operating in an effective manner.3 The Corps employed approximately 300,000 youths annually during its nine years of activity. These young men made significant achievements in conservation efforts, and the C.C.C. was one of the most popular New Deal agencies. However, in 1942, World War II and other developments ended the life of the Corps.4 The accomplishments of the New Deal regarding American blacks were rather inconclusive. Possibly the best description of the policy of the New Dealers in general towards blacks is that Roosevelt and most of his supporters were fainthearted about challenging the racial status quo in the United States,5 and that characterization definitely applies in the case of the Civilian Conservation Corps. A consideration of various features of the C.C.C. reveals that the agency deviated little from the racist practices of the past; segregation and discrimination against blacks were constant features of the Corps.
