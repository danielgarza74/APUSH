President Warren G. Harding and the British War Debt Question, 1921-1923 PHILIP A. GRANT, JR. Professor of History Pace University Throughout the Administration of President Warren G. Harding the United States and Great Britain encountered relatively few problems in the conduct of their diplomatic relations with one another. Perhaps the most exasperating feature of Anglo-American diplomacy was the resolution of the foreign debt question. During World War I the United States had provided between four and five billion dollars in financial support to Britain. The issue of the British war debt had not been included on the agenda of the Paris Peace Conference, nor was it incorporated into the settlement finalized by the Reparation Commission in 1921. Since Great Britain had entered World War I as early as August 1914 and had suffered severely both in terms of human and economic losses, it was hopeful that its financial indebtedness to the United States might be considerably reduced. Un willing to accept the premise that World War I had been a common effort to defeat a uniquely militaristic enemy or that the United States had been spared from the most brutal aspects of the conflict, the American people through their representatives in Congress were adamant in their insistence that the entire British debt be paid without undue delay. The initial reluctance of the British government to acknowledge the validity of the World War I debt obviously strained Anglo-American relations. On December 6, 1921 President Harding, delivering his first State of the Union Message, urged a joint session of Congress to approve legislation designed to seek a viable solution to the lingering question of foreign debts. In February 1922 Congress responded to the President's appeal by passing the World War Foreign Debt Commis sion Act. This statute mandated the appointment of a special panel to negotiate the details of debt payments with the World War I allies of the United States. The commission was instructed to communicate with the various European nations on an individual basis. It was required to determine not only the precise amount of each outstanding debt, but also to arrange a schedule of payments over a period not to exceed twenty-five years and establish the rate of annual interest at not less than four and one-half percent.1 The World War Foreign Debt Commission was chaired by Secretary of the Treasury Andrew W. Mellon. Its other members were Secretary of State Charles Evans Hughes, Secretary of Commerce Herbert Hoover, Senator Reed Smoot of Utah, and Representative Theodore E. Burton of Ohio. None of the agreements conformed to the stipulations imposed by the 1922 statute. Consequently, it was necessary for the House and Senate to review each of the twelve agreements.2 At its initial meeting on April 18 the commission requested the Department of State to inform each of the various debtor nations of Europe of its "desire to receive any proposals or representations which said Government may wish to make for the settlement or refunding of its obligations." Between the adjournment of the April 18 session and the end of 1922 the commission was to assemble for additional meetings on June 1, June 30, July 27, August 10, and September 22.3 On June 15 Secretary of State Charles Evans Hughes instructed George Harvey, the American Ambassador in London, to express the desire of the commission to begin negotiations with the British Government at the earliest convenient date. On July 17 Harvey informed Hughes that a special British delegation would leave for the United States at an unspecified date in early September. On September 28 the British Embassy in Washington reported that the delegation would depart during the latter half of October. Due to the abrupt resignation of Prime Minister David Lloyd George and his Cabinet on October 19 and the necessity of a general election on November 15, it was decided to postpone the voyage of the delegation until the new prime minister, A. Bonar Law, had completed the organization of his government.4 On August 1 Sir Arthur Balfour, the Acting British Foreign Secretary, had addressed a note to France and several other European nations on the question of interallied debts. While acknowledging Great Britain's obligation to the United States, Balfour asserted: . . . But evidently they cannot do so without profoundly modifying the course which, in different circumstances, they would have wished to pursue. They cannot treat the repayment of the Anglo-American loan as if it were an isolated incident in which only the United States of America and Great Britain had any concern. It is but one of a connected series of transactions, in which this country sometimes as debtor, sometimes as creditor, and, if our undoubted obligations as a debtor are to be enforced, our not less undoubted rights as a creditor cannot be left wholly in abeyance. Stating that the British policy "of refusing to make demands upon its debtors is only tolerable as long as it is generally accepted," Balfour concluded as follows: . . . they desire to explain that the amount of interest and repayment for which they ask depends not so much on what France and other Allies owe to Great Britain as on what Great Britain has to pay America. The policy favored by His Majesty's Government is . . . that our surrendering their share of German reparation and writing off, through our great transaction, the whole body of inter-allied indebtedness. But, if this is found impossible of accomplishment, we wish it to be understood that we do not in any event desire to make a profit out of any less satisfactory agreement.
