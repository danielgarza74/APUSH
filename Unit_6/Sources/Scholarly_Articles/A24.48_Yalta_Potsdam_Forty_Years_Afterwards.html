Yalta and Potsdam: forty years afterwards ROBIN EDMONDS" In the course of 1945 what were then called the Three Great Powers met four times, after years of carnage, in an attempt to establish the postwar political structure.1 Of all their meetings it is the first, held at Yalta, that has captured the public imagination. It is immortalized by the photograph of Churchill, Roosevelt and Stalin, taken in the Livadia Palace on 10 February 1945-Churchill smoking his cigar, Roosevelt haggard, a cigarette in his left hand, Stalin composed, with folded hands.2 Within a few years, the Crimean Conference (as it was officially called) was regarded, at one end of the spectrum, as the scene of major political blunders made by the Western leaders, or alternatively, at the other, as a start on the road that should have led-had it not been frustrated-to a stable international order. At the time when some seven hundred American and British officials arrived in midwinter to spend just over a week in the Crimea (of which Churchill remarked beforehand, 'if we had spent ten years on research, we could not have found a worse place in the world'),3 public hopes were running very high indeed. It was not long since Walter Lippman had expressed the belief that 'not since the unity of the ancient world was disrupted' had there been 'so good a prospect of a settled peace'.4 This belief was then by no means uncommon. So great was the general euphoria that the following words could have been written immediately after the signature of the Yalta Declaration on Liberated Europe: The principles of nationality and of political justice are recognised as they never have been before. The measured prose . . . appeals to the imagination . . . with a force beyond that of poetry. It is the poetry of real life, the charter of hope and of national well-being to the peoples it frees . . 5 In fact, this passage forms part of an assessment of an earlier peacemaking attempt-that made at the Congress of Berlin in 1878. Thirty-six years after the signature of the Treaty of Berlin, the host government of that Congress had become the principal enemy of the Western powers, while Russia (described in the same article as 'a peril ... to civilisation itself') had become their principal ally, as it did again in the early 1940s. At a similar distance in time from the great conferences of 1945, there has been a comparable reversal of international roles. Today, the very word 'Yalta' has a sinister ring. 198 YALTA AND POTSDAM: FORTY YEARS AFTERWARDS the flow of prose evoked by the fortieth anniversary of Yalta, to the decisions reached at that conference? That the outcome of the Yalta Conference was oversold at the time is common ground. Nevertheless, this conference cannot be properly assessed in isolation. It must be considered not only against the military background of February 1945, but also in conjunction with the conference that assembled five months later at Potsdam- officially called the Conference of Berlin-where the 'Big Three' presided over a process which began a division of Europe more definitive than anything discussed at Yalta. These two conferences, taken together, should also, I suggest, be regarded as marking the end of an era rather than the beginning of a new one. Today, what is most striking about the Potsdam Conference is that the three leaders left Berlin without discussing jointly the fact that the world whose destinies they were seeking to decide was at that very moment on the eve of entering the atomic age. Objectives at Yalta When Churchill, Roosevelt and Stalin held their first session at Yalta on 4 February 1945, there was one bond that held the Grand Alliance together. It was of paramount importance to all three of them: their common need to defeat Germany and to ensure that it could never again threaten the peace of Europe. The military context of the Yalta Conference now looks simple enough: the meeting took place, as it turned out, only three months before VE Day, four months before the first atomic explosion at Alamogordo, New Mexico, and six months before VJ Day. It looked very different at the time. In Western Europe, General Eisenhower's forces had not yet crossed the Rhine (indeed, it was only seven weeks since the German counter-offensive had taken them by surprise in the Ardennes). In north Italy, General Alexander's depleted forces were bogged down.6 In the Pacific, US forces were about to land on Iwojima; the bitter battle for Okinawa lay ahead; and the final assault on the Japanese mainland was expected to cost massive American casualties.7 In central Europe, Marshal Zhukov's forces, having captured Warsaw on 17 January, had reached the river Oder; the western-most tip of his salient was, at the moment when the Yalta Conference began, thirty-five miles from Berlin. As we now know, Zhukov's own directive of 26 January foresaw the capture of Berlin by 15-16 February, but-for reasons that are still not entirely clear-the assault was postponed, on Stalin's orders.8 One other battle formed a critical part of the political backdrop to the scene at Yalta, although it had been fought-and lost-six months earlier: the Warsaw rising. Of the many capital cities entered by allied troops in 1945, Warsaw was the only one that was not only in ruins but virtually deserted. On 1 August 1944, the day on which Soviet troops reached the outskirts of the city and the Armja Krajowa (AK, the home army) rose against the Germans, the population of Warsaw numbered almost a million.
