Selection Overview

The Friedheims describe Seattle labor as unusually radical and cohesive, yet the 1919 general strike frightened the public into seeing Bolshevik revolution and proved disastrous for the movement, which then fell prey to an open-shop drive. Their focus is a single city's labor movement.

The Seattle Labor Movement, 1919-20 BY ROBERT L. AND ROBIN FRIEDHEIM Labor organization in Seattle during the years following World War I is of particular interest to students of the history of American labor movements. In many ways it was unique-in its radicalism, its cohesiveness, its ideology. It planned and carried out one of the two city-wide general strikes in the history of the United States: the Seattle General Strike of 1919, which kept some 60,000 workers offthe job and virtually closed down the city for five days. But the strike frightened people and led them to believe that a Bolshevik revolution threatened the country; and in the end itproved disastrous for the labor organization whose very effectiveness had made it possible.1 Until World War I (and even considerably later), Seattle had a typical "colonial" economy, dependent upon extractive industries and eastern capital. World War I seemed to offerthe Queen City the opportunity to develop a genuine in- dustrial economy controlled by local capital. Wartime demands fostered a great increase in industrial production in Seattle: in 1914, Seattle's 1,014 industrial establishments produced $64,475,000 worth of manufactured products; by 1919, there were 1,229 industrial establishments which produced $274,431,000 worth. The labor force rose with the upward curve of the indus- trial graph. In 1919 more than 40,000 workers were employed in true industrial pursuits; they were paid more than $63 million in wages-an increase of more than 190 per cent, well more than double the national wage increase for that year.2 The new workers who swarmed into the rap- idly growing Seattle area were quickly organized by existing labor unions. Seattle was well known as a labor town with a tradition of vigorous labor activity: in 1915, 15,000 men were union mem- bers; by 1917, there were 40,000; by late 1918, 60,000. The vigor of the unions' organizing activities may be measured by the fact that there were more unionists than industrial workers in Seattle. Labor leaders were proud that the city seemed well on its way to becoming a closed-shop town. The industrial workers in the shipyards and metal trades were not the only ones union- ized, however; the craftsmen of the building trades, the teamsters, tailors, barbers, and some retail and drugstore clerks were also working under union-shop conditions. Labor claimed that, in 1919, 50 per cent of the eligible labor force in Seattle was unionized.3 The unions of Seattle were formally part of the American Federation of Labor, and, as was com- mon in that organization, the locals were organ- ized along craft lines. In 1919, there were 110 small, autonomous, highly centralized craft locals in the city.4 They were directly responsible to their own craft national or international unions, which in turn federated to make up the AF of L. Cutting across this hierarchical pattern were re- gional, local, and coordinating bodies, whose purpose was to overcome the AF of L tendency toward craft exclusiveness. These were the state federations, city centrals, and trade councils, such as building- or metal-trades councils. The Council had its own Labor Temple in downtown Seattle, and during its regular Wednesday evening busi- ness meetings, affairs of concern to Seattle work- ingmen were discussed. Craft locals, even those holding memberships in trade councils, often affiliated also with the Central Labor Council. Each local sent to the Central a slate of three rank-and-file delegates. There was no AF of L requirement that a local affiliate with a city cen- tral, but Seattle men boasted that their Central had the highest participation of locals in the country.5 It was, then, not the formal organization of Seattle labor that was distinctive, but rather the use to which the organization was put. Instead of feeling responsible primarily to their craft nationals and internationals, Seattle locals gave their loyalty to their local coordinating bodies- the trade councils and the Central Labor Council. No matter what union a Seattle worker belonged to, he was most conscious of being a member of a Seattle labor organization. This intense local- ism was unique. The AF of L during this period did not seek the same goals Seattle workers sought, nor did it envision the same place for workingmen in American society that Seattle workers envisioned. Consequently, the Seattle labor movement caused the AF of L leaders endless trouble, since it stood for everything Samuel Gompers rejected: labor in politics, industrial unionism, and nation- alization of key industries. The Seattle labor movement was so distinctive that even the Indus- trial Workers of the World characterized it as a movement "affiliated- more in form than in spirit- with the American Federation of Labor/'6 The Seattle AF of L manifested its distaste for the parent body by sending its best known local leader, James A. Duncan, to the AF of L conven- tions in 1919 and 1920 to cast the sole vote in opposition to the reelection of Samuel Gompers as president. Preventing his unanimous election was a source of great satisfaction to Seattle labor, a symbol of defiance that could be indulged with- out great cost. But, although relations between Seattle and AF of L headquarters were often strained, they were never broken. While some vocal elements in the local union movement ad- vocated splitting with the AF of L, Seattle's re- sponsible labor leaders rejected the proposal be- cause they did not want to be isolated from the mainstream of American labor. One reason Seattle labor disliked AF of L lead- ership was that they identified it with the East. And to Seattle workers, "the East meant Wall Street." Eastern labor, they maintained, was in- sufficiently militant in fighting Wall Street, per- haps even collaborated with it. The eastern rank and file were lacking in class-conscious "educa- tion." And eastern labor was Wet: "they think more of their dinner parties and socials than they think of business."7 In contrast, Seattle labor thought of itself as disciplined, organized, and militant. "Our news- boys union members are much better posted on economics than some of the Eastern delegates to the AF of L Convention."8 Even the Seattle con- servatives shared the feeling of separateness from the East. Local workers were particularly bitter because they regarded their own employers as prisoners of the hidden Wall Street manipulators. Seattle labor, however, felt close bonds of kin- ship with other West Coast labor movements. When Seattle leaders used the term "Western," the entire Coast was implied: north to Canada, south to San Francisco- and inland to Butte. Night after night in Seattle union meetings, re- ports were made on the progress and problems of brother unionists on the Coast. Visitors from Vancouver, B.C., were encouraged to describe the conditions of the Canadian One Big Union move- ment. Guests from San Francisco, Oakland, Port- land, and Butte were asked to address local labor audiences, and local unions gave visitors permis- sion to raise funds for their embattled unions. The Seattle AF of L movement was more radi- cal than most other American city-wide move- ments. Other cities in other areas of the country had radical fringes, but in Seattle there was wide consensus on accepting radical solutions to the question of labor's place in society. Harry Ault, editor of the Seattle Union Record, described this attitude clearly: I believe that 95% of us agree that the workers should control the industries. Nearly all of us agree on that but very strenuously disagree on the method.
