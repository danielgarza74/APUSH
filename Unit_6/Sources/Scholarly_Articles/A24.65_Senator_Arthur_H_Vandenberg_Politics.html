EXCERPT Original journal pages 219-220

Selection Overview

Hill argues that partisan and institutional rivalry over foreign policy drove Roosevelt, Truman and Congress toward bipartisanship, and that Vandenberg, once a leading anti-interventionist, was the chief Republican architect of that consensus and of its anti-Soviet cast.

VANDENBERG, THE POLITICS OF BIPARTISANSHIP, AND THE ORIGINS OF ANTI-SOVIET CONSENSUS, 1941-1946 The close relationship between partisanship and executive-legislative rivalry in the conduct of American foreign policy had resulted by the 1940s in nearly universal dissatisfaction. The origins of the relationship were political and constitu tional. The fierce partisan struggle that had characterized American diplomacy arose from electoral advantage-seeking, from the expression of partisan visions of American society in foreign policy objectives, and from efforts to use foreign policy to shape the economic development and political evolution of the nation in direc tions which were contested.! The contest over institutional prerogatives was caused by the American Constitution which established separate, independently empowered branches of government and assigned them overlapping powers and responsibilities. The connection between the two was the permanent representation of the political opposition in Congress, which meant that the executive-legislative contest was intensified by partisanship and reflected not only institutional rivalry but also political conflicts of interest and ideology.2 As the result of the partisan warfare and institutional contest that had character ized the fights over the League of Nations in 1919-1920 and neutrality and inter vention in 1939-1941, the President and his subordinates feared legislative obstruc tion of their plans for the postwar world. Legislators sensed their ignorance and impotence in the face of unilateral executive direction of the war. The Roosevelt administration was confronted by the prospect of partisan opposition, the Repub licans by ineffectuality. Informed by a recognition of their respective disadvantages, the President and Congress, and the majority and opposition, became increasingly committed during the war to the development of a new politics of partisan accom modation and institutional cooperation.3 Fashioning this new politics of bipartisanship, which meant both developing the practice of cooperation and forging a political consensus on foreign policy, was the achievement of Senator Arthur H. Vandenberg.
