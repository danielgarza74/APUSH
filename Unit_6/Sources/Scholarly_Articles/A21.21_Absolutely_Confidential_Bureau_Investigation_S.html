Selection Overview

Fall 2023  119 “Absolutely Confidential” The Bureau of Investigation’s Suppression of Evidence in the Centralia Tragedy of 1919 tom copeland A t about 3 p.m. on Tuesday, No- vember 11, 1919, Frederic W.  McIntosh, a special agent with the Seattle branch of the Bureau of Investigation (predecessor of the Fed- eral Bureau of Investigation), received notice of a disturbance in Centralia, Washington, a lumber and railroad center 68 miles south of Seattle. There had been a reported attack by armed members of the Industrial Workers of the World (IWW) union on former servicemen in an Armistice Day pa- rade. Three veterans had been killed, and another was expected to die. Sev- eral others had been wounded.1 McIn- tosh received instructions from Frank Burke, the chief of the Bureau of Inves- tigation (BOI) in Washington, D.C.: “Investigate situation at Centralia com- pletely. Ascertain if any of the subjects are aliens.”2 When McIntosh arrived in Centralia by train at about 7:30 that evening, he reported to Burke that “a perfect jam of humanity” surround- ed the jail where 11 members of the IWW were being held. A “hangman’s noose” was tied on the cross arm of a telephone pole, and the disposition of the crowd was “very ugly.” The Seattle agent did what he could to assist local officers by trying to calm those milling in the street.3 But why was the bureau involved in this case, and why would IWW mem- bers fire on a patriotic parade? And why did McIntosh soon come to chal- lenge the commonly held view that the IWW instigated the violence on that day? This was no simple case of mur- der. It can be understood only in the broader context of the role the IWW and Bureau of Investigation played in the post–World War I era. Founded in 1905, the IWW was a radi- cal labor union organizing previously unorganized workers (women, Black people, immigrants, miners, loggers, and textile workers) with an anticapi- talist message and militant rhetoric. business community as well as within federal and state government agen- cies.4 The IWW was as much a social movement as a labor union, offering Wobblies (as members were called) hope for a better life, not just a bet- ter job. The Wobblies’ optimistic spirit was evident in their “fearless rhetoric, irreverently funny songs, and bold claims that a new world was possible. Free thinkers, artists, songwriters, and poets filled IWW publications with commentary, cartoons, and opinions on a broad range of social issues.”5 This optimistic energy generated by the IWW enabled it to spread its in-­ fluence well beyond the small num- ber of its dues-paying members. The IWW initially organized on the East Coast and achieved some success in large strikes in Paterson, New Jersey; Mc­Kees Rocks, Pennsylvania; and Law- rence, Massachusetts, despite intransi- gent opposition from employers. Later the IWW moved west to organize in the mines and lumber camps of the Pa- cific Northwest. In April 1917 the U.S. entered World War I. The war let loose powerful an- tilabor, antiradical, and anti-immi- grant passions, starting with President Woodrow Wilson and the Depart- ment of Justice (DOJ) and extending down. Wilson saw the war as an op- portunity to suppress labor unions, socialists, and the IWW in particular. 120  Pacific Northwest Quarterly this position was viewed as anti-Amer- ican and even treasonous in the eyes of many.6 The Federal Espionage Act made opposition to the war a crime, and in 1917 Washington State passed a criminal syndicalism law that made it illegal to advocate “crime, sabotage, violence or other unlawful methods of terrorism as a means of accomplish- ing industrial or political reform.”7 The law also prohibited advocating such a doctrine or belonging to an organiza- tion that teaches or advocates criminal syndicalism. Clearly this language was aimed at the IWW, and many other states passed similar measures. O ppression of the IWW peaked in 1917. That year, mine owners and police in Bisbee, Arizona, forced 1,200 striking IWW miners into rail- road boxcars and deposited them in the middle of the desert. In Butte, Montana, vigilantes lynched the Wob- bly organizer Frank Little for trying to organize a copper mine strike. In the fall of 1917, Bureau of Investigation agents rounded up thousands of Wob- blies across the country, charging them under sedition and criminal syndical- ism laws. Hundreds of Wobbly leaders spent years in prison. This repression of the IWW ignored all constitutional rights and civil liberties.8 Undaunted, only a few months af- ter America’s entry into World War I, the union launched the largest IWW- led strike ever organized in the West. Members demanded an eight-hour workday, higher wages, and improved working conditions such as showers, better food, and bedding. The strike sparked a hostile, sometimes violent, reaction from lumber owners and the DOJ. Lumber was a vital commodity in the war effort, and the strike threat- ened wartime production and national security. Despite federal government intervention into the strike on the side of the employers, many of the workers’ demands were met.9 Still, the repression of the IWW con- tinued. Because many Wobblies were sympathetic to the Russian Revolution of 1918, it was common for U.S. gov- ernment officials to label them as Bol- sheviks and traitors. From 1918 to ear- ly 1919, the police shut down almost every IWW hall in the state of Wash- ington. Many Wobblies were rounded up and put in detention camps.10 The town of Centralia was not immune to these national and international events. Despite the fact that a num- ber of Wobblies had been peacefully raising their families in Centralia for years, business leaders remained fear- ful of the continuing IWW organiz- ing efforts in the surrounding lumber camps and mines. Union organiz- ers had been driven from town twice since 1914. Centralia leaders adopted a policy of arresting all Wobblies found riding the freight trains into town or camping along the railroad tracks. They faced 30 days of labor in a stone quarry.11 In 1917 the Wobblies opened a hall downtown for the first time. Later, someone demolished the IWW sign in front of the hall. The public presence of the IWW hall was seen as a provocation by the business com- munity. On May 18, 1918, participants in a Red Cross parade raided the hall, destroyed furniture, burned records, and drove some Wobblies out of town, telling them never to return. No one raiding the hall was arrested. Ever per- sistent, in 1919 the Wobblies opened their hall in Centralia once more, only two months before the Armistice Day parade.12 The leading national organization be- hind the suppression of the IWW was the Bureau of Investigation, within the U.S. Department of Justice. The bureau was founded in 1908 as the country’s principal national investigative agency; it became better known as the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) in 1935. It initially focused on investigating taxation, the sex trade, and antitrust violations. Later it became engaged in antiradical surveillance and draft enforcement. In general, BOI agents in 1919 were not the highly educated, highly competent men associated with the later FBI. The agency was “some- thing of a dumping ground for well- connected, if not especially well-qual- ified, young constituents of influential congressmen.”13 Inexperienced agents had little formal training, faced lim- ited accountability, and too often were incompetent or unreliable. J. Edgar Hoover joined the Justice Department in 1917, and in the summer of 1919, he was appointed the head of the General Intelligence Division (better known as the Radical Division), whose role was to generate files on radicals and moni- tor their activities. By 1924 he became head of the bureau and started requir- ing all new agents to possess either a legal or accounting degree, which up- graded the overall professionalism of the agency.14 During World War I the bureau’s scope dramatically changed. Its primary role became twofold: first, to prosecute fed- eral crimes of treason, sedition, or the overthrow of the government; and sec- ond, to seek the deportation of aliens who believed in or advocated for the overthrow of the government by vio- lence, or who belonged to an organi- zation whose ideology promoted the overthrow of the government as de- tailed in the federal Immigration Act of 1918. In practice, the bureau attempted to suppress all opponents to the war as anti-American. The bureau carried out surveillance on political dissent- ers, used informants, and assisted in rounding up hundreds of suspected alien radicals for deportation in early 1919. Its investigators collected infor- mation about radicals, particularly the IWW, and turned evidence over to lo- cal prosecutors. In doing so, they were spearheading the DOJ’s effort to put the IWW out of business. The bureau disliked labor unions in general but despised and feared the IWW, viewing it as a criminal organization devoted to sabotage, sedition, and threatening the social order.
