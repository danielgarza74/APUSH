Selection Overview

Grossman shows that wartime labor demand opened new opportunities that drew hundreds of thousands of southern Black migrants north, and that the Chicago Defender helped turn the exodus into a mass movement. He cautions that letters from migrants mattered as much and that the paper did not start the migration.

Blowing the Trumpet: The Chicago Defender and Black Migration During World War I JAMES R. GROSSMAN -LJuring World War I, thousands of black Americans abandoned their southern homes and streamed into northern indus- trial centers. Previously unimaginable opportunities for employment arose when the war simultaneously shut off immigra- tion and stimulated the demand for indus- trial products. To many, the North represented a promised land, and the exodus rivalled emancipation as a liberat- ing experience. Initially catalyzed by labor agents from northern industries, the migration soon generated its own momen- tum. The migratory impulse reached into the most remote hinterlands of the South, as letters, rumors, gossip, and newspapers carried word of the higher wages and bet- ter treatment found in the North. The chance to start life anew - an opportunity unequalled since the Emancipation Procla- mation - beckoned blacks northward. The "Flight out of Egypt" grew to stag- gering proportions as some 400,000 to 500,000 black southerners journeyed north between 1915 and 1919, and nearly one million more followed during the 1920s. From cities, towns, and farms, they poured into any northern city where jobs could be found. New York's black popula- tion grew from 91,709 in 1910 to 152,467 in 1920; Detroit's small black community of 5,741 in 1910 mushroomed to 40,838 within a decade. Within ten years, Abbott was purchasing full-page ads in business directories, and circulation of the Defender topped all other black papers in the city.
