Selection Overview

Breitman, building on Wasserstein's study of Britain, questions accounts that blame mainly Breckinridge Long and antisemitism, and stresses instead how Allied wartime priorities and military considerations shaped Washington's and London's limited response to news of the Holocaust.

Richard Breitman The Allied War Effort and the Jews, 1942-1943 The publication of Bernard Wasserstein's Britain and the Jews of Europe 1939-1945 in 1979 implicitly raised questions about existing studies of American response to the Holocaust. Wasserstein's approach and interpretation of British policies toward European Jews differed appreciably from those of scholars who had studied the Roosevelt administration's corresponding activities. Not content with highlighting the Foreign Office's role in the formulation of refugee policy, Wasserstein probed the views of the Home Office and Colonial Office as well. What he found was a virtually solid bureaucratic bloc that opposed most proposals for aid to Jewish victims of nazi persecution. The single most important consideration behind this opposition was concern that Britain might have to introduce Jewish refugees into Palestine, which would have damaged Britain's political and military position. A pro-Zionist Winston Churchill could make only limited headway against the consensus of the professional civil service and, on occasion, the military. Although Wasserstein found a number of cases of official dislike for Jews, most notably in the case of Foreign Minister Anthony Eden,' British policy did not rest primarily on prejudice. It was, rather, a question of priorities. Studies of the United States have concentrated to varying degrees on the activities of the State Department and the pervasiveness of anti-semitic prejudice there. Although surprisingly well-researched, journalist Arthur Morse's While Six Million Died (1967)2 bore the tone and episodic structure of an expose. Morse's main villain, apart from the nazis themselves, was Assistant Secretary of State Breckinridge Long, whose political and social views were said to have kept Jews from reaching the shores of the United States. He allegedly shared the same anti-semitic assumptions as the nazis, and regarded the flow of Jewish refugees as Hitler's principal weapon against the United States.3 Only the exposure of Long's falsification of cables and statistics cleared the way for American initiatives to save Jewish lives. Both Morse and Feingold dismiss Long's fears of espionage by refugees as the reflection of a distorted view of the world. On the other hand, historian Saul Friedman (No Haven for the Oppressed: United States Policy Toward Jewish Refugees, 1938-1945) specifically denies that Long was anti-semitic, accusing him only of inflexible adherence to the letter of the law. Friedman presents a slightly broader picture of State Department bureaucratic resistance to humanitarian initiatives. The cautious bureaucrats who surrounded Cordell Hull in 1942 reasoned that nothing could be done to help Europe's Jews. Publication of unconfirmed reports (about the Final Solution) could only evoke frustration and hysteria and ultimately detract from the war effort. Instead of pursuing this avenue of analysis, however, Friedman turns polemical. In a curious twist, he accuses the American Jewish community of failing to exert sufficient pressure upon the govern- ment bureaucracy and on FDR. Otherwise, Washington might well have moved earlier to save Jewish lives.4 Such a conclusion is little more than an assertion without detailed examination of the policies of various government agencies. How widespread was the belief in Washington that the war effort precluded significant aid to European Jews? On what evidence was it based? There is little way to judge from the existing literature. The only work remotely comparable to Wasserstein's in the scope of its coverage of government, David Wyman's Paper Walls (1968), extends only to 1941. Analysis of the activities of the State Department, the Office of War Information, and the War Department during 1942-1943 reveals that American officials gave extraordinary emphasis and latitude to the requirements of the war effort. Since European Jews were not universally popular, and since nazi propaganda featured alleged ties between international Jewry and the Allies, Washington saw little to be gained militarily or politically in assisting the most prominent victims of nazi persecution.
