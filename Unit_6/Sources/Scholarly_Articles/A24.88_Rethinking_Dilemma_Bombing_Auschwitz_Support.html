Zohar Segev

EXCERPT Original journal pages 265-266

Selection Overview

Segev argues from the archives of Leon Kubowitzki and the World Jewish Congress that leading U.S. Jewish figures appealed to the administration not to bomb Auschwitz and sought alternative military measures, a meaningful strategic step rather than the marginal one earlier scholarship assumed. He concedes it is hard to measure how far this opposition caused the failure to bomb.

Moreover, one cannot discount the symbolic significance of an effec- tive effort on the part of the Allies to target the Nazi extermination appa- ratus. The fact that the death camp was never bombed, along with the dramatically overwhelming nature of the murder of Hungarian Jewry during the final stages of World War II, has turned this issue into a strik- ing symbol of the in­effec­tive­ness of efforts made by the ­free world and Jewish leaders to rescue the Jews of Eu­rope. In this essay, I ­will pre­sent a new approach and rethinking regarding documents in the archives of American Jewish activists concerned with the issue of the bombing of the camp—­above all, Leon Kubowitzki, who headed the Rescue Department of the World Jewish Congress (WJC). Kubowitzki’s personal archives and the documents of the department that he headed provide valuable information on the rescue efforts made by U.S. Jewry during the Holocaust. Particularly fascinating is a bundle of docu- ments found ­there that address the complex issue of the bombing of Aus- chwitz as part of the effort to rescue Hungarian Jewry in 1944 or, at the very least, to limit the number murdered. ­These documents reveal that leading figures within the U.S. Jewish leadership appealed to the U.S. administration not to bomb Auschwitz and endeavored to find alternative military mea­sures to attack the camp. While existing scholarship on the issue of bombing Auschwitz acknowl- edges this re­sis­tance to bombing, it views it as a marginal initiative rather than a significant strategic step on the part of the U.S. Jewish leadership. Scholars have concluded that the impact of this initiative on the U.S. ad- ministration’s decision not to bomb the camp was negligible. The documents that I pre­sent ­here indicate other­wise. Contrary to the picture available ­until now, this was a meaningful po­liti­cal and strategic step designed to incapacitate the extermination camp without bombing it. From the late 1970s onward, and especially following the appearance of David S. Wyman’s studies, the failure to bomb the Auschwitz extermina- tion camp has been at the forefront of the public and academic debate on the Holocaust. The scholarship touches upon one of the most sensitive issues pertaining to the relations between the Jewish and Zionist organ­izations in the ­free world and the Allies, in the context of their efforts to rescue Eu­ ro­pean Jews. This is a particularly complex topic that connects vari­ous research areas and must address the Allies’ technical capacity to bomb the site, examine the knowledge that the Allies and Jewish and Zionist organ­ izations had at their disposal about Auschwitz, and debate the response of po­liti­cal and military ele­ments to the question of the bombing in 1944.
