Ellis W. Hawley

EXCERPT Original journal pages 247-248

Selection Overview

Hawley argues that Hoover's associational effort to reform the coal industry marked him as no rugged individualist, but voluntary cooperation worked poorly in an atomistic, declining industry, and his efforts helped pave the way for 1930s cartel legislation.

In recent years, as one scholar has noted, the 1920's have be- come a "new historiographical frontier," and as research advances, the older image of the period, as a reactionary and barren interlude between progressivism and the New Deal, is fading into the back- ground. The decade, as a number of historians now see it, was a time of transition, one that served not only as a "seedtime" for the New Deal, but also as an important stage in the development of a managerial economy.1 One of its central features was an emerging system of mature corporations, strengthened by the wartime ar- rangements, garbed in progressive rhetoric, and increasingly con- cerned with stability, planning, and orderly growth - all of which clashed with an older set of rural and entrepreneurial values. And one result was a form of American corporatism, an associational ideology that stressed the new values, urged them upon "backward" or atomistic industries, and blended praise of group action and business-government cooperation with proper respect for the older individualistic symbols. In reality, it appears, Hoover was a transitional figure, an embodiment of the conflict in values, and the leading exponent of a view that made him "the first of the new Presidents" and "the last of the old." " As a humanitarian, engineer, wartime planner, and highly successful administrator, he preached the gospel of efficient production and scientific management, the conquest of poverty through economic growth, technological innovation, and elimination of waste, all of which, he seemed to realize, would in- volve cooperative planning, the systematic use of expert knowledge, and an environment stable enough to attract capital and allow the planners to function. Yet, as an idealist, moral philosopher, and self- made man, he remained wedded to the images of laissez-faire and competitive individualism. Social advance, he kept insisting, was largely a matter of individual achievement; managerial planning, particularly when it involved bureaucratic decisions or legal sanc- tions, was a threat to individual liberty; economic organization, if unchecked and uncontrolled, could lead to tyranny; and although excessive competition could be destructive and wasteful, competi- tion itself was a basic feature of a healthy and progressive economy.4 The need, as Hoover saw it, was for a new synthesis, for an ap- proach that would balance liberty with order, corporatism with individualism, competition with cooperation. It was possible, he felt, to have one's cake and eat it too. And the development that was making this possible was the rapid growth of self-governing associations and associational activities.
