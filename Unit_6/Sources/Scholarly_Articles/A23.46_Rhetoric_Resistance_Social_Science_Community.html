Text of this page (original journal page 599)

Rhetoric and Resistance: Social Science and Community Schools for Navajos in the 1930s Thomas James Federal policy toward the Navajo tribe during the 1930s offers a revealing case in the application of social science and educational theory to minority groups in the United States. Public officials, social scientists, and edu- cational planners worked for several years to create a form of community education that they believed would respect the ways of the tribe while accelerating its members toward political empowerment and economic well-being. For a brief time, a consensus emerged about the value of cultural pluralism as a basis for achieving the goals of public policy. The result was a remarkable interplay of social-scientific reasoning and pro- gressive educational thought, a rhetorical breakthrough perhaps most memorable in retrospect because what was intended as an enlightened intervention soon met, and quickly succumbed to, determined resistance. To see the policy experiment in historical perspective, it is necessary to look more broadly at the migration of social scientists into the corridors of power. An unprecedented transfer of experts and expertise from aca- demia to government took place in the 1930s and 1940s, an infusion so dramatic in its effects that more social scientists worked for the federal government during those years than ever before in the history of the United States. World War II brought many of them to officialdom, but the way had been opened for these "service intellectuals," as Richard S. Kirkendall called them, by a vast expansion of public bureaucracy during the 1930s.' Thomas James is assistant professor of education and public policy, Brown University. Text of this page (original journal page 600)

History of Education Quarterly Social scientists were useful in programs enacted to deal with the dislocations of the age-the collapse of the economy, millions of workers unemployed, "distress migrations," regional development plans, retrain- ing and resettlement projects, then mobilization for war. Backed by a popular mandate to revitalize the nation in the New Deal years, the federal government needed to know not only how to build dams, reclaim depleted land, and resuscitate a battered economy, but also how to move people, reconstruct lives, and reorganize human communities. Social pol- icy could accomplish little without social engineers, the technicians of consent and control who could devise legitimate ways of redirecting traditional loyalties toward emerging patterns of planned development in society. The jobs available for such experts were especially numerous in programs aimed at certain groups on the periphery of the dominant social order: migrant laborers, Native Americans, unemployed workers, un- employable youth, sharecroppers, bankrupt farmers. To follow some typical career paths into the public bureaucracy, men and women with advanced training in the social sciences worked for New Deal programs in the Indian Bureau, or studied communities disrupted by public works projects like the Tennessee Valley Authority, or surveyed local popula- tions whose lives were changed by the Soil Conservation Service of the Department of Agriculture, or analyzed the problems of transients, drop- outs, illiterates, delinquents, and other groups while social planners de- vised policies for training, community education, and resettlement. Once in the service of the government, professional careers tended to grow with the public bureaucracy. As roles and responsibilities di- versified, the social sciences became increasingly differentiated within each discipline. Both the formal organization of work and the informal hierarchies of prestige and influence became more complex, represented by separate divisions and professional associations. The "pure" and "ap- plied" subdisciplines branched apart from one another, though not with- out many crossovers and mutual influences. Sometimes those who ventured forth from academic life into government assignments never turned back, though the metamorphosis produced bureaucrats who continued to echo the language of the social sciences. In some cases, social scientists made a career of moving back and forth under dual sponsorship, frequently using philanthropic support to buffer their careers from the vicissitudes of government programs. Others, disgruntled with public life, eventually returned to the more secluded annoyances of academic politics. This broader perspective helps to account for the expansive outlook of many social scientists who turned to the field of education during the interwar years.
