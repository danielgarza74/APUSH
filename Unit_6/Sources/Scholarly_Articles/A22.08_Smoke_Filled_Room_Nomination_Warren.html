Selection Overview

Bagby argues the legend that a few bosses in a hotel room chose Harding in 1920 is a distortion. The convention was deadlocked and not bossed, and Harding won mainly through his appeal to delegates and the postwar mood.

The 'Smoke Filled Room" and the Nom- ination of Warren C. Harding BY WESLEY M. BAGBY The conference in a "smoke filled room" of the Blackstone Hotel at the 1920 Republican national convention in Chicago has become a symbol of the thwarting of popular will by backstage political plotting. The legend is that in the conference a small group of men, led by George Harvey and Senator Henry Cabot Lodge, discarded the leading candidates, General Leonard Wood, Senator Hiram W. Johnson, and Governor Frank 0. Lowden, and chose as the party's nominee the amiable dark horse, Senator Warren G. Harding. This legend, however, is an oversimplification and a considerable dis- tortion of the role of the "smoke filled room" at the convention. Political conditions in 1920 favored control of the Republican party by organization regulars. Theodore Roosevelt was dead; Progressives were chastened or demoralized; and political tides were running so strongly in favor of the Republicans that there was no need to seek a nominee beyond the ranks of the party organ- ization. In many states organization leaders had agreed to work for an "old fashioned" convention with nothing predetermined, and, neutralizing the presidential primaries by means of "favorite sons" and "uninstructed" delegations, they had built a large bloc of organization delegates responsive to instructions from old guard leaders.' The regulars, however, were challenged in the primaries by Gen- eral Leonard Wood and California's Senator Hiram Johnson. King, who was to have handled Roosevelt's presidential cam- paign in 1920.2 King, no Progressive himself, was a Republican national com- mitteeman from Connecticut, and an associate of the Republican boss of Pennsylvania, Senator Boies Penrose. Under King's direc- tion, a "regular" promotion was launched for General Wood. The promotion, which seemed initially assured of success, had the ap- pearance of making Wood a "front" for the regular party organi- zation.3 The Roosevelt group and Wood's less "regular" following, however, distrusting the secretive King and his old guard associates, persuaded Wood to demand an accounting. When King refused a full accounting of expenditures and political deals, Wood dismissed him as manager. King's comment to a Wood supporter was: "There goes your dear friend's presidency higher than the moon." 4 The break with King brought rupture with the regulars, and the enmity between Wood and organization men was deepened by the vigorous primary campaign of Wood's new manager, wealthy but politically inexperienced William Cooper Procter.
