Selection Overview

Gelfand examines the mystique built by Wilson's disciples, who blamed partisan malice for the treaty's defeat, and points instead to Wilson's centralized management of diplomacy and the flawed assumptions of his internationalism, leaving open which mattered more (historiography).

The Mystique of Wilsonian Statecraft* LAWRENCE E. GELFAND In his unpublished memoir the late economist and historian, Herbert Feis, testified to the attraction he felt for Woodrow Wilson's internationalist credo: Wilsonian ideas of the peace he sought captured my mind and have held it captive since. The League of Nations . . . was the most vital creation of our times. I was angered by the refusal of the Senate to approve American membership.1 Feis's words reflected bitter disappointment shared by many Americans when the U.S. Senate failed to uphold the president in 1919-20. So intimately was Wilson associated with the Versailles treaty and its League covenant that the rejection of the treaty was commonly perceived to be tantamount to a repudi ation of Wilsonian leadership. Readers of John Maynard Keynes's best selling polemic, The Economic Consequences of the Peace, would not easily forget the scathing indictment with its stinging sentence: "There can seldom have been a statesman of the first rank more incompetent than the President in the agilities of the council chamber."2 Though defeated, the Wilsonian ideas and principles were kept alive and actively promoted by a loyal band of apostles. These disciples developed a veritable mystique about Wilsonianism. DIPLOMATIC HISTORY nonparticipation by the United States in the League, the World Court, the International Labor Organization, and, above all, in the new security arrange ments provided for in the peace settlement almost certainly would constitute a giant step backward, a reversion to a posture of political isolationism. By no means concentrated in the Eastern establishment, Wilsonian internationalists could be found in all parts of the country, in urban centers and on college-university campuses. As is common with promoters of special causes, particularly causes recently defeated in a hard-fought controversy, the advocates, carrying their brand of internationalist evangelism, went forth to speak before civic, educational, and religious groups.4 In a real sense, the Wilsonian doctrines provided a paradigm for deliberations over international questions during the interwar decades. Programs of the Council on World Affairs in Cleveland, the New York Council on Foreign Relations, and articles in the newly established Foreign Affairs journal reflect the extensive reliance on Wilsonian doctrines.5 Political scientist James W. Garner, writing in the late 1920s, called attention to still another indicator of the vitality of the League issue: A feature of the fight upon the League, which future historians will find hard to understand, was a disposition in many quarters towards intolerance of free discussion of its objects and purposes. There was a time when the mere mention of its name excited passions and violent partisanship, when university professors were cautioned against discussing it before their classes and when college buildings were closed to outside speakers where his name is revered and find this vitriolic feeling at home." See Fosdick to his wife, 24 October 1919, in R. B. Fosdick, Letters on the League ofNations (Princeton, 1966), p. 45. Not only did Wilsonians attribute such motives as are indicated here to the American opponents of the Versailles treaty and to those who rejected the idea of U.S. membership in the League of Nations, but they also had seemingly reliable historical treatises on which to support their views after publication of Denna F. Fleming, The Treaty Veto of the American Senate (New York, 1930); and W. S.
