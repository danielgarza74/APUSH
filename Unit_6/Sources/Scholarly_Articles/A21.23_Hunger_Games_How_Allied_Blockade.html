Economic History Review, 68, 2 (2015), pp. 600-631 Hunger games: or how the Allied blockade in the First World War deprived German children of nutrition, and Allied food aid subsequently saved them 1 By MARY ELISABETH COX* At the onset of the First World War, Germany was subject to a shipping embargo by the Allied forces. Ostensibly military in nature, the blockade prevented not only armaments but also food and fertilizers from entering Germany The impact of that blockade on civilian populations has been debated ever since. Germans protested that the Allies had wielded hunger as a weapon against women and children with devas- tating results, a claim that was hotly denied by the Allies. The impact of what the Germans termed the Hungerblockade on childhood nutrition can now be assessed using a newly discovered dataset based on heights and weights of nearly 600,000 German schoolchildren measured between 1914 and 1924. Statistical analysis reveals a grim truth: German children suffered severe malnutrition due to the blockade. Social class impacted risk of deprivation, with working-class children suffering the most. Surprisingly, they were the quickest to recover after the war. Their rescue was fuelled by massive food aid organized by the former enemies of Germany, and delivered cooperatively with both government and civil society. The ability of former belligerents to work together after an exceptionally bitter war to feed impoverished children may hold hope for the future. The Blockade [was] imposed upon us with the avowed purpose not only of cutting off supplies for the army, but of inflicting bodily and vital harm on Germany's civilian population, women, children, old people and all those unfit for military service. At the advent of the War, Britain rapidly imposed a naval blockade to prevent all imports from entering Germany - a blockade that lasted longer than the war itself. Considered by Britain to be a necessary military action, the naval blockade was deemed by Germans to be an act of war against civilian populations, specifically targeting children and other vul- nerable non-combatants. It is perhaps not surprising that National Socialists later seized on the plight of German children during the Hungerblockade as propaganda to justify their military assault on their old enemies. German military excesses could be excused, they claimed, since the British had already proven their inhu- manity by using hunger as a weapon against women and children.4 British apolo- gists responded that the reports of civilian starvation were grossly exaggerated.5 No one contests that Germany was deeply vulnerable to the ban on imports. 6Yet debates about the impact of the blockade on German civilians have erupted sporadically for a century, with metrics playing a key role. German statistics were deemed to be suspect. Critics claimed that the statistics were inflated. In the resultant ruckus it has been impossible even to reach agreement on German civilian death tolls during the blockade.7 Personal diaries and newspaper articles chronicling wartime hunger have been dismissed by some as anecdotal and unrep- resentative of the common German experience, particularly since diaries were a province of elites rather than average citizens.8 While such ethnographic evidence should play a role in the debate, the inherent subjectivity of these accounts is difficult to overcome. The jury remains out on this vitally important question: did the British blockade of Germany inflict severe nutritional deprivation on German children? If so, did this deprivation end with the end of war?
