Emily Colborn-Roxworthy

EXCERPT Original journal pages 189-190

Selection Overview

Colborn-Roxworthy reads theatre and performance at Manzanar as an ambivalent record: internees staged assimilationist, all-American shows for administrators and outsiders, yet some, like the students' quiet protest over an operetta, signaled limits on that cooperation.

"Manzanar, the eyes of the world are upon you"1: Performance and Archival Ambivalence at a Japanese American Internment Camp Emily Colborn-Roxworthy Applause, distant but ongoing, is the first sound that greets visitors to the Interpre tive Center at Manzanar National Park in Inyo County, California. In National Park Service (NPS) lingo, the operation of such interpretive centers facilitates "the process of helping each park visitor find an opportunity to personally connect with a place."2 Best known on a national level as the desert grounds of the Manzanar War Relocation Center in which the US government interned Japanese Americans behind barbed wire manned by eight machine-gun-equipped guard towers during World War II, the NPS has been welcoming tourists to this historic site since 2004 through the "adaptively restored" auditorium constructed by internees to serve as their theatre and sometimes gymnasium in 1944-19453 (fig. 1). Today, once visitors emerge from the air-conditioned Manzanar gift shop that serves as welcome respite from California's arid Owens Valley Emily Colborn-Roxworthy is an assistant professor on the PhD faculty in the Department of Theatre and Dance at the University of California, San Diego. Her research articles have been published in TDR, Theatre Research International, and The Journal of Dramatic Theory and Criticism. I would like to thank my late dissertation chair, Dwight Conquergood, for the passion with which he anticipated this research on camp performance. I am saddened that Dwight did not live long enough to see it come to fruition in this essay, but hopeful that I am continuing some small part of his large legacy by pursuing such work. His voice reverberates in my head as I seek to understand the performative impulse in the unjust and highly ambivalent context of the Japanese American in ternment camps. Theatre Journal's two anonymous readers also provided crucial voices on this work, metaphorically sitting on each of my shoulders as I revised this essay for publication. My research assistant Tomoyuki Sasaki aided my failing Japanese-language skills in translating the Japanese sec tion of the Manzanar Free Press, reflected more fully in the chapter-length version of this research for my book, The Theatre of Japanese American Trauma: Racial Performativity in World War II (University of Hawai'i Press, forthcoming).
