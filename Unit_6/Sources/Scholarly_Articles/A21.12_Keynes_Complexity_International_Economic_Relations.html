Selection Overview

Carabelli and Cedrini argue that Keynes's Economic Consequences of the Peace reflects his method for handling complexity (organic interdependence, irreducible dilemmas, need for public assistance), and read his critique of Versailles and its punitive reparations as ruinous to Germany and Europe. The article is mainly about Keynes's method and its continuity with Bretton Woods (history of economic thought).

Cedrini Abstract: In the attempt to deepen the understanding of Keynes' thought as an international macroeconomist, we explore the hypothesis of consistency between his general methodological approach to the economic material and his way of reasoning about international economic relations. As a first step toward this direction, we investigate the methodology of The Economic Consequences of the Peace and find that it reflects Keynes' attempt to cope with the attributes of the complexity characterizing the European settlement for the post-war period, e.g., 1) organic interdependence among variables at play, 2) irreducible dilemmas and situations of conflict, as well as 3) the need for external, public assistance to overcome the impasse and promote a "shared responsibilities" approach to the imbalances. Striking similarities appearing with the method of Keynes' economic diplomacy in the 1940s are shown to substantiate the current rediscovery of his plans for Bretton Woods. Keynes' reform schemes in the 1940s deserve the attention they are commanding at present: he was a thinker of global economic integration (see Dimand 2006), and an economist that all policymakers should have in mind in their search for a new order consciously designed to overcome the shortcomings produced by our world's unintended evolution toward an instable, inequitable and expensive "Bretton Woods 2" system. However, revisiting Keynes means approaching a well-defined vision of international economic relations, as shown by David Vines' review of Robert Skidelsky's Fighting for Britain (2000); "a vision of global capitalism as an inter-national system" (Vines 2003, 357), deriving from "an extraordinarily clear understanding of how pieces of global economy interact, driven by the policies of autonomous nations, in an only partly coherent manner" (339). Not only the content but also, and above all, the "focus and method" (358) of Keynes' "international macroeconomics," which was anti-utilitarian and fully aware of the impossibility of understanding international relations solely by means of rational action theory, should consequently be made the object of the rediscovery. Economists and historians are therefore called upon to find traces of methodological coherence between Keynes' general conception and practice of economics, inspiring his peculiar treatment of the "economic material," and his work as an international economist. This would provide opportunity for highlighting the substantial continuity of Keynes' thinking in the different episodes of his economic diplomacy. A reassessment is required, in particular, of the spirit and ultimate aims of his economic diplomacy against the well-established tradition that sees him as a British economist essentially engaged, in the 1940s, in the attempt to save his country from financial dependence of the United States - the essence of Skidelsky's "fighting for Britain" view of Keynes' work after 1937 - rather than the international architect of a sounder global system. As a first step in this direction, this article investigates the vision Keynes exposed in his first major work in international economics, The Economic Consequences of the Peace (ECP). We explore the hypothesis of consistency between Keynes' way of dealing with the economic material and the method underlying his approach to international economic relations in the aftermath of World War I. We show that both Keynes' criticisms of the Treaty of Versailles and the multilateral approach he envisaged for the settlement of war-induced international imbalances were shaped by the use of a method reflecting the characteristics of the complex material on which he had to investigate.
