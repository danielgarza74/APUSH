Selection Overview

Biskupski shows that American planners treated Wilson's Polish point as a vague moral aim, bounded by a conservative wish to preserve the old balance of power and Russia's integrity. Self-determination in practice was limited and misunderstood by the Poles.

BISKUPSKI thirteenth of Woodrow Wilson's famous Fourteen Points signified United States support for the cause of Polish indepen- dence, an expression of goodwill which had yet to be translated into policy. During the last year of the First World War, Americans developed a vision of Poland as a state historically rootless, economically weak, and strategically defenceless, with a large expatriate population. To define the Thirteenth Point was to disclose the president's 'congenital inability to develop applications of his general principles'.1 Indeed, it required an awkward, ifnot impossible, compromise between Wilson's sincere, yet vague, notion of an independent Polish state on the one hand, and the intractable realities of European geography, history, and ethnography on the other. The United States did not desire the radical restructuring of the European map; nor did she wish either drastically to weaken Germany or seriously diminish Russia. The future shape of Poland thus became a problem whose solution was largely foreordained. For the Poles, the question of Polish independence was axiomatically territorial; US endorsement of Polish independence logically included support for sufficient territory to satisfy history and guarantee survival. This made the settlement of Russian and German territorial claims a consequence of decisions regarding Poland, the reverse of the US ap- proach. 250 M.B. Biskupski The question of Polish independence was put back on the international agenda by the outbreak of the First World War. The Russians were the first of the belligerents to attempt to win Polish sympathies by offering political concessions. In a manifesto of 14 August 19 14, the Russian commander-in-chief, Nikolai Nikolaevich, promised the Poles national reunification in exchange for their support of the Russian effort. Where- as the restricted provisions of the manifesto generated little enthusiasm among the Poles, it did send a signal that Poland would not be ignored. When 19 15 brought most of Poland under the joint occupation of the Central Powers, the Germans, after considering a number of options for long-term advantage in eastern Europe, announced the creation of a kingdom of Poland by the Two Emperors Manifesto of 5 November 1916. Although an effort to gain badly needed Polish manpower after the blood-letting of Verdun and the Somme, it 'internationalized' the Polish Question and prompted the other powers to endorse some form of Polish independence at the end of the war. In the United States, Poland became an increasingly important public issue, as both the Wilson administration and broad segments of the public were drawn into what proved a vain effort lasting until the autumn of 19 16 to arrange large-scale relief - similar to the Commission and Jacek R.
