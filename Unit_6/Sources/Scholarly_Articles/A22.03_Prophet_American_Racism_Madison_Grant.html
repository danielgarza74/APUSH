Selection Overview

Alexander argues that Madison Grant, an upper-class New York naturalist, gave American racial nativism a pseudo-scientific Nordic rationale that shaped the exclusiveness of 1920s thinking, while cautioning that his actual influence on the Klan and the Johnson-Reed Act cannot be determined and that racial science was one strand of a wider nativist pattern.

By CHARLES C. ALEXANDER Prophet of American Racism: Madison Grant and the Nordic Myth pRACTICAL APPLICATION of the philosophy of racism by the leaders of Nazi Germany largely destroyed the intellectual respectability of race-thinking and its chief product, racial nativism, in the United States. While the concept of racial superiority continues to be a problem in American society, writers purporting to be "racial scientists" gain small credence in literate circles today. In the early years of the twenti- eth century, however, many educated Americans accepted and propa- gated theories of race, thus both accompanying and influencing the growth of racial nativism in the United States. Racism supposedly reached its fruition in this country during the 1920's with the passage of immigration restriction legislation and the disruptive activites of the Ku Klux Klan. Among American intellectuals who imbibed heavily from the fountain of European race-thinking, added their own touches, and presented racism to the public, probably none is more important yet more neglected than Madison Grant of New York City. Although often mentioned, Grant's ideas have never received full delineation. Some forty years of gradual moving away from the idea of an American melting pot culminated in the racial philosophy of Grant, who did much to send racism toward the exclusiveness and aggressiveness that char- acterized American society in the 'twenties. Madison Grant typified the kind of secure, upper-class, Eastern in- tellectual who embraced racial nativism in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The balding Grant was of medium height and the possessor of dark eyes and a perfect gray mustache. Having never mar- ried, he lived alone in a stately Fifth Avenue apartment, where he formulated his disputatious theories of human history. He was born in New York City in 1865. His father was Gabriel Grant, a rather wealthy doctor who had served as a major and division surgeon during the Civil War. A combination of wealth and distinguished background quali- fied the Grant family for inclusion in the inner circle of New York so- cialites; Madison was quite proud of the fact that since the Colonial period every member of his family had been born within fifty miles of the New York City Hall. Grant received his early education in private schools, then entered Yale University, where he obtained a B.A. degree in 1887. Since his father left an ample inheritance, Grant never put his legal training to use, but instead turned to the study of zoology, genealogy, and later anthropology. Large animals had a par- ticular attraction for him, and he wrote scientific papers on such crea- tures as the moose, deer, and bison. In Alaska he discovered a unique form of caribou, which was named after him.' Grant sometimes put aside his work as a naturalist to dabble in re- form activity. In 1894 he and his brother De Forest worked for reform mayoralty candidate William Strong and the eradication of New York bossism. He was a member of the Bronx Parkway Commission from 1901 to 1925, and shared credit for the construction of parks and bridges along the notable highway. His enthusiasm for conserving wildlife and nature led to years of activity in organizations like the American Bison Society, the American Geological Society, the Save the Redwoods League, and the Society for the Preservation of Fauna in the British Empire. He was a trustee of the American Museum of Natural History, a vice-president of the National Institute of the Social Sciences, and a founder and presi- dent of the famous New York Zoological Society.2 One writer has called Grant "a man of forceful character and marked executive ability, intensely loyal and possessing a deep sense of hu- mor." 3 Perhaps this is an accurate description. He was also quite aristo- cratic in both thought and action. Grant was always reluctant to accept the incursion of average citizens into his stilted environs. He became furious when someone suggested that the practice of checking cameras before entrance into the New York Zoo be abandoned. As a director of the zoo he battled to the end to keep the cameras out. On his deathbed he sent for the secretary of the New York Zoological Society and was barely able to whisper: "Are we winning?" He always kept at arm's length "the very people to whom the society looked for its home and more than half its annual appropriations." 4 Grant's aristocratic sentiments furnished an obvious foundation for the acceptance of American nativism, which he embraced whole- heartedly in the early twentieth century. Always an inveterate "joiner," he became affiliated with such groups as the American Defense Society, formed 1916 to promote military preparedness, and the New York branch of the General Society of Colonial Wars, a genealogical and patriotic organization of 1893 vintage. He joined the cause of the Immigration Restriction League and was elected to one of the League's several vice- presidencies in 1909.
