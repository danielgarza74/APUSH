Selection Overview

McKechney (historiography) surveys the Revisionist case (Beard, Tansill, Morgenstern) that Roosevelt maneuvered the nation into war through the Pacific, and concludes the Internationalists (Langer and Gleason, Feis, Butow) have the better of the argument. In his view Roosevelt did want war with Germany but was held back by isolationist opinion, and the Pearl Harbor attack was not of his making but the work of Japanese militarists.

This is what I have dreamed of, aimed at, and worked for, and now it has come to pass.3 Provocative as these speeches by the two British Prime Ministers may be to American ears, no one has yet accused England of engineering the Pearl Harbor debacle. Some equally interesting statements by less prominent members of the London and Washington governments, however, have no doubt inspired a rash of historians of the so-called Revisionist school (those his- torians who revise or refute the traditional interpretation of historical events) to point an accusing finger at the American President and his Secretary of State. Speaking before the American Chamber of Commerce in London, June 20, 1944, Captain Oliver Lyttleton, Production Minister in Churchill's cabinet, asserted: "America provoked Japan to such an ex- tent that the Japanese were forced to attack Pearl Harbor. It is a travesty on history ever to say that America was forced into war."4 Then, there is the often quoted entry Roosevelt's Secretary of War made in his Diary for November 25, 1941: Then at 12 o'clock we (General Marshall and I) went to the White House, where we were until nearly half past one. At the meeting were Hull, Knox, Marshall, Stark, and myself. There the President, instead of bringing up the Victory Parade, brought up entirely the relations with the Japanese. He brought up the event that we were likely to be attacked perhaps (as soon as) next Monday, for the Japanese are notorious for making an attack without warning, and the question was what we should do.
