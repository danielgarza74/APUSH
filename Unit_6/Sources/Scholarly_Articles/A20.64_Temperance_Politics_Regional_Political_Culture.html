Selection Overview

Pegram uses the Anti-Saloon League's failure in Maryland to show that the group succeeded in the South when it fit regional political culture, including Democratic loyalty and white supremacy. He ties southern prohibition to a rising middle-class, state-centered Progressivism.

Temperance Politics and Regional Political Culture: The Anti-Saloon League in Maryland and the South, 1907-1915 By THOMAS R. PEGRAM IN 1907 THE STRUGGLING ANTI-SALOON LEAGUE (ASL) OF MARYLAND welcomed William H. Anderson, its vigorous new superintendent. An- derson had achieved notoriety in his native Illinois as the chief archi- tect of an aggressive temperance lobbying strategy that produced a landmark local option law. Over the next three Maryland legislative sessions, Anderson pursued the same strategy that had led to success in the Midwest: organizing the Protestant clergy for the anti-saloon cause; insisting that supporters ignore party lines and cast ballots only for legislators who supported the ASL's local option bills; and con- ducting a loud and very public lobbying effort, hounding lawmakers, pillorying opponents in the press, and sending delegations of temper- ance advocates to the statehouse. Similar efforts dried up most states, eventually resulting in national prohibition, and established the ASL, in the words of one scholar, as "the prototype of the political pressure group" in modem America.1 But in Maryland these actions proved fruitless. They reflect differences in regional political culture that help il- luminate the distinctive features of southern prohibitionism and its close relationship to Progressive reform and expanded government re- sponsibilities in the South. Maryland in the early years of the twenti- eth century was situated on two critical borders. The first separated "national" political culture from the peculiar emphasis on race and fi- delity to the Democratic party that characterized the southern political system. The commitment of the Maryland Democratic party to dis- franchisement, the appearance of strict racial segregation in the state early in the twentieth century, and the explicit self-identification of many white Marylanders as southerners justify placing Maryland on the periphery of southern political culture during the Progressive Era. On the other hand, the city of Baltimore clearly fit into the social and political patterns of northern society. Throughout the Progressive Era, Baltimore's population remained at least 200,000 greater than that of New Orleans, the South's largest city. Nor could any southern city ex- cept New Orleans match Baltimore's influential foreign-stock com- munities or its authority in state politics. Such factors made Maryland an amalgam of political styles that, along with those of Kentucky, Ok- lahoma, Texas, Louisiana, and Florida, represented the political com- plexity that characterized the outskirts of the South.3 2 William H.
