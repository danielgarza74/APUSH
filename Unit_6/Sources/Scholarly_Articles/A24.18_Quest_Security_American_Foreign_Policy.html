Selection Overview

The Quest for Security: American Foreign Policy and International Control of Atomic Energy, 1942-1946 BARTON J. BERNSTEIN M OST historians of the Cold War acknowledge that the American nuclear monopoly and the disputes over international control of atomic energy contributed to the breakdown of Soviet-American relations. Beyond this limited consensus, however, there is considerable disagreement about America's diplomatic uses of the bomb, about the reasons for and the gener- osity of America's proposal for international control of atomic energy, and about the reasons for and the reasonableness of Russia's rejection of this proposal. These subjects merit close scrutiny and explain much about the form that the Cold War took. But any analysis of these problems, contrary to the practices of some his- torians, must begin in the Roosevelt, not the Truman, years. Franklin D. Roosevelt made the decisions on the international relations of atomic energy that shaped the limits within which Harry S. Truman could comfortably operate. In turn, then, any analysis of the Cold War and of the sources of Soviet-American mistrust must also take into account, at least, the actions of the Roosevelt administration and Roosevelt's decisions to establish a nu- clear alliance with Great Britain and to exclude the Soviet Union both from that alliance and from any knowledge of the Manhattan Project. Roosevelt understood that the bomb could be a military counterweight to Soviet armies and that it could help America gain its diplomatic objectives. Roosevelt's decisions loosely defined the limits of comfortable action for his successor, but the Truman administration was not confronted with a predetermined choice. The administration delayed an approach to the Soviets, followed strategies that Roosevelt's nuclear policy allowed, and then belatedly ad- vanced a plan for international control of atomic energy. That plan, widely heralded as magnanimous, was unsatisfactory to the Russians because it protected the American nuclear monopoly and threat- ened Soviet security and industrial development. The Soviet plan, in turn, was unacceptable to the United States because it called for destroying atomic bombs and sharing information while delaying inspection until later. Di- vided by deep mistrust, both nations were unprepared to make the conces- sions necessary for agreement and to risk bestowing advantages on the other great power. America's research on the atomic bomb, initiated to overcome the Ger- mans, was concealed from the Soviets. Major General Leslie Groves, di- rector of the atomic bomb project, later bluntly explained the rationale: "Russia was our enemy and . . . the project was conducted on that basis." His own statement, while made at the height of the Cold War, expresses the concerns of American policy makers during World War II. The Grand Alliance was always uneasy, and American leaders did not regard the Soviets with trust. More than a quarter century of suspicion could not be erased by an alliance conceived in necessity and strained by rival hopes and plans for the postwar world.1 Even relations with Britain were strained periodically by differences on the sharing of information on atomic energy. A loosely phrased agreement, reached by Roosevelt and Prime Minister Winston Churchill in June 1942, fell apart in the autumn when the British demanded to participate in the construction and operation of nuclear facilities.
