Selection Overview

Craig argues that Williams, Hofstadter, Stourzh, and Kennedy wrongly read Beard's postwar anti-Roosevelt books as the culmination of his 1930s anti-interventionism. In his view the 1930s Beard offered a cogent economic critique of elite-driven intervention, which he abandoned after 1941 when 'free security' collapsed, leaving a narrow focus on Roosevelt's abuse of power (historiography).

CAMPBELL CRAIG The Not-So-Strange Career o Charles Beard Charles Beard's legacy as a founder of the economic approach to the history of American foreign relations was undermined by his last work, in particular by the two strange books he wrote after World War II about Franklin Roosevelt and the coming of the war. Two historians who disagreed about many things, William Appleman Williams and Richard Hofstadter, appear to have been in agreement about Beard: during the 1930s, they concur, Beard allowed his thoroughgoing economic approach to American history and politics to be subsumed by a stubborn, nativistic nationalism, which inspired him to oppose American intervention all the way until Pearl Harbor, and culminated finally in the obsessive anti-Rooseveltism of his last years.1 Williams thus describes Beard's later work as "a terribly expensive flight from reality," while Hofstadter's account of Beard's last years reads like a Greek tragedy.2 This interpretation of Beard's demise is more comprehensively asserted by Gerald Stourzh and Thomas Kennedy, whose specialized treatments of Beard's views on foreign policy emphasize his growing obsession with FDR, his increasingly strident nationalism, and a long history of irrationality.' In explain ing Beard's last books, both Stourzh and Kennedy argue at length what Hofstadter suggests and Williams implies: Beard's one-man crusade against Roosevelt in the late 1940s was the end product of his populist anti-interventionism of the mid- and late 1930s.
