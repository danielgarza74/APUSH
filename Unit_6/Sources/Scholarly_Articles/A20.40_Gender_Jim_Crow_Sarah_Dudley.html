Gender and Jim Crow: Sarah Dudley Pettey's Vision of the New South Glenda Elizabeth Gilmore IN THE SUMMER of 1896, when Sarah Dudley Pettey picked up her pen to begin her "Woman's Column" in the A.M.E. Zion newspaper the Star of Zion, she promised readers that they could expect much more from her than recipes, gossip, and church news. She announced that she would not be "narrowed down to the limited confines of womanhood."1 Dudley Pettey believed that she stood on the threshold of a new world, a world in which women would soon be able to enter "every door of usefulness now ajar to the sterner sex only."2 Then, she clearly stated her position on racial and gender equality: "Some would say that woman is good in her place. This reminds me of what some white people say of the Negro: That 'he is good in his place;' The Negro won't accept it and neither should woman."3 Sarah Dudley Pettey was an African-American feminist who lived in New Bern, North Carolina, in the 1890s. Before the triumph of Jim Crow and disfranchisement in 1900, she thought that North Carolina's African Americans had advanced remarkably in a single generation and that race relations continued to improve in the state. As a member of an optimistic and aspiring middle class, Dudley Pettey predicted that whites would recognize black accomplishments and that class sympathies could grow across racial lines. Ms. Gilmore is a doctoral candidate at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. The Star of Zion usually did not number its pages. Sarah Dudley Pettey used a hyphen between her maiden name and her married name when she began her column. Although she stopped using the hyphen, she continued to use her maiden name in conjunction with her married name. That has been done here, without a hyphen, both to honor her own expression and to distinguish her voice from that of her husband, Bishop Charles Calvin Pettey, to whom the author refers as Pettey. 2 Star of Zion, October 21, 1897. Sarah Dudley Pettey's writing style and metaphors recall the work of Frances Ellen Watkins Harper, an African-American temperance leader and lecturer who traveled in the South and wrote for national publications. For example, Harper wrote, "To-day women hold in their hands influence and opportunity, and with these they have already opened doors which have been closed to others." 262 Glenda Elizabeth Gilmore North Carolina's black middle class grew rapidly in the state's towns and cities during the last two decades of the nineteenth century. In addition to being artisans and shopkeepers, African-American men followed their professional training as teachers, pharmacists, lawyers, and doctors.4 Black women became teachers, nurses, clubwomen, and missionaries. African Americans rapidly increased their share of the state's urban real estate after 1880.6 As they moved forward economically, they adopted strategies for success that historian Stuart Blumin has associated with middle-class formation.6 Black men and women formed voluntary reform associations, preached temperance, deferred gratification, and lauded reason over passion. Yet in the community at large they were not free to exercise class prerogatives because of racial restrictions. 4 In the postbellum South, black artisans and craftsmen lost ground until 1890, but from then until 1920 they reversed the trend and retained or gained in their market share of most crafts. Black participation in industry increased in the late nineteenth century because of an expansion of the industries that traditionally employed blacks: sawmills, railroads, and mining. See Sterling D. Spero and Abram L. Harris, Black Worker: The Negro and the Labor Movement (New York: Columbia University Press, 1931), table 9, p. 160, and C. Vann Woodward, Origins of the New South, 1877-1913 (Baton Rouge: Louisiana State University Press, 1971), 360, hereinafter cited as Woodward, Origins of the New South. In Arkansas, John William Graves finds a small but thriving group of artisans in the 1890s, along with shopkeepers and professionals. See John William Graves, "Jim Crow in Arkansas: A Reconsideration of Urban Race Relations in the Post-Reconstruction South," Journal of Southern History, LV (August, 1989), 427, hereinafter cited as Graves, "Jim Crow in Arkansas." For black professionals in North Carolina, see Todd L. Among blacks, there were many women teachers, no women lawyers, and no women pharmacists in the state during the 1890s. There were, however, two women physicians, L. Hughes Brown, a college classmate of Sarah Dudley Pettey, and Lucy Bragg Anthony. 5 Robert C. Studies of middle-class formation have generally focused on the North and on the experience of whites. Middle-class formation began much later in the South than it did in the North, in the 1880s and 1890s rather than in the 1830s and 1840s. Delayed industrialization and the region's general poverty produced a northern-controlled and stunted economy for both whites and blacks. White supremacy had an even greater impact on class formation and altered success strategies for both races in the South. In an important example of this difference, the development of middle-class gender roles in both races varied from the northern model because women's public conduct in a racially charged public sphere took on variations specific to the southern situation. The discussion of class formation is informed by the following works: Stuart M. Blumin, The Emergence of the Middle Class: Social Experience in the American City, 1760-1900 (New York: Cambridge University Press, 1989); Mary P. Ryan, Cradle of the Middle Class: The Family in Oneida County, New York, 1790-1865 (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1981); Paul E. Johnson, A Shopkeeper's Millennium: Society and Revivals in Rochester, New York, 1815-1837 (New York: Hill and Wang, 1978); and John Gilkeson, Jr., Middle Class Providence, 18H0-191+0 (Princeton: Princeton University Press, 1986).
