BINOY KAMPMARK "No Peace with the Hohenzollerns": American Attitudes on Political Legitimacy towards Hohenzollern Germany, 1917-1918* Although the issues of the war look complex, they are in fact simple, and our own experience in attempting to deal with the German Government shows how futile the acceptance of any pledge from it would be. —Woodrow Wilson1 As World War I was drawing into its final stages, the uncompromising diplo macy of the Allies towards the Central Powers hardened. The position of the United States, most notably towards Imperial Germany, highlighted Prussian militarism and autocracy. These features of the German political system, it was said, had laid the foundations for the war.2 The Wilson administration and various influential officials in the United States thereby sought to encourage a political reshaping of Germany. This would be done through a vigorous diplo matic strategy targeting the German political system itself. The Wilson admin istration thereby refused to negotiate with the political institutions they regarded as responsible for the conflict. This invalidating approach towards the German government revealed a value-driven view of international affairs that has characterized stages of U.S. diplomatic history in the twentieth century.3 This article examines the position on political legitimacy taken by Wilson towards Imperial Germany in the final two years of the Great War. It histori cizes the policy by charting the development of various approaches in interna tional diplomatic currents. It argues that the constitutional criteria applied by Wilson to Imperial Germany (that the government be a democratically elected, representative one), while radical in its own right, had a firm historical footing in existing U.S. diplomatic practice, not least towards South America. *The author thanks the two anonymous reviewers for highly instructive comments on a previous draft of this article; Dr. John A. It points to a concept of political legitimacy that became, in time, a cornerstone of U.S. foreign policy. The fact that this principle has often resulted in Washington's denial of sovereignty and national legitimacy vis-à-vis other states has not prevented this principle from being asserted. In order to be a legitimate member of the international family of nations, the foreign govern ment, according to this strand of U.S. diplomatic practice, must embody the popular sovereign will of the people. The will of the people cannot, it follows, be embodied in the will of a supreme, unelected leader, notwithstanding their consent towards that end. Washington's reluctance to recognize the governments of supposedly "failed" states demonstrates a modern trend in this argument.
