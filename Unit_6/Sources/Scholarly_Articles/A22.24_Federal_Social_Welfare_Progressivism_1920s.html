Federal Social Welfare Progressivism in the 1920s Joseph B. Chepaitis University of New Haven During an era long considered conservative, the Sheppard-Towner Mater- nity and Infancy Act of 1921 emerges as the first federal measure passed under the successful prodding of the recently enfranchised women. Its demise coincides with and explains the weakened clout of the female voters by the end of the 1920s. The legal challenge of the opponents to the law in two Supreme Court cases offered the first test cases for federal grants-in-aid; the Court upheld the grant program. The debate throughout the history of the Act bared many of the dominant concerns of the 1920s. Although it lasted only seven years, it served as a model for future federal grants-in-aid. No longer is it tenable to maintain that the 1920s were a political interim between the Progressive Era and the New Deal. Clarke A. Chambers, Arthur Link, Roy Lubove, and Burl Noggle attest to the revisionist findings unearthed by scholars during the past two decades (8, 22, 23, 31). A continuum is evident through a series of progressive federal laws and debate in the fields of agriculture, labor, and public utilities.1 The 1920s also saw the embryonic development of federal social welfare legislation. Proposals included development of old age pensions; care of handicapped and mentally retarded children; eradication of illiteracy; and development of workmen's compensa- tion, a bureau of unemployment compensation, and federal depart- ments of health, education, and welfare. The only new social welfare bill signed during the Republican 1920s was the Sheppard-Towner Maternity and Infancy Act of 1921; renewed in 1927, it became a pioneer and a model for subsequent federal grants-in-aid (10:145). The Sheppard-Towner Act was the lone exception to the federal refusal to embrace responsibility for social welfare until the New Deal. The law functioned as one of the tenuous links between two reform periods; it was a product of the fading Progressive Era and a precursor of the New Deal. Questions immediately arise. Was it merely the support of a Republican president that passed this pioneer bill? Why was it signed fourteen years before any other similar legislation? What segments of the public lobbied for its passage, and which opposed it? While most progressives lost power during the 1920s, the social justice group, particularly organized women, continued to be politi- cally potent. Over the hotly contested ground of this law battles were fought between advocates of federal responsibility and those of states' rights; between anti-Communist alarmists and social welfare reformers; between champions of fiscal responsibility and those of national social responsibility; and between medical prac- tioners and nonmedical laymen, as well as a skirmish between the sexes. The controversies surrounding this legislation from 1918 until 1929 revealed many pertinent facets of the New Era. Early Attempts at Maternal and Infant Care The impetus for maternal and infant protection developed gradually but steadily, first through municipal agencies and private organiza- tions, then on the state level, and finally within the federal govern- ment. From the infant milk stations in 1893 through subsequent school health programs, nursing services (especially in New York City and Boston), and municipal bureaus of child hygiene, the movement expanded to encompass prenatal care. The American Association for the Study and Prevention of Infant Mortality, a private national group (later to join the Child Health Organization of America and finally become the American Child Health Associa- tion), was founded in 1909. As statistics showing the results of this work became available, the pressure of private groups led to further municipal reform, and then to state action. Between 1912 and 1930, thirty-two state divisions or bureaus of child hygiene were organized (60:269-70; 61:235-36).
