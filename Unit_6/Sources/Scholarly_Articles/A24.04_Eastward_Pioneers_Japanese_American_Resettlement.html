Eastward Pioneers: Japanese American Resettlement during World War II and the Contested Meaning of Exile and Incarceration ALLAN W.AUSTIN IN 1982-1983, THE COMMISSION on Wartime Relocation and In ternment of Civilians [CWRIC] issued Personal Justice Denied, its history of the exile and incarceration of Japanese Americans during World War II. The report included a chapter that examined the "high psychic price" paid by Japanese Americans [or Nikkei] for their wartime experiences. Second generation Japanese Americans [or Nisei], the CWRIC suggested, had coped with their mistreatment in negative ways by ignoring the past, los ing faith in "white America," blaming themselves, or "[identifying with the aggressor by refusing to associate with other Japanese Americans and proudly proclaiming ignorance of Japan, its language and culture."1 Such reactions are not surprising given the government's goals for resettlement from the camps that encouraged widespread dispersal of the Nikkei com munity designed to promote individual assimilation. While many Nikkei undoubtedly coped in these ways, Personal Jus tice Denied failed to mention a more constructive, if less prominent, re sponse. While Japanese Americans hold almost universally negative atti tudes towards exile and incarceration today, some recall positive aspects of their experiences. In attempting to construct optimistic meanings for mass incarceration, some resettled Japanese Americans, the majority of whom were Nisei, resisted efforts on the part of both public and private agencies to foster assimilation through the dispersal of their ethnic com munity. Working within military limits that offered either eastward mi gration and assimilation or continued confinement, some resettlers echoed the government's rhetoric of dispersal and assimilation while simultane ously pursuing integration as an ethnic community. In doing so, these east ward pioneers shifted the battleground of the meaning of exile and in carceration from the War Relocation Authority [WRA] camps to the Intermountain West, Midwest, and East as they began to take advantage of the government's resettlement program in increasing numbers after 1942. Taylor's generalization that resettlement in the Intermountain West "both inflamed racism and ameliorated it" captures a basic reality of Nikkei wartime migration throughout the entire country.2 Understanding how resettled Nikkei defined their experiences provides a fuller conception of Japanese American history in both the immediate postwar era and beyond. Whereas almost 90 percent of Nikkei had lived on the West Coast in 1940, just under 70 percent lived there by 1950. Ac cording to WRA statistics, by 1946 57,251 Nikkei lived in the West Coast states, barely more than one-half of the 112,353 who had resided there in 1940. Although return migration continued after the war so that the West Coast Nikkei population reached 98,310, or almost 90 percent of its pre war level, significantly larger Nikkei communities emerged in new areas, especially in Illinois, New York, Ohio, Minnesota, and Michigan, largely due to significant growth in cities such as Chicago, New York, Cleveland, Minneapolis, and Detroit. While none of these states boasted anywhere near the total of California, Nikkei living in these states should not be ig nored.3 Historians, however, have focused much more attention on exile and incarceration than on resettlement. As a result, resettlement, despite its promise to complement our understanding of the complexities of wartime America by drawing connections between Japanese Americans and the society in which they lived, remains relatively understudied. Seen through the lens of resettlement, Japanese American history becomes an integral part of American historiography, in the process breaking down ar bitrary barriers between narrowly defined scholarly fields.4 NIKKEI RESPONSES TO WAR AND INCARCERATION Facing a chaotic situation in the aftermath of Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor, Nikkei did not agree on a single strategy as the government drifted toward what it euphemistically labeled "evacuation and relocation." Divi sions instead occurred as Nikkei debated two equally unhappy options? cooperation or resistance. Faced with intense pressure as the government froze bank accounts of enemy aliens and the FBI staged unannounced raids and arrested prominent Issei, or first-generation, leaders, most Nikkei pas sively awaited their fate.5 Other Nikkei, most prominently Nisei leaders of the Japanese Amer ican Citizens League [JACL], favored cooperating with the government.
