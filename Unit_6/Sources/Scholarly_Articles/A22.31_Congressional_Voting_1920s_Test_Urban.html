Congressional Voting in the 1920s: A Test of Urban-Rural Conflict Charles W. Eagles In the 1950s, historians explained many phenomena of the 1920s as products of conflicts between urban and rural areas. The Ku Klux Klan, Fundamentalism, Pro- hibition, immigration restriction, and Al Smith's defeat in the presidential election of 1928 were understood as examples of urban-rural tensions. More recent scholars have found evidence to challenge the urban-rural thesis by examining election results, state legislative voting, Klan membership, and support for Prohibition and Fundamentalism.1 Before scholars reject the urban-rural interpretation, however, it can be tested in a new way: analysis of roll-call voting in the House of Representa- tives in the 1920s. Such an analysis assumes that the intrinsic and symbolic importance of certain issues to urban or rural legislators outweighs other factors (friendship, partisanship, and pressures from House leaders, the president, and special interests) in deter- mining voting patterns. For a more precise refinement of the urban and rural classifications, congressional voting of representatives from metropolitan districts and extremely rural ones will be compared. The urban-rural (and metro-most rural) divisions cannot be perfect and complete, but differences in voting should be ob- servable if the urban-rural thesis is valid. Not all issues of the 1920s can be examined through roll calls; many did not come before Congress for a vote. On two questions central to the urban-rural interpreta- tion, however- Prohibition and immigration restriction - Congress did act. Four votes on each will be considered. Two roll-call votes on immigration restriction in- volved voting first to approve an amendment to a bill to reduce the suspension of all immigration from two years to just fourteen months, and second, to pass the bill. Four years later the House accepted an amendment to another bill to limit the burden of proof required of aliens to stay in the country, and then it approved the bill itself that restricted immigration to 2 percent of the 1890 population. The first vote on Prohibi- tion involved the House's approval in 1917 of the Eighteenth Amendment providing for Prohibition. Less than two years later, when Congress considered the Volstead bill to enforce Prohibition, the House accepted an amendment to weaken the penal- ties included in the bill, and then it approved the Volstead measure. In 1929, the House passed a bill to increase the penalties for violating the Volstead Act. Advo- cates of Prohibition would have voted for the constitutional amendment, against the amendment to weaken the Volstead bill, for the Volstead bill, and for the 1929 bill to strengthen the punishment of offenders.2 Measuring urban-rural conflict in the House of Representatives requires careful definitions of "urban" and "rural." The Census Bureau provides some assistance. In 1920, for the first time, it defined as "urban" any incorporated place with a popula- tion of 2,500 or more, and as "rural" any incorporated place of fewer than 2,500 and all unincorporated areas.3 Accepting the census definition of "rural" presents the least difficulty-certainly unincorporated areas and towns smaller than 2,500 can hardly be considered "urban." In the examination of roll calls, "rural" will mean the 254 congressional districts in which at least 50 percent of the population, ac- cording to the 1920 census, lived in unincorporated areas or in towns of fewer than 2,500 people. The remaining 181 districts can be considered "urban" according to the census standard because more than 50 percent of the population in each lived in towns of more than 2,500 population. The census designation of "urban" contains serious liabilities. A population limit of 2,500 seems inappropriately small for any meaningful definition of urban. An alternative uses the Census Bureau's new 1920 classification of "metropolitan dis- tricts." Often disregarding city boundaries, the census ranked metropolitan districts that included urban/suburban territory surrounding core cities. For roll-call vote comparisons, the eleven largest metropolitan districts, with a total of 100 congres- sional districts, will be used. To highlight any possible urban-rural conflict, the votes of representatives from "metro" districts will be compared to the votes of representatives from the "most- rural" districts. The latter will be defined as the 106 congressional districts that in 1920 had more than 80 percent of their populations in unincorporated areas or in towns of fewer than 2,500.
