EXCERPT Original journal pages 65-66

Selection Overview

Graves, a graduate student writing in 1962, evaluates Garvey's racial nationalism as a social program. He credits Garvey with giving Black Americans pride and self-reliance but judges that Garvey misunderstood the American racial problem, ran failed enterprises such as the Black Star Line, and left no lasting solution.

Current Trends in Negro Education and Shorter Papers Section A: The Social Ideas of Marcus Garvey JoHN L. GRAVES Graduate Student, Columbia University T ODAY, MILITANT GROUPS OF MUSLIM NEGROES are complicating America's race problems. As a religion it possesses an emotional and spiritual hold on its members. Islam among Muslim Negroes advocates black nationalism. A great deal of this agitation contains elements of racism. However, most of the ideology and propaganda that are spread by them have a peculiar resemblance to many of the ideas of Marcus Garvey and his Uni- versal Negro Improvement Association. Al- though very little in the way of perma- nent work perpetuated by Garvey remains, many of his ideas still have wide currency, if only among the Muslims. It, there- fore remains to be fully examined by present day standards. It is the purpose here to analyze some of these ideas as they are related to American solutions to the race problem. Garveyism will be treated herein as an aspect of the broader problem of Ameri- can nationalism. To be sure, his advo- cacy of Negro nationalism was intended to offset the stringent white nationalisms of racial supremacist. They necessarily opposed the "melting-pot" theory of Amer- ican nationalism. However, Garvey also found it a useless concept. The question then becomes whether or not Negroes should fight for full citizenship and be- come Americans in the "melting pot" sense of the term or accept second class citizen- ship? Garvey's answers to this problem are significant to our understanding of various approaches to this problem. But his answers must meet the test of two basic questions before they can claim legitimacy to current beliefs or as a solu- tion to the race problem. First, how completely did Garvey understand the American racial problem? Second, how positive was his solutions and what were the implications for the future of race relations. This examination intends to evaluate Garvey's social thought in order to appraise critically his qualifications as a leader of his people from the positive- ness and strength of his social thoughts. A large portion of the immigrants to America after World War I were Negro immigrants of English, French and Span- ish tongues. It is believed that Negro immigration was highest in 1924 when some 12,243 Negro immigrants were ad- mitted to the United States. Of these 12,243 some 10,630 or 86.6 per cent of the total were Negroes from the West Indies.! A majority of these West In- dians were from a peasant background and they brought over to the shores of the United States a more revolutionary tradition than was found among the American Negroes. Coming from that British colonial country, thley possessed a different reaction pattern to white people. There was a tripartite color caste system in Jamaica which was quite different from racial segregation in America. According to the Jamaican system, at the apex of the triangle of freedom and opportunity were the white 'English fam- ilies. On the next level, in the middle, were the mulattoes or Negroes of mixed parents. At the bottom were the great masses of dark-skinned Negroes. 66 THE JOURNAL OF NEGRO EDUCATION reflected the English doctrine of "divide and rule" which used the mulattoes as a buffer class between the upper-class whites and lower-class blacks. As the operation of this system became recognized, Negroes of darker color became suspicious of lighter-skinned Negroes. Thus in Ameri- ca, West Indian Negroes had to adjust to a new caste system. While in many cases, American whites and American Ne- groes made no distinction between Ne- groes of lighter or darker color, the West Indian Negroes merely applied the same standards they were accustomed to in Ja- maica. There also existed a linguistic and cultural difference between Ameri- can Negroes and West Indians which made for further divergence. The result was suspicion and distrust between the two groups for some time until the con- ditions of living for both groups became similar in many respects. The West Indian immigrants were full of hope, as were most other immigrant groups. They were usually very ambi- tious and intended to act upon the Ameri- can ideal of equal opportunity for all. Once faced with prejudice and discrimina- tion from American whites, they did not lapse into apathy. Rather, they radically criticized the American ideas of democ- racy and they criticized American Negroes as well, especially those who seemed to be "taken in" by these ideas. The American Negro's conditions, to be sure, left a great deal to be desired. Deprived of much of the freedom and opportunity around them, they became dis- illusioned. This was especially true of Negro leaders during World War I who agreed to close ranks with their white brethren and fight against the common enemy. But the lofty ideals preached by the leaders of the United States were not put into practice by the masses. Coupled with this affront to Negro loyalty, there arose small bands of militant white groups expounding a chauvinist doctrine of white superiority. Above all, violence and ter- roristic methods were used by the Ku Klux Klan, Knights of the White Came- lia and Anglo-Saxon clubs. American Negro disillusionment equalled that of the West fndian Negro and both existed in a plight of utter hopelessness of ever having full rights to freedom, let alone happiness, in America. It was in such a temper of the times that Marcus Garvey came to the United States. Born in St. Ann's Bay on the Northern coast of Jamaica on August 17, 1877, he travelled widely throughout Central America. Everywhere he went he met nothing but a down-trodden Ne- gro race in a state of apathy and discour- agement. Looking about the world and seeing the ferver of nationalism in every group but his own, he asked himself the obvious question. "Where is the Negro Republic, the black president, our army, navy-our nation?" The non-existence of Negro nationalism troubled him to the point wvhere he felt he had an original idea and a message for his people. Not possessing the intellectual prerequisite nor the financial genius to undertake what was to be an enormous plan for racial re- demption, Garvey relied on his stirring heroic propaganda and an emotional need among his people that amounted to a Messianic expectancy. Thus commenced one of the foremost Negro mass-move- ments in history. Garveyism stood for African national- ism and strove to create a homeland for all Negroes of the world. Racism per- meated nearly every iota of this ideology. It was a school of thought which held that racial separation was the only solu- tion to the race problem in the United States, or anywhere else. Its immediate effect as a centrifugal force was to give Negroes a pride in being black and the idea that Negroes were at least as good or better than whites. To be sure, the logical conclusion of any racist ideology tends to be founded upon the superiority of one over the other. History has pointed this out in case after case. The result of Garveyism was to turn Negroes against whites, it was a gospel of hate for whites. However, Garveyism did not provide whites with any notions that would make them hate Negroes. Garvey felt this already existed.
