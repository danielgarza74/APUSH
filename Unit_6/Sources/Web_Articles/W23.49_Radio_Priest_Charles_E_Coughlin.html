The Radio Priest: Charles E. Coughlin and American Antisemitism
United States Holocaust Memorial Museum
 
AUTHOR United States Holocaust Memorial Museum
WEBSITE Holocaust Encyclopedia (United States Holocaust Memorial Museum)
PUBLICATION Updated September 2, 2026
Excerpt · 1,117 words

Antisemitism is prejudice against or hatred of Jewish people. It has existed for thousands of years. It has also existed in many parts of the world, including in the United States. This article is about Father Charles E. Coughlin, a Catholic priest who became one of America’s most influential radio personalities in the 1930s. Coughlin used his popular radio broadcasts to promote antisemitism in the United States.

Father Charles E. Coughlin was one of the most popular media personalities in the United States during the 1930s. He spread lies on the radio about Jewish people’s political loyalties and economic influence. Coughlin portrayed himself as a champion of the working class. He claimed that he was giving voice to the concerns of ordinary Americans. His opposition to the United States’ involvement in World War II contributed to the decline of his popularity.

Early Life and Career

Charles E. Coughlin (1891–1979) was born in Ontario, Canada, to an Irish Catholic family. He was ordained as a Roman Catholic priest in 1916. Afterward, Coughlin began teaching history, literature, and other subjects at Assumption College, a boys’ school in Sandwich (later Windsor), Ontario.

While teaching at Assumption, Coughlin traveled many times to Detroit, Michigan, just over the US-Canada border from Windsor. In Detroit, he spoke at churches, clubs, and local events. Coughlin soon gained a reputation in the Detroit area as a talented and entertaining speaker.

Eventually, the bishop of the diocese of Detroit gave Coughlin the opportunity to establish a new parish in Royal Oak, a suburb north of Detroit. Coughlin accepted the appointment and left Assumption College in 1924. In Royal Oak, Coughlin helped to establish a church called the Shrine of the Little Flower. There, he ministered to a community made up mostly of automobile plant employees and other industrial workers. He served as Shrine’s pastor for more than 40 years, even as his influence reached well beyond his home church.

Coughlin Takes to the Radio

As Father Coughlin established himself at his new parish, he also started broadcasting on the radio. He delivered his first radio address on October 17, 1926. At the time, commercial radio was a new, powerful form of media that had been around for only six years. After Coughlin’s first radio address, five listeners sent him fan letters. And his popularity only grew from there. The number of fan letters increased significantly, and some listeners even donated money to the Shrine of the Little Flower. Just one year after his first broadcast, Coughlin could be heard on 20 stations in Michigan and surrounding states.

Religious and Political Messages

During Coughlin’s first broadcasts, he taught catechism classes for children. However, he soon began to include political opinions alongside religious instruction. Coughlin spoke out against using birth control. He also criticized the Ku Klux Klan, a white supremacist terror organization that targeted African Americans, Catholics, Jews, and immigrants. After the Great Depression began in 1929, Coughlin embraced populism. He claimed to be a voice for American workers who had lost their sense of economic and psychological security.

Building a Popular Audience

Coughlin quickly built up a large audience. By 1930, his addresses were broadcast on a national network, the Columbia Broadcasting System (CBS). He regularly had 30 million listeners, at a time when the entire US population was approximately 122 million. A new post office was built in Royal Oak just to receive the letters that listeners sent in each week—80,000 on average.

Despite Coughlin’s popularity, officials at CBS became concerned that his radio programs had become too political. In 1931, the network did not renew his contract. But, owners of individual stations were drawn to his program in part because it was so popular and profitable. Coughlin began to arrange his own contracts with many individual stations. By 1934, he could be heard on more than 30 radio stations.

Coughlin’s Populist Crusade

During his radio broadcasts, Father Coughlin shared populist ideas and claimed to speak for people who lacked power, especially the working class. He often said that justice could not prevail when wealth was concentrated among the upper classes. Coughlin linked these populist ideas to Catholic social activism that sought to combat mass poverty and political radicalism. His ideas were aligned with Catholic teachings on social justice.

Coughlin also encouraged his listeners to ask how the once strong US economy could fail so spectacularly and why workers had to suffer. He answered these questions by blaming financial institutions and demonizing communism. When he talked about financial institutions, he referred to bankers as “money changers.” This term came from the gospels of the Christian Bible and was often used as an antisemitic code for “Jews.” According to Coughlin, “money changers” were evil actors who benefited while ordinary American workers struggled.

In a February 1933 radio speech, Coughlin warned that “modern Shylocks” had “perpetuated the ancient crime of usury” and were profiting from the Great Depression.1 This single statement amplified multiple antisemitic stereotypes. It echoed the false idea that Jews controlled banking and finance, despite the fact that most Jews did not work in these professions. It also relied on the antisemitic slur “Shylock,” which referred to a vengeful moneylending character in William Shakespeare’s play The Merchant of Venice. Coughlin's mention of “usury” referred to the practice of lending money at high interest rates. This practice was condemned as a sin by Christians and was associated with Jews who allegedly took economic advantage of non-Jews.

Coughlin’s populism often included other barely veiled references to Jews as a driving force behind both financial institutions and communism. He frequently used the term “international bankers” as a code for the false assertion that Jews controlled the banks and world financial markets in order to exploit non-Jews. Although he did not always mention Jews explicitly, many of Coughlin’s references were antisemitic. They were most likely recognizable to his listeners across the United States.

The Christian Front

In July 1939, Coughlin began to encourage his supporters to back a new political association called the Christian Front. Coughlin promised that the Christian Front would defend the United States from Communists and Jews.

The militia-like Christian Front organized “Buy Christian” rallies throughout the United States. Some of its members supported the German American Bund, a pro-Nazi organization in the United States. At times, Christian Front members took to the streets to harass Jews, including Jewish women and children. The Christian Front made national headlines in 1940 when the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) arrested 18 members in Brooklyn, New York, on suspicion of conspiring to overthrow the government.

Coughlin told his listeners that the Christian Front’s path forward would be difficult. He celebrated its members’ suffering by comparing them to Jesus Christ. Coughlin spoke of a “call to arms,” where Christian Front members would fight against Jewish Communists, who he often referred to as Judeo-Bolshevists.
