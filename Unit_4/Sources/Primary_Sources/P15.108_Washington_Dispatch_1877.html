Historical Context

In late February 1877, as the electoral count neared its end, Representative Charles Foster and Stanley Matthews, both Ohio Republicans close to Hayes, wrote to southern Democrats John Young Brown of Kentucky and John B. Gordon of Georgia.

They pledged that Hayes would allow South Carolina and Louisiana to govern themselves. Brown published the letters in March, and newspapers across the country reprinted them as evidence of a bargain.

[. . .]

Washington, March 29. John Young Brown publishes the following letters in connection with a narrative of events pledging the President more strongly than the letters themselves: House of Representatives, Washington, Feb. 26th. Gentlemen: Referring to the conversation had with you yesterday, in which Gov. Hayes' policy as to the status of certain Southern States was discussed, we desire to say in reply that we can assure you in the strongest possible manner of our great desire to have adopted such a policy as will give to the people of the States of South Carolina and Louisiana the right to control their own affairs in their own way, and to say further that we feel authorized from an acquaintance with and knowledge of Gov. Hayes and his views on this question, to pledge ourselves to you for him that such will be his policy. Charles Foster. To Hon. John Young Brown and Hon. John B. Gordon. Washington City, Feb. 27, 1877. Gentlemen: Referring to the conversation had with you yesterday, in which Governor Hayes' policy as to the status of certain Southern States was discussed, we desire to say that we can assure you in the strongest possible manner of our great desire to have him adopt such a policy as will give to the people of the States of South Carolina and Louisiana the right to control their own affairs in their own way, subject only to the Constitution of the United States and the laws made in pursuance thereof, and to say further that from an acquaintance with and knowledge of Gov. Hayes and his views, we have the most complete confidence that such will be the policy of his administration. Respectfully, (Signed,) Stanley Matthews, Charles Foster. To Hon. John B. Gordon and Hon. John Young Brown.

[. . .]

The following letter was addressed to the President by Governor Hampton this morning: Willard's Hotel, Washington, March 29th To the President Sir: In compliance with your invitation, I am here for the purpose of uniting my efforts with yours to the end of composing the political differences which now unhappily distract at this time the people of South Carolina. I beg you to believe that my anxiety to bring about the permanent pacification of that State, a pacification in which the rights of all shall be safe, and the interests of all shall be protected, is as sincere as I feel assured is your own for the accomplishments of the same ends. My position for years past, in reference to the political rights of colored citizens, and my solemn pledges given during the late canvass in South Carolina, that under my administration all their rights should be absolutely secure, should furnish a sufficient guarantee of my sincerity on these points, which appear to be the subject of special anxiety. I have the honor to ask at what hour it will be your pleasure to receive me. I am, sir, very respectfully, your obedient servant, Wade Hampton, Governor of South Carolina.
