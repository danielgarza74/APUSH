Selection Overview

Shlomowitz traces how cotton plantations moved after emancipation from centralized gang labor, paid by wages or a collective crop share, to family parcels worked for rent or shares, often through an intermediate "squad system." Using South Carolina Freedmen's Bureau contracts, he argues the contracts were enforced and largely market-driven, giving planters a group incentive and freedmen some independence.

Women in the Labor Force 333 This increase in demand was associated with a rising demand for products that used cleri- cal labor as an input, with institutional changes that increased the demand for record keeping, and with the decline in the cost of clerical output that resulted from technological innovation in office production. The most notable of the technological advances in the of- fice was the typewriter, but there were myriad other innovations (mechanical and organi- zational) which reduced the unit cost of clerical output. These technological advances did not merely expand the demand for clerical labor. They transformed work processes in such a way as to increase the demand for a different kind of labor than had previously been hired in offices. The new techniques of office pro- duction caused a change in the nature of the skills required of clerical labor. Prior to the development of the modern, mechanized office, the skills required of clerical workers had been specific to the individual firm and many clerical jobs were entry points to job ladders with managerial functions attached. When skills are firm-specific, it is expected that the firm would be very concerned with worker turnover and would seek to hire clerical work- ers expected to have a long work life with the firm. With increased mechanization and routinization of clerical tasks, the skills required of clerical workers became much more firm-general. This allowed employers to hire for such jobs the young, educated women who had high expected turnover and who desired clean, high-status employment. The rapid development of commercial education (both public and private) greatly aided this change in the sex composition of the growing clerical work force. The dramatic increase in women's share of clerical employment was due to both supply and demand shifts. We observe an expansion in both the demand for female clerical labor and the supply of educated young women during the period when there were rapid in- creases in the employment of young women in clerical jobs. Did demand or supply shifts dominate? The timing of the growth of the relevant magnitudes suggests an answer. The secular growth in women's labor force participation and in women's educational attain- ment was relatively smooth throughout the period. Women's participation in clerical em- ployment grew more rapidly and shows a more volatile pattern. The evidence on timing leads to the conclusion that whereas long-term growth in female clerical employment was undoubtedly related to the secular growth in female labor supply, short-term fluctuations were more clearly related to demand factors, especially to technological advances. ELYCE J. ROTELLA, San Diego State University The Transition from Slave to Freedman Labor Arrangements in Southern Agriculture, 1865-1870 This thesis is concerned with the reorganization of work on cotton plantations that was associated with the emancipation of the slaves. Immediately after the Civil War, most planters attempted to revive the centralized plantation using gang labor. The most popular modes of contracting were either for individual members of the plantation labor force to receive a stipulated wage or for the plantation labor force to receive collectively a share of the crop produced on the plantation. The centralized plantation, however, did not prove to be viable with free labor. Beginning in the late 1860s and continuing during successive decades, the plantation system was completely restructured. The vast majority of planta- tions eventually were subdivided into parcels of land that were operated separately by in- dividual families either for a stipulated rent or, more often, for a share of the crop. The latter arrangement became known as sharecropping.

334 Shlomowitz The focus of the thesis is on the mechanism of transition by which the centralized plan- tation using gang labor became the decentralized plantation where separate tenant fami- lies operated their own parcels of land. It is argued that this change very often was not made directly-in many cases, the transition was effected by an intermediate stage which was called by contemporaries "the squad system." Under this system, a number of sub- groups of the plantation labor force, called squads, worked for a share of the crop on the separate parcel of land allotted each squad. Thus, three types of the share agreement are distinguished: the collective share arrangement for the whole plantation labor force; the collective share arrangement for subgroups, or squads, of the whole plantation labor force; and the share arrangement for individual family units. It is suggested that the transforma- tion of the plantation system and the associated change in the way field labor was orga- nized is closely related to the process of scaling down the size of the collective share ar- rangement. More generally, the thesis is concerned with the economic and social causes and con- sequences of these new work arrangements. The evidence used in interpreting these issues was expanded by using the unpublished records of the Freedmen's Bureau. As part of their many duties, Bureau officials supervised the negotiation of labor contracts between 1865 and 1868. Thousands of these labor contracts have been preserved at the National Archives. For the purposes of this thesis, a 5 percent random sample was taken of the la- bor contracts for South Carolina. This amounted to 640 contracts, covering over 6,000 freedmen. The number of freedmen covered by these contracts exceeds the number of contracts because employers usually contracted with their labor force as a group. The structure of the thesis is as follows: Chapter 1 focuses on the issues addressed by the thesis; the data base on which this set of issues can be interpreted; a conceptual framework on "team work and incentives"; an analysis of the essential and special features of the slave system of work organization; and an exploratory analysis of the nexus between the way slave labor was organized and the social structure and culture of the slave commu- nity. In Chapters 2 and 3, the institutional structure of the Bureau-supervised labor mar- ket is investigated. Unpublished reports of the sub-assistant commissioners of the Bureau in South Carolina are used to evaluate the degree of competition in the labor market; the extent of the enforcement of these labor contracts by Bureau officials; the demand by em- ployers and freedmen for the services of the Bureau in approving labor contracts; and the labor turnover of the freedmen. The sample of Bureau-approved labor contracts is used to analyze the incidence of various payment schemes that were tried both for field labor and for artisans and foremen. The contracts also provide other information, for example, in- formation on the value of field labor during the year. The main conclusions of these two chapters is that these contracts were enforced. The extraordinary diversity of contracts within each district and between districts, and the of- ten detailed and subtle arrangements made between planters and freedmen, make it un- likely that these were merely pro forma contracts having little or no relationship to actual behavior. The evidence suggests that the contracts were largely determined by the free in- terplay of market forces. The most popular mode of contracting for field labor was the col- lective share arrangement for the whole plantation labor force. The considerable variation in the fraction of the crop received by freedmen for their labor is explained by considering the share as a variable that was negotiated by employers and freedmen. For example, evi- dence is presented showing that the share was inversely related to the quality of land and to the number of work animals per laborer. It is argued that the collective share agreement was not simply imposed by planters on the freedmen. Rather, it was widely adopted as it proved satisfactory to both planters and freedmen. From the point of view of planters, it was considered to be a group incentive scheme. Supervision costs could be reduced since freedmen would have incentives to engage in self-policing and reciprocal monitoring of each other's performance. From the point of view of freedmen, the share agreement was considered a way of obtaining a measure of independence from planter control not pos- sible under the wage system.
