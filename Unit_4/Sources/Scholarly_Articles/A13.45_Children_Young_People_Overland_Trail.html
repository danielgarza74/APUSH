Selection Overview

Moynihan draws on diaries and memoirs to recover the experiences of children and adolescents on the overland trail, many of whom remembered the trip as grueling yet exciting. She explains that families headed for Oregon and California seeking fertile land, opportunity, and a healthful climate, escaping malarial fevers and hard times, and judged the trail's hazards little worse than those they already faced.

Children and Young People on the Overland Trail RUTH BARNES MOYNIHAN t was a long, long journey; full of grave responsibility to the older members of the family .... But for me, a lad of fifteen, it was . . . the most interesting six months' period of my life," said D. B. Ward, sixty years after his overland journey west in 1853. The senti- ment was shared by thousands of other children and adolescents who took part in that historic American migration, though the trip was often both grueling and challenging. Some of those adolescents kept diaries, and some of the children later wrote memoirs, which enable us to recreate their experiences. Among the most interesting are the diaries of eighteen-year-old Helen Marie Stewart and seventeen-year-old Moses Schallenberger, and the memoirs of Ward, Elisha Brooks, Jesse A. Applegate, Fred Lockley, Sarah J. Cummins, and 0. M. Pringle.' When they made the trip, Sarah was a newly married sixteen-year-old, Pringle, a boy of fourteen, Brooks was eleven, and Applegate and Lockley were both seven. All of these youths, and many others, were members of large families heading west in wagon trains between 1843 and 1853. 280 THE WESTERN HISTORICAL QUARTERLY July Americans in the 1840s were bent on settling Oregon and California, and for several reasons. Occupation would help refute British and Mexican claims to those areas.3 Physical possession of the land was the first step to political control. Added incentives were the stories of fertile land, personal opportunities, and healthful climate. Many settlers were only too glad to leave malarial areas of Michigan, Illinois, and Missouri where they suffered from recurrent fevers and high mortality rates.4 James Reed of the Donner party, among others, expected both "to escape the hard times afflicting the Mississippi Valley in the forties" and to find "that the already famous climate might benefit his invalid wife." 5 Families traveled the overland trail because the little-known hazards did not seem that much graver than the dangers of the lives they were already leading, even for pregnant wives or babes in arms. At the same time, the opportunities seemed much better. Toddlers and young children could not be easily controlled as the wagons rolled across the prairies. Although they might stay asleep for a few hours after the early morning starts, they were usually out exploring the other wagons or the nearby countryside the rest of the day. As the trains advanced at a speed of only two or three miles an hour, children probably found the trip little more tiring than an average day of play around a settled home. Mothers, older children, fathers, or friends usually walked with them or carried them. Despite the advantages of this shared child care, there were also hazards. Elisha Brooks described an incident in which a three-year-old boy was missing at suppertime when the train made camp. Everyone searched the dense prairie sagebrush that night and the next morning even though a violent storm and the howling of wolves had them convinced the child must be either dead or devoured.
