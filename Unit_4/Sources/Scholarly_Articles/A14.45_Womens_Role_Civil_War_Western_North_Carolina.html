Women's Role in Civil War Western North Carolina Gordon B. McKinney Historians of the Confederacy have turned their attention increasingly to the home front to find an explanation for the collapse of the southern army and government in 1865. Both works of broad synthesis and narrow specialization have concluded that the destruction of civilian morale was the crucial factor that led to the massive number of desertions that left the Confederate armies unable to resist Federal advances. Moreover, several scholars have argued that women played a central role in the decline of the southern willingness to sacrifice for the cause. Drew Gilpin Faust has stated: "Southern women undermined both objective and ideological foundations for the Confederate effort; they directly subverted the South's military and economic effec tiveness as well as civilian morale."1 Faust, Paul D. Escott, Michael Fellman, Phillip Shaw Paludan, and George C. Rabie have described in detail how southern women were brutalized by events of the war.2 They have carefully documented the growing disenchantment and overt actions that compromised the Confederate drive for inde pendence. McKinney was one of the several areas in the Confederacy where no major battles were fought, large standing armies were not stationed, and few of the inhabitants were forced to leave their homes and become refugees. Despite all of those seeming advantages, women in the region were physically threatened and attacked; social, political, and economic divisions abounded; deprivation and scarcity became the normal pattern of life; and the mountain counties were a center for deserters and Unionist sympathizers. By 1864 and 1865, women in western North Carolina, with a few exceptions, had stopped supporting the war and worked actively or passively for peace. All of that took place in a part of the South where some scholars have assumed that a more closely knit traditional society had great inherent strength to resist outside pressure. The evidence in this study suggests that for women in western North Carolina during the Civil War such insularity was not the case. In fact the events of 1861-1865 seemed predestined to force mountain women to undermine the Confederate war effort. As Jeffrey J. Crow and Paul D. Escott have observed, North Carolina civil society disintegrated under the constant pressure applied by the four years of war. That consistent battering progressively weakened social institutions and exposed many of the animosities buried beneath the state's closed social system. As the structure collapsed from the accumulated blows, the nonelite challenged the position and the assumptions of the entrenched leadership.4 That interpretive framework explains much about the lives of women in the state's western counties during the war. The demands of the war itself were sufficient to destroy the infrastructure of mountain society. The destruction of the community economy, local institutions, and the family ensured that mountain women could not support the Confederacy. In their attempts to preserve their traditional life-style, the women of western North Carolina weakened the fight for southern independence. Opposition to the Confederacy was usually not a conscious decision—although in the case of some Unionists it was—but simply a reaction to the growing demands of the war itself. The failure of mountain society to deflect the pressures placed upon women provides an opportunity not only to assess the impact of the war but also to use the comments made by the participants to construct a more accurate picture of life in the mountain counties of North Carolina. The traditional life of most mountain women was part of the much broader world of rural southern and American women. Western North Carolina was one of the most isolated farming regions in the United States. The 1860 census revealed an almost total absence of commercial agriculture.5 While slavery was of greater consequence in the highland counties than previous scholars thought, there were few large commercial farms specializing in staple crops in western North Carolina. The economy was both more diversified and less advanced than in other parts of the South. Because the slave-owning elite depended upon using its bonded labor in a variety of commercial ventures to sustain its position, no single economic interest group—like cotton or rice planters—could suppress the growing influence of merchants.
