In both periods the state earned the reputation of having in its midst a large disaffected popu- lation resistant to the demands and discipline of the reigning govern- ment. The situation in North Carolina in both eras fits a classic definition of irregular warfare with independent bands of partisans or guerrillas harassing and terrorizing their antagonists, interrupting lines of communication, destroying supplies, and fighting skir- mishes. Such violence, sporadic at first, became chronic as the larger conflicts wore on. After the British invaded the Carolinas in 1780- 1781, the Tories were able to muster hundreds of effectives who operated largely on an unsupervised basis with only nominal over- sight from British officers. During the Civil War Confederate authorities repeatedly had to send regular troops into Piedmont North Carolina to quell, neutralize, and capture hundreds of deserters, draft dodgers, and militant Unionists.' Even more striking than the recurring appearance of opposition in North Carolina were the similarities in the nature of resistance during the two periods. Despite the passage of over eighty years of popula- tion growth and economic and political evolution, violent disorder presented the same face to society. Thus this article focuses not on comparing obvious changes over time but on investigating the under- lying social arrangements and forces that produced such similar phenomena. 2 The study of internal conflict is intrinsically related to the long established examination of revolutions and the newer interest in comparative history. See Crane Brinton, The Anatomy of Revolution (New York, 1965); Barrington Moore, Jr., Social Origins of Dictatorship and Democracy: Lord and Peasant in the Making of the Modern World (Boston, 1966); George M. Fredrickson, "Comparative History," in Michael Kammen, ed., The Past Before Us: Contem- porary Historical Writing in the United States (Ithaca, N. To some extent a comparative approach is implicit in this article, although in North Carolina's case the prominence of similarities rather than differences over time diminishes the utility of comparison through contrast. The comparative approach would be even more suited for an analysis of internal conflict in different colonies during the Revolu- tion and different states of the Confederacy.
