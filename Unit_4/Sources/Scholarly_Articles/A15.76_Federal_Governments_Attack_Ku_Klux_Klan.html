THE FEDERAL GOVERNMENT'S ATTACK ON THE KU KLUX KLAN: A REASSESSMENT Richard Zuczek* ON OCTOBER 17,1871, PRESIDENT ULYSSES S. GRANT SUSPENDED the writ of habeas corpus in nine counties of South Carolina, firing the opening barrage in the federal government's war on the Ku Klux Klan. For years the Klan had terrorized Republicans and wreaked havoc with the fledgling southern Republican Party. At long last the government struck back. The Palmetto State would serve as a test case; the flexing of federal muscle was intended to punish civil-rights violations and deter future ones. Success was expected to bolster the southern Republican Party and Reconstruction in general, while failure might embolden conservative whites and further endanger Republican lives and Republican programs. Historians hold differing views on the scope of the federal campaign, but generally agree on its results. Most scholars contend that the intervention in South Carolina restored peace, destroyed the Klan, and bolstered the administration's enforcement program. For instance, Allen Trelease, in what has become the standard account of the Klan during Reconstruction, maintains that Klan terror ended only "as a result of federal intervention." Herbert Shapiro claims that the federal government's "limited" steps were enough to "destroy the organization," while Joel Williamson credits "a crushing imposition of federal power" with the destruction of the Klan. James Sefton emphasizes the army's role, stating that "the application of military force broke the Klan in South Carolina." Robert Kaczorowski, on the other hand, believes that "federal prosecutions in South Carolina so demoralized members of the Ku Klux Klan that its leaders issued orders to stop all Klan activity." Even Eric Foner, in the most important Reconstruction synthesis of our time, Reconstruction, 1863-1877: America's Unfinished Revolution, characterizes the "crackdown" as a strategic victory; he argues that in its "larger purposes ? restoring order, rein vigora ting the morale of Southern Republicans, enabling blacks to exercise their rights as citizens ? * Assistant editor at the Papers of Andrew Johnson, University of Tennessee South Carolina Historical Magazine 97 No. 1 (January 1996)

For too long, however, scholars have attributed great significance to federal actions in South Carolina without examining in detail the actual events. Historians who came of age in the 1950s and 1960s were eager to find a solution to the civil and racial strife rampant in America; when historians found a decline of violence in South Carolina coinciding with the onset of federal activity, the conclusions seemed both logical and timely. Unfortunately, these conclusions are not wholly accurate. The Grant administration attempted to make an example of South Carolina by destroying the Klan there; instead, the federal "crackdown" demonstrated the enforcement program's weaknesses rather than its strengths, and ultimately damaged Reconstruction more than it did the Klan. THIS IS NOT, HOWEVER, AN ARTICLE ON THE KU KLUX KLAN, but rather an examination of the federal government's response to it. Lawlessness was rampant throughout the former Confederate states, and the federal government did little in the years following Appomattox to curb disorder. As with other southern states, South Carolina's adjustment to the post-war world was a violent one. The election of 1868 ? the first since readmission ? marked the appearance of the Ku Klux Klan in the state. Bands appeared in South Carolina in the spring of 1868 following the success of the new state constitution and black suffrage. Republican state authorities offered little resistance; the few loyal constables and the poorly trained black state militia were incapable of deterring Klansmen and arresting criminals. Through two years and two state elections, black and white Republicans suffered whippings, assassinations, and midnight visits by the hundreds. By 1870 conservatives were instigating riots and carrying out well-organized assaults on black militia units and Republican meetings.
