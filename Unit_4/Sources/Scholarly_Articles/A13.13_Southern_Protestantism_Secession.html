Early in the summer editors of various denominational weekly newspapers took a cautious position on the campaign, the election, and political activities. One of the first papers to discuss current political issues was the Due West Telescope; on July 13 it advised all Christians to take a vital interest in the forthcoming election but cautioned them not to enter into political squabbles. Christians, it noted, should plan to vote and also pray that the political storm raging throughout the land should pass peacefully. Several weeks later the Virginia Baptist paper, the Religious Herald, asked its readers to "invoke the aid of God" to quell the attacks of Southern disunionists and Northern fanatics or else they would destroy the nation. The Central Presbyterian denounced secession in September and declared that disunion would result in a "horrible Civil War." Similar sentiments were also expressed by denominational periodicals in the deep South. Three weeks prior to the election the New Orleans Christian Advocate, a Methodist journal, explained that if the "Black Republican" nominee, Abraham Lincoln, were elected, it would not endanger Southern institutions. Southerners were cautioned to be calm, remembering that, if elected, he would serve for only four years. The Church Intelligencer, an official publication of the Protestant Episcopal Church, recommended that Friday prior to the election be observed as a day of fasting and prayer "for peace, prosperity, and perpetual union of our beloved land." Presbyterian synods in North Carolina and Virginia asked that Christians beseech Almighty God to "forgive our national sins, heal our differences, and continue us a united and prosperous people."1 The Synod of Virginia urged clergymen to preach sermons, prior to the election, on the duty of Christians as peacemakers. The one by Robert Lewis Dabney, moderator of this synod, delivered on November 1, denounced the activities of passionate men who had disturbed the unity of the nation and aroused fears of war. He pleaded with the citizens to vote for virtuous men and to pray for peace.2 Extensive research has not disclosed a single religious newspaper or denominational leader which officially and openly espoused secession prior to the election of Abraham Lincoln.
