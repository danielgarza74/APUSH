The Freedmen's Bureau as a Legal Agent for Black Men and Women in Georgia: 1865-1868 By Sara Rapport May 1868, a number of "unruly" field hands in Cuthbert, Georgia, were brought by their employer before an agent of the Freedmen's Bureau because they refused to work.1 All promised to labor diligently in the future, with the exception of a woman named Mary, who announced that "all the Bureaus out can't make her work." Eventually the agent had Mary and her husband Isaac arrested and brought before the bureau again, where, after a lengthy investigation, he determined that Mary had worked a mere seven days in exchange for two months' rations. "Mary," the agent concluded, "is an impudent vulgar woman." He then annulled the contract between Mary and her employer, and ordered Isaac to keep his wife "in sub- jection."2 Mary's story is interesting, not only for what it reveals about the agent's conception of Mary and Isaac's relationship, but also for what it reveals about Mary herself. Yet because she was not in a strong position for seeking affirmative relief (she was, after all, the defendant), she could only "act" by reacting through resistance. On other occasions, when freedpeople shifted their posture from that of defendant to that of complainant, they made specific demands upon the bureau, and, in the process, worked more affirma- tively than could Mary to make the institution their own. Freedpeople made their demands by speaking directly to bureau agents. These agents had two types of legal respon- sibilities. First, they adjudicated disputes among blacks and be- tween blacks and whites in "all places [including Georgia] where local courts disregarded] the negro's right to justice before the laws. . . ."3 Second, they acted as the freedpeople's advocate in the local judicial system. Many became "watchdogs," observing how police officials responded to reports of outrages commit- ted against freedpeople and how courts handled their authority over freedpeople's complaints.4 In Georgia, as in many states, the number of agents varied dramatically from 1865 through 1868. Davis Tillson, the sec- ond assistant commissioner of the Georgia bureau, attended the state's convention in late 1865 to seek from among the delegates men who would serve as agents.
