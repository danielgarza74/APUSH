review essay Women, Gender, and the Boundaries of Reconstruction catherine a. jones This essay surveys Reconstruction scholarship centrally concerned with women and gender published since the 1990s. By drawing on gender theory, particularly the insights of intersectional analysis, women’s historians advanced a redefinition of politics that has transformed Reconstruction historiography over the past twenty- five years. This essay examines the tension between scholarship that emphasizes the importance of gendered exclusions in grounding Reconstruction’s civil rights gains and research that foregrounds women’s engagement in the era’s radically plu- ralistic politics. The western turn in Reconstruction scholarship raises new ques- tions about emancipation’s impact on women’s lives and the connections between women’s suffrage and white supremacy. This essay contends that gender remains an essential analytical category for historians seeking to integrate regional strands of Reconstruction scholarship; at the same time, it argues that recognizing women’s agency in advancing and curtailing the era’s equalitarian potential is essential to developing comprehensive new narratives of the postwar era. Historians of women were largely absent from the renaissance in Reconstruction scholarship of the 1960s and 1970s. Women’s exclusion from the post–Civil War expansion of suffrage was a historic loss with historiographical consequences; it helped make Reconstruction, a period largely defined by electoral politics, appear to be inhospitable terrain for U.S. women’s historians. Beyond the important work Ellen Carol DuBois and others did in the 1970s to write the history of the era’s suffrage move- ment, there seemed little else to say. 1 In the 1990s, however, an outpour- ing of research on women, gender, and Reconstruction began to appear. postwar era. They also redefined politics itself, ranking households and schools alongside legislatures and polling places as key sites of political contest. In this new scholarship, Reconstruction often receded as the pri- mary object of investigation as gender historians instead focused on such themes as emancipation and changes in household order. By stretching Reconstruction thematically, the gender scholarship of the 1990s also strained the era’s traditional periodization and helped set the stage for cur- rent debates about how to frame the history of the postwar era. 3 This essay surveys Reconstruction scholarship centrally concerned with women and gender that has emerged since the 1990s, both to reflect on its accomplishments and to suggest where historians have more work to do. Three big questions inform much of this literature: To what degree did the abolition of slavery transform women’s lives and gender relations? Did the postwar revolution in citizenship curtail or expand women’s capacity to shape the postwar world and their own lives? And to what extent did the consolidation of U.S. sovereignty depend on women’s subordination and the promotion of white supremacy? While treatments of these questions tend to be anchored in a single region (the South, the North, and the West, respectively), this essay suggests that putting regional narratives into con- versation is vital to deepening our understanding of the postwar era and Reconstruction’s impact on it. Gender scholars, particularly those who emphasize continuity in wom- en’s subordination across the Civil War era, reveal the limitations of the question that has organized much Reconstruction historiography: why did Reconstruction fail? 4 While the question signals Reconstruction’s value as a political resource for the present, it is premised on a declension narrative that focuses historians’ attention on moments of collapse and risks over- stating Reconstruction’s rupture with the past. The question constrains historians’ analysis, for example, by foreclosing examination of women’s exclusion from many of the era’s equalitarian gains. Recent gender schol- arship suggests that the tension between Reconstruction’s democratic and exclusionary dimensions was an endemic feature of the postwar context rather than one revealed in the moments of reversal that conventionally mark Reconstruction’s end.
