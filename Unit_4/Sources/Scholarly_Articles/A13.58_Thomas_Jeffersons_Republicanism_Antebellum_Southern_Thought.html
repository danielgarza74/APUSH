Thomas Jefferson's Republicanism and Antebellum Southern Thought By ROBERT E. SHALHOPE THOMAS JEFFERSON HAS FASCINATED SCHOLARS FOR WELL OVER ONE hundred years. Throughout that time, however, he has not been immune to the tendency among historians to create a single and substantial "reality" in the period they are studying and then to judge individuals, groups, or movements by that standard. Those reflecting the fundamental realities of an age merit favorable judg- ment, while those who depart from full awareness present irrational or moral aberrations. An individual's ideas either contain true or false descriptions of the "real" world; ambiguous statements ema- nate from confused minds.1 Thus, measured by one historian's conception Jefferson was a great liberal statesman, whereas by another's he was an opportunistic hypocrite. In recent years research in a striking variety of disciplines has converged on an area of investigation which casts doubt on the historian's effort to establish the reality of an era. This "cognitive" scholarship-intent upon understanding the function of ideas in human experience-views man as a "symboling" creature.2 That is, man, in his effort to cope with a complex and at times over- whelming universe, constructs schemes of reality which impose form on that universe. Individuals rarely encounter objective real- ity; instead, they confront a reality which is filtered through images in their minds, images which link one experience with another in meaningful ways. Humans see the world only through a "mental set," or a cluster of expectations about what they will see. To employ Thomas S. Kuhn's term, communities create "paradigms" which set the form for their enquiry.3 In effect, these paradigms establish the mental space within which a community thinks and acts. In the light of this research historians should become aware of the possibility that separate groups within a particular society might well encounter their environment through distinctive social constructs. Thus, a given historical era could produce a number of different "realities." A fuller knowledge of history may, therefore, come only as a result of a complementarity of views. With this in mind, it is hoped that an analysis of the paradigm which structured his thought will futher an understanding of Thomas Jefferson and perhaps provide access to the mind-set through which a growing number of antebellum southerners experienced the early nine- teenth century.4 The idea that the American South underwent a conservative reaction in the second quarter of the nineteenth century has en- joyed great popularity among historians for quite some time.5 Scholars adhering to this perspective delineate "Jeffersonian" and "post-Jeffersonian" phases of antebellum southern history.
