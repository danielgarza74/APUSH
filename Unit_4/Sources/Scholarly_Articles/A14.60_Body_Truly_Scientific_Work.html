Selection Overview

Schwalm argues that the U.S. Sanitary Commission's scientific work included a large effort to catalog supposed evidence of racial inferiority in the bodies of African American soldiers. She questions the familiar story of wartime medical progress, pointing to inferior care for black southerners and to an intransigent racial essentialism among northern whites that endured after slavery's destruction.

leslie a. schwalm A Body of “Truly Scientific Work” The U.S. Sanitary Commission and the Elaboration of Race in the Civil War Era During the Civil War, the U.S. Sanitary Commission was much more than a phil- anthropic soldier-relief organization. It also engaged in what commission presi- dent Henry Bellows called “truly scientific work,” work that Bellows believed was the commission’s main chance at “being valued as men of mark in time to come.” A sig- nificant portion of that scientific work consisted of a large-scale endeavor to iden- tify and catalog anatomical and physiological evidence of racial inferiority in the bodies of African American soldiers. Using the tools of anthropometry and social surveys, the northern white members of the commission expressed their commit- ment to an increasingly intransigent racial essentialism that endured long after the wartime destruction of slavery. Was American medicine transformed by the Civil War? If so, did the changes result in an improvement in medical knowledge and practice, and in the role of medicine in American society? Most historians of medicine answer these questions with a conditional yes. While acknowledging the war’s fearsome rates of morbidity and mortality, the impact of a poorly organized and understaffed Army Medical Department plagued by incom- petency, and the lack of new insight into disease processes, many histo- rians—including the authors of the two most recent surveys of Civil War medicine—conclude that American medical knowledge and practice took a significant step forward. The war led to substantial improvements in the organization and delivery of emergency and chronic medical care; greatly extended surgical knowledge; popularized the use of disinfectants, vac- cines, and quarantine to limit the spread of disease; gave physicians valu- able experience with unprecedentedly abundant clinical material (sick and injured bodies and cadavers); secured the victory of Regular practitioners in the sectarian competition; and elevated the quality and circulation of the nation’s medical print culture. Public health especially benefitted from the increased acceptance of ideas about hygiene and sanitation. 1 647 ORIGINAL JOURNAL PAGE 647

Yet this widely accepted checklist of wartime and postwar advance- ments suggests that our assessment of wartime medicine can or should be isolated from the impact of the well-studied disdain many civilian and mil- itary health providers held toward soldiers and civilians of African descent, or from an accounting of the institutional failures that produced markedly inferior or absent health care for black southerners in need. The resulting wartime health crisis that black soldiers and southern black refugees from slavery encountered has been well-documented, most recently in the work of historians Gretchen Long, Margaret Humphreys, Chandra Manning, and Jim Downs; we know from their scholarship that the chaos shap- ing the wartime path to freedom forced former slaves through a gaunt- let of epidemic disease, exposure, starvation, and brutal exploitation. 2 Furthermore, in the postwar nation, health disparities between whites and people of African descent continued to grow, despite the dismantling of slavery. White medical practitioners and public health professionals increasingly concentrated on black bodies as perceived risks to white pop- ulations and communities. 3 Racial exclusion and segregation continued to shape the infrastructures of medical education, practice, and research, and medical print culture increasingly espoused the inferiority of black bodies and the threat they posed to white public health. Among southern blacks especially, the war failed to transform either their access to health care or the ill health perpetrated by racist beliefs and practices of most white medical practitioners. 4 The story of missing treatment and mistreatment of black soldiers, refugees from slavery, and freedpeople should erode our confidence in assuming a forward march of medical progress after the war. But so, too, should the abundant evidence that an increasingly intransigent racial essentialism was among the accomplishments of wartime and postwar medical sciences. Despite the Civil War’s erosion and then destruction of racial slavery in the United States, northern whites continued during and after the war to hold African American bodies captive to the exercise of white dominance. Whether in passing through public spaces or in striv- ing to gain entrance to jury boxes, schools, or first-class train cars, African Americans encountered rhetorical and physical resistance from northern whites in their efforts to connect freedom with citizenship.
