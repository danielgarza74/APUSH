If the hundreds of studies of the political settlement and its aftermath and of the actors involved have not produced a definitive interpretation of the New South, at least the main outlines of the story are clear. The same cannot be said of the economic history of Reconstruction and the New South. The relatively few studies in this area have not resolved many of the most basic questions of what happened-to say nothing of how and why they occurred. We know that land was not confiscated and distributed to the freedmen (with a few notable exceptions) and that various forms of tenancy grew up in the South, but the details concerning how and precisely when this took place remain shrouded and obscure. Similarly, we know something of the credit system-the stores, the furnishing merchants, the crop liens-but our knowledge of how the system arose, how it changed over time, and how it affected those involved is murky and impre- cise. In a word, as Charles B. Dew has noted, "The economic history of the New South remains an underdeveloped area of schol- arship. 524 THE JOURNAL OF SOUTHERN HISTORY The sad state of scholarship in the social and economic history of the New South stands in marked contrast to that of the prewar period. Few historians are unaware of the enormous contributions to the social and economic history of the antebellum South by scholars such as Kenneth M. Stampp, Stanley M. Elkins, Robert William Fogel, Stanley L. Engerman, Eugene D. Genovese, John W. Blassingame, Herbert G. Gutman, and George P. Rawick-to name but a few. Their work has not simply added to our storehouse of information; it has also provided us with new methods and new perspectives from which to view familiar information. To be sure, these studies do not provide definitive answers. They often con- tradict one another, and all have elicited sharp critiques; indeed, the criticisms themselves have added immeasurably to our under- standing. During the past decade a growing number of scholars have begun to apply new methods and approaches to the history of the postbellum South. Although much of this work has just begun to appear, it is already clear that we can expect a welcome renewal of interest in the social and economic history of the New South. In this essay I propose to outline the main directions this work is taking, to assess its contributions-and limitations-and to suggest possi- bilities for further research. In the two decades since its work has begun to appear the new economic history-or cliometrics-has firmly established itself in the profession. The astounding publicity that accompanied the publication of Robert Fogel and Stanley Engerman's Time on the Cross, the startling revisionism of their conclusions, and the vigor- ous debate that followed have awakened even the most somnolent historians to the existence of a new approach to economic history.
