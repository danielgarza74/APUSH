Neighbor against Neighbor: The Inner Civil War in the Randolph County Area of Confederate North Carolina By William T. Auman* Historians of the Civil War period in North Carolina have noted that desertion and disloyalty appeared early in Randolph and surrounding counties, but they have had little to say about anti-Confederate strength there.1 This study takes a detailed look at the wartime events in Ran dolph and, to a lesser degree, in the adjoining counties of Davidson, Moore, Montgomery, Guilford, and Chatham in an effort to uncover not only what happened but also to understand the basis and extent of the disaffection. It argues that the "Randolph County area"2 was a hotbed of desertion, disloyalty, and persistent Unionism during the war and that the discontent increased as the war progressed.3 Further, it contends that three underlying causes accounted for the radical anti-Confederate stance taken by many of the people of the Randolph area: social class antipa thies, staunch Unionism, and cultural factors. *Mr. Auman is enrolled in the graduate program in history at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. A somewhat longer version of this paper was presented at the meeting of the Southern Historical Association at Memphis, Tennessee, on November 5, 1982. The term "Ran dolph area" embraces all of Randolph County and adjoining portions of the adjacent coun ties, i.e., roughly the southern half of Guilford, the western half of Chatham, the north western quadrant of Moore, the northern half of Montgomery, and the eastern half of Davidson. The Quaker Belt was comprised of the counties of Randolph, Alamance, Chatham, Davidson, Davie, Forsyth, Guilford, Surry, and Yadkin as well as southwest Orange, northwest Moore, northern Montgomery, northeast Iredell, eastern Wilkes, eastern Alleghany, and western Stokes coun ties. This region, a hotbed of Unionism and Confederate desertion and disloyalty, was popu lated by Quakers, Moravians, Lutherans, German Reformed, Dunkards, and Wesleyans who were pacifists and abolitionists. The Randolph area formed the eastern half of the so-called "Quaker Belt" of central North Carolina. Eastern North Carolina had a sizable Quaker presence in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, but by the Civil War only small numbers of Quakers remained in the eastern counties of Wayne, Northampton, Hertford, Perquimans, and Sampson because of migra tion to the piedmont and to the Old Northwest. By 1860 the central North Carolina counties of Alamance, Chatham, Randolph, Guilford, Yadkin, and Surry had the largest concentra tions of Quakers, while the counties of Stokes, Iredell, Davie, Forsyth, Davidson, Moore, Montgomery, Orange, and Alleghany had relatively smaller numbers.
