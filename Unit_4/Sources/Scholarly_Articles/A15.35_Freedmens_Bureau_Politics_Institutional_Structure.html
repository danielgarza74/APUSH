Lincoln's act transformed the Civil War from a sectional rebellion to a war of lib- eration. Although the proclamation's immediate legal effect was minimal, since it touched only those areas still resisting Union authority, its political effect was far reaching and dramatic. Despite their new legal status, they were faced at the war's end with a social structure that regarded them as inferior, treating them with contempt at best and with violent fury at worst. In emancipating the slaves and overturning the economic and social basis of southern society, the federal government shouldered the responsibility of finishing what it had begun, making the freedmen citizens. To fulfill this responsibility, the government created the Bureau of Refu- gees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands, better known as the Freedmen's Bureau. The bureau constituted an experiment in social policy as well as in institutional development. It was, in essence, the first federal antipoverty program, the first attempt to impose broad federal authority over individual citizens through bureaucratic means. The bureau was intended to perform a comprehensive array of tasks aimed at providing sustenance in the short run and promoting economic independence in the long run. It was to pur- sue these goals not by relying on local governments or private institutions but by creating a federal bureaucracy with new powers to provide benefits to a disadvantaged minority. The Freedmen's Bureau ultimately failed to achieve the long-term aims of its proponents and of the freedmen themselves; it was unable to pro- tect the freedmen's political and economic independence. After 1877, while southern blacks retained their legal freedom, they were gradually disenfran- chised and remained tied to the land in a repressive system of tenancy and crop liens that differed little from slavery except in name. Yet the federal government, from a position of apparently unassailable strength, had taken unprecedented measures to alter the South's political landscape. This grand historical view of failure, however, does not consider what the bureau actu- ally accomplished. When judged by standards of bureaucratic effectiveness, the bureau's failure does not appear so complete. Bureaucratic success is defined here as the effective performance of the specific tasks that an orga- nization is mandated to perform, such as delivering relief to prevent starva- tion, providing education, or protecting civil rights. These tasks may be in the service of a larger goal that motivates both politicians and administra- tors, but it is the tasks and not the goal that define what an organization does from day to day. Success and failure in the performance of specific tasks are relative; no organization will do everything right all the time.
