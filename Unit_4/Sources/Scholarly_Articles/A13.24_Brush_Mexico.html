PIERO GLEIJESES A Brush with Mexico On 13 May 1846—two days after President James Polk had informed Congress that Mexican troops had crossed the Rio Grande and that American blood had been shed on American soil—the Washington National Intelligencer published a letter from a Whig representative from Maine, Luther Severance: "It is ... on Mexican soil that blood has been shed. The war has not been commenced by Mexico but by the President of the United States, without the authority of Congress and without any necessity."1 It is well known that Polk's war message provoked a fierce, albeit brief, debate in Congress and in the press. John M. Clayton (Whig-DE) spoke for many in the Senate when he said, "The whole conduct of the Executive ... has been utterly unjustifiable. If the acts of the Executive do not amount to acts of war, they are acts which necessarily tended to provoke war, and to bring on war, and that without consulting Congress." And Garrett Davis (Whig-KY) spoke for many in the House when he concluded, "It is our own President who began this war. He has been carrying it on for months in a series of acts.'" Davis was right. Polk had been pursuing a belligerent policy toward Mexico since the fall of 1845, when Mexico had agreed to receive a U.S. envoy to discuss the annexation of Texas to the United States. But where were Severance, Clayton, Davis, and the other members of Congress who expressed such outrage in May during the preceding months? Did they voice their opposition while Polk carried out the "series of acts" that, in Clayton's words, "necessarily tended to provoke war"? And was this opposition echoed in the newspapers that harshly criticized Polk's war message after it was sent to Congress? Or were Polk's moves so stealthy that no one could see the drift to war? 224 : D I P L O M A T I C HISTORY that preceded the conflict is provided in John Schroeder's excellent Mr. Polk's War: American Opposition and Dissent, 1846-1848, which notes merely that "Polk's Mexican activities were neglected by politicians of both parties as the nation drifted toward war." A few Whig and Democratic leaders "were dis turbed by the situation along the border, but chose to remain silent."4 Why did they remain silent? In the months that preceded the war, Polk's key moves toward Mexico were relatively transparent. He sent a minister to nego tiate with Mexico and by early 1846 it was public knowledge that the mission was stalemated; he ordered General Zachary Taylor's army to the Rio Grande and by early March his order was public knowledge. The drift to war was like a slow-motion fall. Why, then, were Polk's activities "neglected" by those politicians and news papers editors who later opposed the war? Was it because they were preoccu pied with the danger of war with England over Oregon? Was it because they thought victory over Mexico would be easy? Did they feel no moral outrage over the prospect of a war of aggression against a weak neighbor? And did they not have even a hint of anxiety that war with Mexico could deepen the sectional conflict? An examination of the domestic debate in the months that led to war sheds light on Polk's freedom of maneuver as he developed his policy toward Mexico. It also clarifies the United States' responsibility for the war. A narrow focus— on the brouhaha that followed Polk's 11 May message—may be comforting. It indicates that Americans rose in moral outrage to protest the administration's violence against the weak neighbor—but it masks the larger issue: where was the opposition when it could have made a difference, before American blood had been shed "on American soil"? This essay will focus on the debate in the American press and the U.S. Con gress over relations with Mexico from the fall of 1845 until 11 May 1846. In order to provide the necessary context, I begin by describing the Mexican American quarrel from its inception through the outbreak of hostilities. I con clude the essay with a reassessment of the responsibility for the war of the different protagonists: Polk, his critics at home, and Mexico. James K.
