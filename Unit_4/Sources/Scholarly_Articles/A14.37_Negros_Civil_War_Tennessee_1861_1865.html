Selection Overview

Lovett argues that Black Tennesseans helped win their own freedom and Union victory. Although Confederate Tennessee drafted free Black men for military labor in 1861 and masters expected loyalty, enslaved people escaped to Union camps by the hundreds, forcing Grant to adopt a contraband policy; Lovett contends the Union's better use of Black manpower made the difference between victory and defeat.

Lovett The State of Tennessee approved the Ordinance of Secession on June 8, 1861, and the Confederate States of America's flag was raised over the capitol at Nashville on June 16, 1861.1 Little did these white Tennesseans2 know that their action would be reversed by the Union Army some eight months later. And little did they know that the despised and socially unwanted slave was destined to aid in the realization of the destruction of the peculiar institution of slavery. These slaves made their contributions under the most adverse conditions of racism and physical punishment that most human beings can endure. This article is intended to demonstrate the firm and postive contributions of black Tennesseans in obtaining freedom, their own dignity as men and women, and military victory for the United States of America. Such a demonstration is fitting and proper in light of our bicentennial celebrations for 1976. Confederate Tennessee tried to exploit black labor to a great extent in its efforts to defend against approaching Yankee armies in late 1861. The state of Tennessee did impress some blacks into building forts and other fortifications, but by the winter of 1862 Tennessee had fallen into Union hands and the state government fled to Memphis.3 As early as the spring of 1861, Memphis newspapers were publishing suggestions that free blacks be organized to help defend against the Union Army. On June 28, 1861, the Tennessee State General Assembly passed the first act in the country to draft blacks for military service. The act had the following provisions: (1) All free black males between the ages of fifteen and fifty were eligible for the draft into military labor units; (2) each laborer was to receive eighteen dollars, clothing and rations each month; and (3) sheriffs were to arrest those who refused to serve, and charge these persons with a punishable misdemeanor.4 The quasifree blacks of Tennessee found themselves legislated into the Civil War whether they liked it or not. The Tennessee White's paternalistic illusions (see Eugene Genovese, Roll Jordan Roll, 1974, on paternalism) led him to believe falsely that all slaves would remain loyal to their masters during the American Civil War. Tennesseans believed that blacks were "lazy, stupid, and unfortunate beings" who would perish without their masters and mistresses to look after them. THE NEGRO'S CIVIL WAR 37 some free blacks of the City of Nashville offered their services to the Confederate cause in April of 1861 .5 Nathan Bedford Forrest6 and other Confederate officers used their own slaves and also captured blacks for use as teamsters and servants in the Confederate Army. Generals did not trust blacks as teamsters with their supplies and arms because blacks were known to be Union spies, and to run straight for the Yankee lines if they got half the chance. The Confederacy, including Tennessee, began too late to realize that if it could prevent desertion of whites, secure enough mules, stop the planting of cotton, and secure all Negroes between the ages of eighteen and forty that the Mississippi Valley could be secured against the Yankees. But blacks flocked to the Union lines and the culminating fact was that the United States Army and its volunteer units had a better organization for utilizing black resources.7 Such efficient utilization of that valuable source of manpower made the difference between victory and defeat for the United States. The slaves of Tennessee, in spite of the notorious slave patrols (paddyrollers) and the threat of starvation, flocked to the Union Army encampments by the hundreds. The onlV welcome General Ulysses Grant and the Union Army received when they marched into Clarksville was from the crowds of ragged but jubilant blacks.8 Union officers tried to send most of the fugitive slaves back to their masters because the army had not yet developed an official policy concerning slavery. Slave masters tried to scare the slaves by telling them that Yankees "kill all niggers".9 However, these obstacles were not enough to stem the human tide that began to overflow from Tennessee's plantations and elsewhere. The increasing numbers of fugitive slaves forced the Union Army to make some use of them. In a letter to General Grant, some citizens of Clarksville complained that on Sunday, Sep- tember 7, 1862, a detachment of 200 men from Fort Donelson took over the town and permitted stolen goods to be distributed to Negroes. They also claimed that some 200 of their slaves were sworn into the federal service (laborers) without their permission.10 General Ulysses S. Grant and the Union Army were forced by the need of labor and the ever-present sea of black faces to develop a contraband policy, despite the fact that he and General H.W. Halleck, the commander in the West, felt that the black problem was no concern of the military. With the approval of the Secretary of War, General Grant ordered John Eaton, a former Chaplain of the 27th Ohio Infantry Regiment, to set up a camp for contrabands.
