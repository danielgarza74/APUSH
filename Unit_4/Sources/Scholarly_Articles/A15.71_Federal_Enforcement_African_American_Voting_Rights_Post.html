The 1879 Louisiana state constitution did not disfranchise the freedmen. Hence there was the ongoing possibility that the federal government would intervene to protect the black franchise. Whites also considered the possibility that they could manipulate the black vote as they believed the Republican Party had done during Reconstruction. Continuing access to federal patronage, moreover, demonstrated that the Republican Party in Louisiana was not entirely defunct.3 The "elimination" of the black vote, as Michael Perman labels it, was a gradual process that evolved over a twenty-year period. Ballot box stuffing, a voter registration process rife with fraud, and a secret ballot that served as a literacy test all served to discourage African American voters as their numbers diminished.4 By the time the southern states rewrote their constitutions around the turn of the century—1898 for Louisiana—the U. S. Supreme Court had cleared the way for constitutional disfranchisement with its pro segregation decisions in Plessy v. Ferguson and Williams v. Mississippi.5 This paper analyzes the federal judiciary's response to electoral violence in Louisiana during the immediate post-Reconstruction period and confirms that the Republican national government's commitment to African American voting rights did not end with the Compromise of 1877. Nor had the Supreme Court's pinched interpretation of the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments in U. S. v. Cruikshank and U. S. v. Reese foreclosed the possibility of successful prosecution in voting rights cases.
