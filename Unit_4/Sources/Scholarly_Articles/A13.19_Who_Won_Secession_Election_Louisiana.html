Selection Overview

Dew explains why Louisiana seemed the Lower South state most attached to the Union in 1860, citing New Orleans's northern business ties, immigrant voters, sugar planters' stake in the tariff, and hill farmers' Unionism. Because official returns of the January 7, 1861, convention election were never made public, he uses newly discovered manuscript returns to test whether Secessionists truly won.

Who Won the Secession Election in Louisiana? By CHARLES B. DEW O F ALL THE STATES IN THE LOWER SOUTH, LOUISIANA SEEMED the most firmly attached to the Union in 1860. New Orleans, the South's largest and most cosmopolitan city, had strong commercial ties with the upper Mississippi Valley, and her shipping and financial activities were closely associated with the major eastern ports, New York in particular. In addition, the city's business elite had a large and prominent northern-born element, and many of these influential businessmen were, at best, lukewarm to the secessionist cause.' New Orleans' sizable immigrant popu- lation also displayed a notable lack of enthusiasm for secession during the 1850s. In the opinion of Senator John Slidell, the master manipulator of the state Democratic organization, the Irish and the Germans in the Crescent City were "at heart abolitionists."2 The radical cause had much more support in rural areas of the state, but even here the dissidents were numerous. Secessionist sentiment ran strongest in the cotton parishes along the Mississippi and Red rivers, districts characterized by large plantations and a heavy concentration of slaves. In the Delta parishes of southern Louisiana, however, the protective federal tariff on sugar tem- pered enthusiasm for secession, and the yeomen farmers in the northern part of the state, like their counterparts elsewhere in the southern hill country, also showed a strong strain of Unionism.3 In October 1860 when Governor William H. SECESSION ELECTION IN LOUISIANA 19 Lincoln were elected, Governor Thomas 0. Moore replied in terms that reflected the state's decidedly mixed feelings on se- cession. Even if the Republicans won in November, "I shall not advise the secession of my state," Moore wrote, "& I will add that I do not think the people of Louisiana will ultimately decide in favor of that course."4 Less than three months after Governor Moore made his pre- diction, the people of Louisiana voted in a statewide election to leave the Union. Or so it appeared. The election held on January 7, 1861, for delegates to a state convention apparently resulted in a victory for the Secessionist forces, since a majority of the win- ning candidates were pledged to support the state's unconditional withdrawal from the Union. But doubts about the secession elec- tion have persisted, in part because of the previous strength of Unionism in the state but primarily because the official election returns were not made public either at that time or subsequently. Not only have we lacked proof that the Secessionists really won a majority of the popular vote and, if they did win, what their margin of victory was; we have not even known the names of all the candidates.5 The recent discovery of the manuscript returns of this crucial election makes it possible to answer these questions and settle the long-standing controversy over the only referendum ever held in Louisiana on the question of secession. The actual count in the January 7 election is published here for the first time. Louisiana took her first formal steps toward disunion shortly after Lincoln's victory. On November 19, 1860, Governor Moore, apparently under the influence of his political patron, John Slidell, abruptly reversed his earlier stand and issued a call for a special legislative session to consider the critical situation then facing the state.'
