On the Front Line of Freedom: Freedmen's Bureau Officers and Agents in Reconstruction Georgia, 1865-1868 By Paul A. Cimbala Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands, established on March 3, 1865, was an extraordinary War Department agency born of the expansion of federal power during the Civil War and the Union's desire to do something for the South's emancipated slaves. There was no precedent for such a government agency potentially capable of influencing a wide range of affairs previously left outside of Washington's sphere. The Freedmen's Bureau's charge to oversee all subjects relating to the ex-slaves presented its officers and agents with the opportunity to challenge old notions of labor, civil rights, and other aspects of the South's racial status quo. Consequently the Bureau was in a position to play a crucial role in the im- plementation of Reconstruction policy, translating directives, laws, and constitutional guarantees into the reality promised by emancipation.1 To understand such a role, one must comprehend the mo- tives and actions of the agents and officers who served in the agency, in this case at the state and local level in Georgia. Equally significant is an understanding of how the service influ- enced their own lives. Certainly, many Georgia civilian agents served the interests of their white neighbors and both Yankee agents and officers fell prey to corruption, racism, or the desire to establish their own competence as planters. Regardless, many had a desire to see justice done; some even had a strong sense of mission. Those who worked for the Bureau's goals found themselves living a lonely, often dangerous existence. And enough expressed regret when they left their work unfinished to suggest that they believed the Bureau's job was one worth doing. In Georgia, according to some scholars, changes in the Freedmen's Bureau's assistant commissioners - the officers in charge of the agency at the state level - signaled shifts in the agency's approach to carrying out its charge. General Rufus Saxton supervised Bureau affairs in Georgia from May 20, 1865 until his successor General Davis Tillson assumed command of most of the state on September 22, 1865. Critics of Tillson have judged this change as a transition from an administration com- mitted to the freedpeople's best interest to one that favored planters' labor needs over the ex-slaves' rights.
