WAS THE EMANCIPATED SLAVE A PROLETARIAN? Alex Lichtenstein Over three decades ago, James McPherson wrote in The Negro's Civil War (1965) that "The Negro was not merely a passive recipient of the benefits conferred upon him by the war. Negro orators and writers provided leader- ship in the struggle for emancipation and equal rights. Blacks were active in the movements to bring education, suffrage, and land to southern freedmen. And perhaps most important of all, the contribution of Negro soldiers helped the North win the war." Conspicuously absent from this list of African- American contributors to freedom, however, were the slaves themselves.1 Following the lead of historians of slavery, who during the 1960s and 1970s returned those in bondage to center stage in accounts of the peculiar institution, American historians over the past two decades have begun to take a fresh look at the role of slaves in the Civil War and the process of emancipation. This reevaluation of the transition from slavery to freedom has been closely allied with the ongoing effort to rewrite the social history of Reconstruction. Two scholarly projects in particular have set the tone for the new work in emancipation studies. The benchmark for nearly all recent practitioners in the field remains Eric Foner's magisterial study of the social and political transformations wrought by black freedom, Reconstruction: America's Unfin- ished Revolution, 1863-1877 (1988). Based on nine years of research, this work laid to rest once and for all the unquiet ghost of the Dunning school and a once deeply influential Reconstruction historiography that portrayed emanci- pated African Americans as manipulated dupes of vindictive Northerners, utterly unprepared for the tasks and responsibilities of freedom. Indeed, this work placed black agency-especially that of the former slaves-at the very center of the momentous social upheaval that accompanied African-Ameri- can freedom at its birth.2 Foner's Walter Fleming lectures, published as Nothing But Freedom: Emancipation and Its Legacy (1983), brought a compara- tive perspective to bear on the study of emancipation as a social process for the first time. Radical Reconstruction in the American South stood out from all other emancipations in an era that saw many, for only there did former slaves "enjoy universal manhood suffrage and a real measure of political power."3 Reviews in American History 26 (1998) 124-145 © 1998 by The Johns Hopkins University Press

LICHTENSTEIN / Was the Emancipated Slave a Proletarian? 125 At the same time, however, Foner's work has demonstrated how "Recon- struction emerges as a decisive moment in fixing the dominant understanding of freedom as self-ownership and the right to compete in the labor market, rather than propertied independence." Few historians have done more than Foner to use the drama of slave emancipation to illuminate the narrowing conception of freedom in a society increasingly devoted to the market as its arbiter, or to portray the freedpeople themselves as full and worthy human participants in this drama. "In being forced to deal with freedom as a matter of concrete policy," Foner proclaimed in his 1994 Presidential address to the Organization of American Historians, "Americans were compelled to recog- nize how thoroughly their own society had changed" during the course of the nineteenth century's market revolution.4 Complementing Foner's oeuvre is the collective endeavor of the Freedmen and Southern Society Project (FSSP), begun in 1976 and published so far in four volumes as Freedom: A Documentary History of Emancipation (1982-1993).5 Housed at the University of Maryland and overseen by historians Ira Berlin and Leslie Rowland, this genuinely collaborative project has drawn on the talents of a diverse array of scholars who have helped shape the field with a small library of their own articles and monographs.6 Together they have produced a multi-volume work that lends enormous documentary weight to the emerging interpretation of emancipation as a process generated by the actions of slaves as much as by Abraham Lincoln, the Union Army, or black and white abolitionists. Both Foner and the FSSP, in turn, trace their legacy back to W.E.B. Du Bois's Black Reconstruction in America, 1860-1880 (1935). In that work, ignored by the historical profession at least until the 1960s, Du Bois began his chapter on the Civil War, entitled "The General Strike," with this provocation: "When Edmund Ruffin ... fired the first gun at Fort Sumter he freed the slaves."7 Remarkably, Black Reconstruction rested almost entirely on published sources, many of them less than sympathetic to Du Bois's radical interpretation. The work of the FSSP has now assured for posterity that anyone with access to a library will find at hand ample documentation of Du Bois's prophetic account of slaves as contributors to their own liberation and the Civil War as the opening salvo in a noble but stunted struggle for interracial democracy. By fleeing to union lines the slaves "acted resolutely to place their freedom ... on the wartime agenda," the editors of Freedom write.
