Selection Overview

Phillips argues that the usual story of Federal policy moving from conciliation to hard war late in the conflict does not fit the western Border States. In Kentucky and especially Missouri, Union troops confiscated property, suppressed newspapers, and even killed alleged disloyalists early on, as when Iowa soldiers shot a young man over a secession flag at Stewartsville in June 1861.

christopher phillips Lincoln’s Grasp of War Hard War and the Politics of Neutrality and Slavery in the Western Border States, 1861–1862 For nearly two decades, historians of the American Civil War have ascribed an evolution of Federal war policy toward civilians in the slave states. According to this interpretation, both Abraham Lincoln and his command- ers initially believed the war required a limited Federal military incursion into the slaveholding states to restore order, and with it the Union. For the fi rst year of the confl ict, the Lincoln administration and military com- manders stayed the “hard hand of war,” implementing a benign Federal strategy that historian Mark Grimsley has termed conciliation. Less a coherent military policy than a general sentiment, conciliation (or as historian Stephen V. Ash has termed it, the “rosewater policy”) derived ostensibly from Federal war makers’ collective determination to assist the perceived loyalist southern majority, believing that the mass of white slave state residents were loyal yet cowed by an aggressive secessionist minor- ity. Military and political goals and strategies entwined as the vagaries of the ground war forced the commander in chief and leading war makers to broaden their once limited defi nitions of military necessity, none more written about than emancipation. Only when battlefi eld reverses failed to end the war did Federal authorities expand the confl ict against disloyal civilians in the Confederate states, fi rst by interfering with their “domestic institutions”—the most important being slavery—and later by initiating a “hard war” that included the destruction of civilian property and the uni- versal emancipation of slaves. 1 The wartime experience of the Border States, the fi rst slave states that Federal troops reached, both assists and complicates this interpretation. The historian William Gienapp has attributed the long-held “hands-off ” theory of the Lincoln administration’s delicate handling of these states— as well as historians’ general inattention to the actual wartime conditions in them—to the president’s careful approach to two critical events: seces- sion and emancipation. Nowhere was this more evident than in the westernmost of these states, Kentucky and especially Missouri. Early on June 15, 1861, a detachment of the 2nd Iowa Volunteers debarked from a commandeered train at Stewartsville, Missouri, a sleepy northwest Missouri rail town. The unit was nearing the end of a two-day rail expedition ordered by Samuel R. Curtis to secure the Hannibal & St. Joseph line against reputed saboteurs. At several prior points, Curtis reported encountering those he called rebels, confi scating powder and secession fl ags, suppressing a disloyal newspaper, arresting disloyalists, and fi ring on others who fl ed. Within minutes of arriving at Stewartsville, one of the Iowans shot to death an alleged disloyalist, nineteen-year-old Donald M. McDonald. Later reports alleged that a displayed secession fl ag had triggered the shooting. The incident mirrored the previous month’s much-publicized shooting of Illinoisan Elmer Ellsworth, a Federal offi cer and a former law student of the president, by a secessionist in Alexandria, Virginia. Yet this latest killing occurred in an ostensibly loyal state. In his offi cial report, Curtis declared that the “homicide” resulted from the man attempting to fi re a revolver at his troops, a charge disputed by onlook- ers, but the detachment’s commander, Lt. Col. James M. Tuttle, averred that his soldiers were informed of the fl ag while still in Hannibal and they determined then to kill this disloyalist. Charges of premeditation in the young Missourian’s death appeared to have little troubled Tuttle. “Perhaps I am responsible,” the Iowan defi antly asserted years later when confronted publicly about the incident. “If so, I have nothing to take back. Our busi- ness down there was to put down the rebel colors and of course we com- menced as soon as we saw where the work commenced. . . . His fl ag came down and so did he.” 3 The hard warfare early and widely evident in these slave states derived from more than Federal military personnel’s collective belief in the coercive authority of a democratically elected government to discipline and punish disloyal citizens alongside enemy combatants.
