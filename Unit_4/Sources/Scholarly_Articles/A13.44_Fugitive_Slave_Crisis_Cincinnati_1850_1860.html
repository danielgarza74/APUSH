THE FUGITIVE SLAVE CRISIS IN CINCINNATI, 1850-1860: RESISTANCE, ENFORCEMENT, AND BLACK REFUGEES By Stephen Middleton* Article IV Section 2 of the U.S. Constitution made fugitive slave legislation possi- ble. The article provided for the capture and removal of fugitives from labor. The federal Fugitive Slave Law of 1793 enacted the clause when it approved the rendi- tion of escaped slaves, and made it a crime to harbor or conceal alleged runaways.' The Fugitive Slave Law (FSL) went almost without notice for four decades. When the antislavery movement accelerated during the 1830s, its leaders con- demned the law, claiming that it went beyond the scope intended by the framers. The Founding Fathers, they contended, recognized that this provision could only be implemented by individual states. Yet, Congress seized the opportunity in 1793 to legislate on slavery.2 In recent years, historians have also criticized the legislation. They claim its am- biguity influenced free states to implement the law. Northern states cooperated with slaveholders by providing for the employment of public officers to arrest and try runaway slaves. But there was nothing in the article which mandated this action. Thus, it is no wonder that this initiative brought on the wrath of abolitionists by the 1830s, especially those in southern Ohio.3 Cincinnati abolitionists James Gillespie Birney and Salmon Portland Chase led a cadre of attorneys who launched a legal assault on the FSL.4 These abolitionists continued their protest in ensuing years, and after September 18, 1850 when Congress adopted the second FSL, they voiced strong objections. The editors of the Dayton Evening Empire wrote: "There seems to be a determination in certain quarters to resist the operation of the law recently passed by Congress, for the apprehension of fugitive slaves and their restoration to their owners."5 The framers of the FSL had drafted the legislation to provide a more uniform method for the capture and removal of runaway slaves. Before 1850, antislavery litigation in the North had led to the creation of liberated zones, thereby weakening the first FSL. The second FSL was designed to overcome this deficiency by: 1) the appointment of federal marshals who would capture runaways; and 2) the installa- tion of federal commissioners who would review claims for alleged slaves. * Stephen Middleton is an Assistant Professor of History at North Carolina State University, Raleigh, North Carolina.

FUGITIVE SLAVE CRISIS 21 Cincinnatians vigorously opposed the FSL; and the law did little to discourage blacks who escaped from slavery, seeking refuge in the North. Nevertheless, federal officers strictly enforced the legislation. Hence, opposition to the FSL of 1850, the escape of slaves, and the zealous pursuit of them by slaveholders, created tension in Cincinnati throughout the 1850s. Cincinnatians considered the new law more obnoxious than its predecessor. Local newspapers reported the mood of the state upon adoption of the second FSL. In Cincinnati, explained the Enquirer, the law produced a "good deal of comment [and generated] much excitement in some quarters."' The editors of the Clermont Cou- rier considered it "a joke" that passage of the law settled the slavery question. Any attempt to enforce its provisions would meet "concerted evasion" or "overt resis- tance". The law would never succeed in returning even "one-tenth of the fugitive slaves living in free states."7 Other quarters of the state also denounced the FSL. Abolitionists in Cleveland adopted resolutions which declared the law unconstitutional and "demanded its im- mediate and unconditional repeal."8 Abolitionists also organized in Dayton, Mayes- ville, and surrounding counties to condemn the law. At these meetings, the Empire reported, the sentiments voiced "were of the most violent and inflammatory charac- ter, [encouraging] opposition to the law to the death."O The FSL was the catalyst for the protest. Its framers had not appreciated the transformation which had taken place in Ohio since the 1840s. Before, slaveholders had routinely brought slaves into Ohio (while visiting, in transit, or hired-out) who were unaffected by its constitutional prohibition against slavery or proscription in the Northwest Ordinance of 1787. Ohio seldom extended such considerations after 1841 when state courts adopted its law of freedom, which automatically freed slaves when they entered the state. Ohio courts did not originate this rule of law. Judge Mansfield announced the doctrine in England in 1772 when Charles Stuart brought his slave James Sommersett into the commonwealth. A few American courts adopted the rule in 1836.10 Before 1841, the authorities in Cincinnati had routinely remanded runaway slaves. In 1827, for example, Frank's Kentucky master had leased his services in Cincinnati. Frank claimed his freedom when he learned of the legal principle of Judge Mansfield who had freed Sommersett in England. Nevertheless, the Common Pleas Court remanded him to slavery after his eight-year residence in Cincinnati."1 In 1837, Matilda Lawrence suffered the same fate when brought into Cincinnati by a Missouri slaveholder.12 During the decades which followed, antislavery lawyers prevailed on the courts and they sensed a metamorphosis. Salmon P. Chase, who directed the legal van- guard, felt misunderstood when "first entrusted with the standard of freedom and reform in Ohio." By 1850, after the transformation of Ohio courts, he felt vindi- cated claiming "the cause of freedom and reform" of slavery was winning accept- ance in the state.13 James G. Birney, who originated Ohio's antislavery law, immediately attacked the framers of the FSL. He denounced Henry Clay, who had backed the legislation, as one who compromised the "matter of slavery between the North and South."
