"Swelling That Great Tide of Humanity": The Concord, Massachusetts, Female Anti-Slavery Society SANDRA HARBERT PETRULIONIS N 1833, abolitionist leader William Lloyd Garrison predicted that "the destiny of the slaves is in the hands of the Ameri- can women, and complete emancipation can never take place without their co-operation." Yet it is likely that not even Garri- son foresaw the pivotal role women would play in his abolition- ist organization. Following the lead of British women a decade earlier, women in the United States began organizing in the 1830s; by 1838, hundreds of women's antislavery societies, with a total membership of over six thousand, were meeting regu- larly to plan local efforts that would yield tangible political re- sults in the war to end American slavery. From Cincinnati to Boston to Salem to Philadelphia, and numerous small towns in between, women made their voices heard. Only recently, how- ever, have scholars begun to scrutinize the specific accomplish- ments of these organizations and to identify and credit individ- ual women for their groundbreaking activism.' 386 THE NEW ENGLAND QUARTERLY In her recent comprehensive study of the female antislavery movement, Julie Roy Jeffrey has shown that despite the fact that large, urban organizations claimed more members, many small women's groups exerted a significant influence.2 Yet sur- prisingly, one of the most influential and active of all the female antislavery societies has received virtually no scholarly attention to date, nor does Jeffrey mention it. The Concord, Massachu- setts, Female Anti-Slavery Society boasted the wives, sisters, mothers, friends, aunts, and neighbors of men whose public support eventually directed worldwide political and philosophi- cal attention to the antislavery cause. Abolitionist historiogra- phy routinely invokes the names of Henry David Thoreau, Ralph Waldo Emerson, Amos Bronson Alcott, and others when recounting Concord's abolitionist past; but adequate recogni- tion of the women who urged them forward is long overdue. Two works that provide the most thorough coverage of the soci- ety-Joan Trumbull's 1942 thesis "Concord and the Negro" and Wendell Glick's 1950 dissertation "Thoreau and Radical Abolitionism: A Study of the Native Background of Thoreau's Social Philosophy"-remain unpublished.3 2Julie Roy Jeffrey, The Great Silent Army of Abolitionism: Ordinary Women in the Antislavery Movement (Chapel Hill: University of North Carolina Press, 1998). 3In particular, the biographers of Henry D. Thoreau, Lidian (Mrs. Ralph Waldo) Emerson, and Abigail "Abby" (Mrs. Amos Bronson) Alcott mention the organization, as do a handful of works chronicling nineteenth-century Concord. See Walter Harding, The Days of Henry Thoreau (1962; reprinted, Princeton: Princeton University Press, 1992); Ellen Tucker Emerson, The Life of Lidian Jackson Emerson, ed. Delores Bird Carpenter (Boston: Twayne, 1980; reprinted, East Lansing: Michigan State University Press, 1992); Cynthia Barton, Transcendental Wife: The Life of Abigail May Alcott (Lanham, Md.: University Press of America, Inc., 1996); Franklin B. Sanborn, "The Women of Concord IV: Mary Merrick Brooks and the Anti-Slavery Movement" (1906), reprinted in Transcendental Epilogue: Primary Materials for Research in Emerson, Thoreau, Literary New England, the Influence of German Theology, and Higher Bibli- cal Criticism, 3 vols., ed. Kenneth Walter Cameron (Hartford: Transcendental Books, 1965-82), 3:44-47; Len Gougeon, Virtue's Hero: Emerson, Antislavery, and Reform (Athens: University of Georgia Press, 1990o); Gary Collison, Shadrach Minkins: From Fugitive Slave to Citizen (Cambridge: Harvard University Press, 1997), pp. 151-58. Gougeon credits the influence on Emerson of Mary Merrick Brooks, the society's most persevering member, while Collison narrates one of the most dramatic episodes in the society's history-its members' overnight vigil with the runaway slave Minkins.
