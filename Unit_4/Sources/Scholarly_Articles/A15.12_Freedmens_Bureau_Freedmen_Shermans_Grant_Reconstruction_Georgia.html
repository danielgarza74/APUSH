CIMBALA F OR A BRIEF PERIOD DURING 1865 AND 1866 A SMALL PORTION OF Georgia's black population held on to parcels of land along the Savan- nah River, in the Ogeechee district of Chatham County, and on the barrier islands along the coast. Encouraged by General William T. Sherman's Special Field Orders, No. 15, which in January 1865 had set aside the land for their exclusive use, these blacks organized com- munities and planted rice and cotton free from the commands of old masters or new white bosses. The Freedmen's Bureau mediated the restoration process and thereby played a central role in the resolution of the land question on the Georgia coast. Consequently, the agency provides a clear focus for understanding both the process by which the government rescinded the promise of land and the coastal freedmen's increasing estrangement from their northern emancipators. The bureau, as the government's enforcer of an unpopular policy, does not fare well in most assessments of its performance in the coastal drama, whether contemporary or modern, history or fiction.3 A reading of the events that transpired within the Georgia reservation, the area of land set aside for the freedmen, suggests that criticism of the bureau was not unwarranted. Needs defined by strangers were inadequate substi- tutes for black aspirations; the bureau's officers and agents clearly believed that they knew what was best for the freedmen and what was possible. Nevertheless, the bureau, like the freedmen it claimed to help, deserves more than a one-dimensional caricature. A broader, more detailed picture does more than polish the agency's reputation; it illustrates the tension that was inherent in the Reconstruction process. The Georgia coast was an avenue to freedom even before Sherman completed his famous march from Atlanta to the sea emerging from the Georgia interior in late 1864.
