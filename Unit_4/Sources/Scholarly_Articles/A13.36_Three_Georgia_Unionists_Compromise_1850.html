Three Georgia Unionists and the Compromise of 1850 By John T. Hubbell# ΓΊΓΛ HE United States Congress that convened in December, J^ 1849, was called upon to resolve one of the most critical issues in the histpry of the Republic. Born of war and nurtured by sectional malice, the crisis centered on the question of slavery, or more explicitly, the extension of slavery into the territories newly won from Mexico. Impassioned debate in the chambers of Congress was echoed in press and pulpit. Traditional party lines were endangered by emerging sectional alignments. "Free soil and free men" was the oft heard cry in the North; the disciples of Calhoun answered with talk of southern rights and dark threats of secession. Caught in the maelstrom were calmer men whose sectional loyalties were balanced by a desire to preserve the Union, though they too were sometimes moved to intemperate language. From Georgia came three men of this persuasion. Two of them, Alexander H. Stephens and Robert Augustus Toombs, were Whigs; the third, Howell Cobb, was a Democrat. Each was to play a vital role in that dramatic year of compromise. Cobb, scion of a prominent Athens, Georgia, family, was first elected to Congress in 1842. His constituency, in northeastern Georgia, was composed mainly of a small white-farmer element. Although they had little direct interest in slavery, they were keenly interested in state and national politics. Cobb shared their strongly Unionist sentiment to the extent of refusing to assist Calhoun in forming a Southern Rights party. Calhoun's aim, he said, was "to supplant in the south the democratic party. Stephens, chiefly remembered as the anti-Davis Vice-President of the Confederacy, entered the House in 1843. Initially his high- pitched voice provoked laughter, but his colleagues soon learned that his gaunt, almost skeletal figure housed an indomitable will and powerful intellect. An honor graduate of the University of Georgia and a skilled lawyer, his foremost goal in politics was the defense of what he deemed Constitutional rights, i.e., southern rights and interests.2 Robert Toombs, for some years a power in Georgia politics, first received national prominence during the winter of 1849- 1850 for his eloquent statements of southern policy. He, like Cobb, was the son of an eminent planter, and like Stephens attended the University of Georgia. But unlike Cobb and Stephens he did not graduate. Instead, he was asked to withdraw after what has been described as an "escapade." He subsequently completed his college education at Union College in New York. Toombs, for all his irascible qualities, was at home either in the drawing room or tavern, equally fond of good bourbon and the classics. He proved a powerful as well as colorful addition to the Georgia delegation to Congress.3 Presaging the legislative battle to come was the bitter contest for the Speakership. The House members, 112 Democrats, 105 Whigs, and 14 Free Soilers, were embroiled in sectional conflict from the first caucus. Stephens and Toombs, fearing an alliance of Free Soilers and northern Whigs, presented a resolution in the Whig caucus to the effect that Congress ought not interfere with slavery either in the territories or in the District of Columbia. Upon refusal of their fellows to entertain the resolution they bolted and thereafter refused to assist their party in organizing the House. Indeed, they proved to be an obstructive force.4 Neither of the two major parties could command a majority, as two splinter groups, the Free Soilers and the disaffected Whigs, held the balance of power in the House.
