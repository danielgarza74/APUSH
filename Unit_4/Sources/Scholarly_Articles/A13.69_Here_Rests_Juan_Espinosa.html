WEBER In a deserted graveyard somewhere in California, it is said that a tomb- stone bears the inscription: Aqui reposa Juan Espinosa; Nunca en su vida hizo otra cosa. (Here rests Juan Espinosa; That is all he has ever done.) In an article which appeared in the April 1978 issue of the WHQ, "Californios and the Image of Indolence" (p. 181), David J. Langum raises an important question regarding historical explanation: what caused Cali- fornios, such as the apocryphal Espinosa, to have an "image of indolence"? Langum's answer is only partially satisfactory. He correctly notes that Anglo- American visitors to California in the first half of the nineteenth century consistently described Spanish or Mexican males as an indolent people. Some historians, as Langum points out, have sought to explain this image of indolence by arguing that the Anglo-Americans' vision was distorted by their "militant Protestantism, Anglo-Saxon racism and Manifest Destiny." If these three ideas distorted Anglo vision and contributed to the popular image of indolence, then Langum seems to suppose that observers from other countries would see the Californios differently than did the Anglo-Americans. To test his supposition, Langum subjects the Anglo-American image of indolence to a "comparative analysis." He examined the accounts of pre- 1846 visitors to California and found that one Swede, one German, three Russians, six Englishmen, and seven Frenchmen also viewed the Californios as lazy. Thus Langum concludes that "by the persistence of a commonality of viewpoint regarding Califorio indolence, it becomes clear that Protes- tantism, Manifest Destiny, and Anglo-Saxon racism were not the causal agents of American attitudes toward Mexican Californians..." (p. 182). It seems to me that Langum has based his argument on a faulty assump- tion. Instead, it may be they were causal and that a commonality of the broad impulses of "religion, racism, and nationalism" (p. 181), which took the particular American form of Protestantism, Anglo-Saxon racism, and Manifest Destiny, influenced Eu- ropeans as well as Americans. Anglo-American attitudes toward Hispanic Californians seem to derive from a set of assumptions about Spaniards which Americans shared with their European forbears. Europeans and Americans alike have assumed that Spaniards possessed an unusual number of serious character defects such as extreme cruelty, treachery, pride, fanaticism, cowardliness, corruption, decadence, and authoritarianism. Perhaps the least of these was indolence. Some historians know these anti-Hispanic attitudes as the Black Legend.1 Although the roots of the Black Legend can be traced into fourteenth and fifteenth century Italy, Hispanophobia seems to have developed most fully in the sixteenth century as propaganda against Spain's highly successful imperialism. Anti-Hispanism seems to have reached its most virulent form among Protestants in northern Europe. In Germany and the Netherlands, anti-Hispanic propaganda represented a nationalistic and religious response to the domination of Spain's first Hapsburg monarch, the Holy Roman Emperor Charles I and V. In the late sixteenth century similar motives brought this anti-Spanish propaganda to England, where it served to arouse patriotic and religious fervor against the Protestant Queen Elizabeth's chief rival in the race for empire. The northern Europeans also gave the Hispanophobia a racist tinge by emphasizing the dark color of "black Spaniards" and using them as a foil against whom their presumed Nordic superiority could be enhanced. The Black Legend flourished in Catholic as well as Protestant countries, including Italy and France, Spain's rival and neighbor. The religious impulses behind the Black Legend cannot be reduced to a simple Protestant-Catholic dichotomy. Many French, German, and Italian Catholics came to view Spanish Catholicism as an aberrant form that had been tainted by centuries of exposure to Judaism. By the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, when Europeans began to visit California, anti-Hispanism had become well entrenched in Europe. Even philosophes, such as Voltaire and Montesquieu, seem to have accepted and furthered the Black Legend, aiding its spread throughout Europe. It came to America as well.
