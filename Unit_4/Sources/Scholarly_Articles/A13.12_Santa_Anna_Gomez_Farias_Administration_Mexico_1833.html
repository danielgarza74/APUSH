Selection Overview

Costeloe sets out to explain why Santa Anna betrayed the liberals' first attempt at reform during his first presidency. Tracing the 1832 revolt that began at Veracruz, he argues that Santa Anna posed as a mere mediator while seeking the presidency by arms, using the campaign against Bustamante's ministers as a pretext that might win favor with federalists who feared centralized power.

SANTA ANNA AND THE GOMEZ FARIAS ADMINISTRATION IN MEXICO, 1833-1834 DESPITE numerous biographies, Antonio L6pez de Santa Anna Sremains one of the most enigmatic characters to have emerged in nineteenth century Latin America. Often dismissed by his many detractors as an unprincipled opportunist in the worst military caudillo tradition, he nevertheless dominated the turbulent and often chaotic Mexican political scene in the thirty years between independence and the Reform. His life has naturally been subjected to close scrutiny by both his contemporaries and more recent historians but his genius for po- litical manouevre and equivocation have left many episodes in his career obscure and subject to doubt. Perhaps nowhere is this more the case than in his first presidency from 1833-1834 and in particular in his relations with his vice-president G6mez Farias' administration. This article seeks to examine his activities and ambitions in these years, and to suggest rea- sons, which hitherto have been overlooked or at least not given sufficient attention by his biographers, why he chose to betray and destroy the liberals' first attempt at reform.' Santa Anna's campaign for the presidency began at Veracruz on 2 January 1832. On that evening a plan was issued in the name of the local garrison commander, Pedro Landero, calling on vice-president Busta- mante to dismiss his cabinet.2 Article 3 stated the intention of the pro- nunciados to invite Santa Anna to assume military command. The next day he left his estate near Jalapa and hastened to Veracruz. On 4 January he wrote to Bustamante saying that he had been aware for some time of the opposition to the ministers, but he had now come to the conclusion that there was real public dislike and even hatred of them. Public opinion had to be obeyed, he declared, and he himself had received letters and exhortations for some time urging him to act. "Have no doubt', he wrote, "my sword will sustain at all cost the vice-president of the republic, D. Anastasio Bustamante."13 Santa Anna's altruistic claims to be no more than a mediator were re- ceived in the capital with even less credibility than the rebels' statement that their only aim was the removal of the cabinet. In retrospect it is clear that, having spent his days since the downfall of Guerrero in 1829 in retirement on his estate, he had concluded that he was to be the next president of the republic.4 He faced however, various difficulties. In strict- ly constitutional terms, the next presidential election was due to be held in the autumn of 1832, with the successful candidate taking office on 1 April 1833. There were two routes open to him; first, campaign and win the election, or secondly, achieve the same end by armed revolt. His de- cision not to rely on the electoral process reflected a corrct assessment of his own political support. Most of his partisans were in the lower ranks of the military, and within the party political spectrum included only those on the extreme fringe, the former yorkinos and Guerrero adher- ents. The latter had been mostly deprived of their public offices by the Bustamante government and hence they had no control or influence in the vital state legislatures which were to elect the next president. Perhaps an even more important consideration was that other candidates for the presidency, especially a group supporting General Mier y Ternn, were rapidly gaining organization and strength. They were attracting the al- legiance of the political moderates, both conservative and liberal, the so- called hombres de bien, who were growing disillusioned with the pro- military and pro-clerical policies of Lucas Alamain and his cabinet col- leagues. Santa Anna knew that he had no chance of electoral support from them. Hence he rejected reliance on the ballot box in favour of revolt but here timing was a crucial factor. He had to act quickly to forestall his more orthodox presidential rivals but in January 1832 it was too early to rebel openly in pursuit of the presidency. He needed a pretext for the time being and this was provided by the campaign that had already been underway for some months against the ministers, particularly against Alamain and Facio.
