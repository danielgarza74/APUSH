WILLIAM PARROTT, AMERICAN CLAIMS, AND THE MEXICAN WAR Peter M. Jonas The subject of claims is important in the history of relations between the United States and Mexico prior to the Mexican War. Mexico's stormy history following independence, its close proximity to the United States, and the presence of a considerable number of American citizens and businessmen in Mexico all strained relations between the two nations. A number of American claims accumulated and created controversy in diplomatic channels. Eventually, political and economic problems enshrouded foreign relations. William Stuart Parrott was an American who played a significant role as a private businessman and agent of the United States government. Parrott's business career and subsequent claims against the Mexican government were typical of the early and mid-nineteenth century history of American merchants in Mexico. Few Americans, however, became so involved in such important affairs. If Parrott had been either solely a entrepreneur or a diplomat, he might have succeeded. His attempts to run both in double harness were at the heart of his undoing as the demands of the two enterprises often clashed. Parrott was born around 1796 to a well-respected Virginia family. A respected and successful individual, he has remained relatively obscure, and even his contemporaries did not seem to have a clear impression of the man. The Parrott family emigrated to Halifax County, Virginia in 1800, settling into the serene and isolated western frontier. The majestic scenery, melancholy atmosphere, and rough lifestyle made a lasting impression on young William. Religion and education proved important elements of Parrott's early years; he took advantage of all available opportunities. Imbued with a wilderness spirit and supported by his family, William Parrott received a well-rounded education. His childhood was ordinary, simple, and filled with practical lessons in life. At the age of seventeen, stirred by war and inspired by a strong admiration for General Andrew Jackson, Parrott volunteered for service in the Tennessee militia with aspirations of fighting in the War of 1812. Jackson commanded this force, and the two men established a passing friendship.2 Serving as a private in the regiment, Parrott was young but performed admirably in the battle of New Orleans, evoking high praise from superior officers. The private was remembered for his bravery, his merits as a gentleman, and as a man who gallantly fought by the side of the future President Jackson. During the war, Parrott received a severe leg wound, which caused him to limp the for remainder of his life.3 Thus began his long, meritorious service in the government of the United States. In 1818, motivated by an adventuresome spirit and a vision of a new world, Parrott traveled in search of fortune and fame to Cuba, even though other Americans viewed the island as a "pest-hole" because of rampant disease. While realizing neither fortune nor fame, Parrott did acquire a fascination and appreciation for Spanish culture. After Parrott returned to the United States, he completed studies in dentistry sometime prior to 1822, then migrated to Mexico in order to practice his chosen profession.
