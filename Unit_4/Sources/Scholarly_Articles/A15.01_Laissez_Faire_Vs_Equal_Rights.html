By WILBERT H. AHERN Laissez Faire vs. Equal Rights: Liberal Republicans and Limits to Reconstruction T HE FAILURE of the Second Reconstruction to dissipate racial discrimi- nation or prejudice has made historians more aware of the tenacity of these American characteristics. This awareness has led to some val- uable correctives and some potential distortions. Reacting to a vision of the mid-nineteenth century "as an interlude of redeeming virtue... when white Americans ... put aside their racism," such scholars as Eu- gene Berwanger, Leon Litwack, Loren Ratner, V. Jacques Voegeli, and Forrest G. Wood have underscored the presence of prejudice. While racial antipathy was incontestably present, historians must not let this new emphasis obscure the real contrasts between the Reconstruction period and either the antebellum slave society or the height of jim crow and "respectable" racism of the early twentieth century. To do so is to miss the complexity of the dynamics of racism. The danger lies in equating race prejudice with racism and neglecting other variables than prejudice that contribute to patterns of discrimination along racial lines., An emphasis on race prejudice makes less surprising the failure of Reconstruction to secure freedom for the freedmen, but it does little to explain why a policy of equal rights went as far as it did. An examina- tion of the Liberal Republican movement of 1872 offers more insight into why Reconstruction went so far but no farther. Despite their failure in the presidential election, the Liberals proposed solutions for the future of the freedmen and for the relationship of the federal government with the South which received increasing support in the following years and bore a striking resemblance to the policy which President Rutherford B. Hayes instituted in 1877. Was their action an abandonment born of racial antipathy? The evidence suggests that their role was more para- doxical and reveals how a nonracist ideology assisted racism.2 The stalwart Republicans of 1872 first articulated the theme that the Liberals abandoned the Freedmen. LAISSEZ FAIRE VS. EQUAL RIGHTS 53 congressional investigation of the Ku Klux Klan, published in February, 1872, made the "Southern question" an important issue in 1872. The Grant administration's vulnerability to charges of corruption and ex- cessive partisanship and a public interest in national reconciliation led the Republicans to make the fate of the freedmen the foremost issue. Republicans charged that their Liberal opponents' policy of "recon- ciliation" and "local self-government" would return the freedmen to slavery in fact if not in name. Thomas Nast's mordant caricatures of Liberals and ex-Confederates joining hands over the corpses of the freed- men captured the thrust of Republican rhetoric.3 This charge, strengthened by the decline of the position of Southern blacks following Reconstruction, has led many historians to question the commitment of the Liberal Republican movement to equal rights. Recent historiography adopts the Stalwart charge that the Liberals "abandoned" the freedmen. They reversed themselves on Reconstruction, some scholars have argued, because of a growing disillusionment with the racial capacities of Freedmen, a fear of a social revolution, and/or despair with Southern governments. In fact, however, the 1872 action reflected no reversal. While evidence exists of some racist-tinged dis- illusionment or concern for social stability, these attitudes only reflected and confirmed Liberal assumptions that existed at the first stages of Reconstruction.4 Those whom the Stalwarts accused of "abandoning the freedmen" had been prominent figures in the founding of the Republican Party. Samuel Bowles, William Cullen Bryant, Horace Greeley, Whitelaw Reid, Theo- dore Tilton and Horace White were much younger and different men in the 1850s but from their varied locales and newspapers they worked for the emergence of the Republican party. More obviously behind a party for "free soil, free labor, free men" were such politicians as Francis W. Bird, Roeliff Brinkerhoff, Jacob D. Cox, B. Gratz Brown, Austin Blair, Jacob M. Howard, George W. Julian, Gustave Koerner, John M. Palmer, Carl Schurz, Charles Sumner and Lyman Trumball, all of whose rise to national prominence came through the Grand Old Party. Moreover, men who would join the Liberal movement in 1872 could point to a record of opposition to discrimination against black Americans that contrasted favorably to the overall record of the Republican party.
