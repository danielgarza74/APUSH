From Home Front to Battlefront: Brothers In The Civil War Jeremy Youngs, Independent Scholar T he prominent Civil War historian James M. McPherson writes that “One of the phrases often used to describe the American Civil War is ‘The Brothers’ War.’” 1 McPherson was referring to a divided nation and government, but he went on to assert “most of the men in a volunteer com- pany had enlisted from the same community or county. Many of them had known each other from childhood.” 2 In her study on death and the Civil War, Drew Gilpin Faust notes “most Civil War combatants were very like one another-metaphorically if not literally, brothers.” 3 Both of these award winning historians of the American Civil War and its aftermath discuss the idea of the war being fought by individuals who, through the perils of war, became brothers in arms. In New York State, many biological brothers enlisted in the same regi- ment. These men camped, marched, and fought side by side, but this criti- cal aspect of the war has not received adequate attention from historians. This study has its origins in Isaac Hall’s The History of the Ninety-Seventh Regiment New York Volunteers, which describes the experience of two German brothers George (forty-three-years-old) and Gottfired Gleasman (forty-four-years-old). According to Hall the two “brothers were stand- ing in line together, when one of them was killed by the unerring aim of a Confederate marksman who had steadied his piece against a tree. This study seeks to answer this question first by exploring how biological brothers influenced one another while serving in the Union Army during the Civil War and second, by examining the war’s impact on brothers who fought side-by-side. The presence of a brother, serving in the same regiment, offered strength and stability to his sibling. As a result, these mentally and emo- tionally fortified soldiers contributed to the integrity of the regiment and the Union war effort. Men who had their brothers with them handled the harsh reality of combat and military life better than those who faced the war alone. The men’s letters illustrate that when a soldier had his brother with him, he was less likely to feel homesick, afraid and anxious. Brothers provided the resilience men needed to confront the battlefield. To compare the soldiers who served with their brothers with those who did not, the study is divided into two groups of soldiers: “broth- ers” and “loners,” each of whom served in regiments from Upstate New York, primarily the 97 New York Volunteer Infantry from Boonville, but also the 126 New York Volunteer Infantry from Geneva, the 114New York Volunteer Infantry from Norwich and the 117New York Volunteer Infantry from Rome. 5 The context of each letter was critical in interpreting a soldier’s frame of mind. It was necessary to be aware of which battles the soldier had experienced near the time each letter was written. 6 The dates of battles and the separation of brothers due to furlough, injury, or sickness proved vital in understanding the letters.
