Why Here A Currier campaign print for the American Party ticket of 1856 shows Millard Fillmore and Andrew Jackson Donelson framed by flags under the slogan “The Constitution and the Union,” with no anti-Catholic or anti-immigrant imagery. Students can weigh whether, by 1856, the Know-Nothings were selling themselves less as nativists than as the party that could hold the Union together.

Historical Context
In February 1856 the American Party’s national convention in Philadelphia nominated former president Millard Fillmore, a New York Whig, for president and Andrew Jackson Donelson of Tennessee, nephew and former private secretary of Andrew Jackson, for vice president. Many northern delegates walked out after the convention refused to oppose the Kansas-Nebraska Act, and much of the party’s northern wing soon moved to the new Republican Party. In September the remnant of the Whig Party also endorsed Fillmore.
Fillmore campaigned as a moderate who would calm the sectional quarrel over slavery in the territories, warning that a Republican victory would divide the nation. Nathaniel Currier’s New York firm sold campaign portraits like this one for every major candidate in 1856. Fillmore finished third with about 21 percent of the popular vote, strongest in the border South.
