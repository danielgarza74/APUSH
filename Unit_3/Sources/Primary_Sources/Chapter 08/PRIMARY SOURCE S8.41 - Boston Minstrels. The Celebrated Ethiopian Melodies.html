Historical Context
Blackface minstrelsy, in which white performers darkened their faces with burnt cork and played exaggerated versions of enslaved and free Black people, became the most popular form of stage entertainment in the North in the 1840s. After the Virginia Minstrels’ success in New York in early 1843, many troupes formed, among them the Boston Minstrels, and music publishers sold their songs as sheet music for home pianos, with lithographed covers advertising the performers.
Minstrel audiences were drawn heavily from urban workingmen, including Irish immigrants. The shows mocked Black speech, dress, and ambition, contrasting a supposedly contented plantation “darky” with a ridiculous Northern “dandy.” Historians have argued that this entertainment helped native-born and immigrant workers define themselves as white at a time when wage labor was eroding the independence of artisans.
