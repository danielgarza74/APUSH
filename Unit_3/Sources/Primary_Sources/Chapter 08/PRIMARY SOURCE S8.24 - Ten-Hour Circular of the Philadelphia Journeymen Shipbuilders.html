Historical Context
In the spring of 1835 Philadelphia’s skilled journeymen, led by coal heavers on the Schuylkill wharves and by carpenters, masons, and shipbuilders, struck for a ten-hour day, “from six to six” with time off for meals, in place of the traditional sunrise-to-sunset schedule. Supported by the city’s General Trades’ Union, the movement spread across more than a dozen trades in June 1835, and the city councils and most private employers soon granted the ten-hour day.
Journeymen in the shipyards along the Delaware drew up written terms for their masters, and their circulars traveled to other ports. A copy of the Philadelphia shipbuilders’ circular was forwarded to the Secretary of the Navy by Commodore James Barron and survives in the records of the Board of Navy Commissioners; federal navy yard workers in Washington and elsewhere took up the same demand that summer.

Document Text
Transcription of the printed text
RULES TO Regulate the Work OF THE JOURNEYMEN SHIPWRIGHTS, JOINERS. CAULKERS AND MAST MAKERS OF PHILADELPHIA.
The Journeymen Shipwrights, Joiners, Caulkers and Mast Makers, deeming it proper as Citizens and Freemen, to regulate the hours in which they are to Labour, reasonably and more consistent with the Constitution of Man—propose the following Rules, by which they are willing to be governed, and which shall be all the year regulated by the ringing of the Mechanics Union Bell.
In May, June, July & August, we will begin work at Sun-rise and leave off at 6 o'clock, P. M. reserving one hour for breakfast and two for dinner.
In December, January and February, we will get breakfast before going to work, begin work at 45 minutes after Sun-rise, reserving one hour for dinner, and work untill Sun-set.
The remainder of the year from Sun to Sun, reserving one hour for breakfast and one hour for dinner.
☞ The hour for dinner 12 o'clock, the year round.
