Petition of Citizens of Minnesota for a Telegraph from St. Paul
Residents of Minnesota Territory · May 21, 1850 (petition to Congress)
 
Field Detail
TOC Fit Chapter 8 · Essay prompt 8.1 (Revolution or Evolution?); Essay prompt 8.2 (Who Built the Infrastructure?)
Why Here Frontier settlers explain why they need the telegraph and why Washington should pay for it with a grant of public land. Students can weigh how ordinary Americans valued fast communication and what role they expected the federal government to play in building it.
Treatment Public-domain text, complete (signatures omitted)
Historical Context
Samuel F. B. Morse’s electromagnetic telegraph carried its first public message between Washington and Baltimore in 1844. Within six years private companies had strung wires linking most eastern and midwestern cities, but lines reached only where investors expected profit. Minnesota, organized as a territory in 1849, had a few thousand white settlers, a capital at St. Paul, and military posts and Indian agencies scattered across the upper Mississippi valley.
In winter, when the river froze, mail to St. Paul traveled overland and could take weeks. The petitioners asked Congress for a township of public land (about 23,000 acres) that the territory could sell to finance a line, the same land-grant method Congress was then beginning to use for canals and railroads. St. Paul was not connected to the national telegraph network until 1860, and then by private enterprise.
Editorial note. The Norton Source Collection credits this petition to the National Archives; this reader reproduces the original manuscript petition (Records of the U.S. Senate, Record Group 46, Territorial Papers, NAID 306499) from the transcription posted in the National Archives Catalog by a volunteer Citizen Archivist, checked against the paragraphing Norton prints. The transcription’s “form [from]” in the opening prayer has been silently corrected to “from”; the list of about two dozen signatures that follows the text has been omitted. The petition was received by the 31st Congress in 1850; it did not result in a land grant.
Document Text
To the honorable the Senate and House of Representatives of the United States, in Congress assembled:
The undersigned citizens of the United States, and residents of the Territory of Minnesota, humbly pray your honorable body to pass an act granting to the Territory of Minnesota, a Township of the Public Lands, lying in said Territory, for the purpose of constructing a Magnetic Telegraph from St. Paul, the present seat of Territorial Government, to connect with Telegraphic lines South and East.
Among the considerations upon which your petitioners base their prayer, and respectfully urge it upon the attention of your honorable body, is the grave and direct interest Government has in the construction of a Telegraph, to enable it to transmit orders to, and receive intelligence from the different military posts, and remote Indian agencies and sub-agencies of the North-West; and the imperative necessity which exists, that more speedy means of communication should be opened than the present tardy and uncertain mail arrangements supply. Instances arise, as in cases of Indian wars, Indian outrages, &c., when the interposition of Government, to be effective, should be immediate; yet for five months in the year, communication with and from this Territory is dependent upon the ice of the Mississippi; and not unfrequently government orders and advices are from thirty to forty days reaching the seat of Territorial government.
Another consideration which your petitioners would respectfully urge, is the increased value a Telegraph would give to the public domain. The mere existence of a Telegraph will add to the value of the public lands in this Territory, an amount far exceeding the congressional valuation of a solitary township. By creating this additional value, it will facilitate the sale of the lands, and at an early day augment the revenues which Government will derive from this source.
Your petitioners rely for favorable consideration of their prayer, upon the clearly defined powers of Congress:
To provide for the National defence.
To provide for the transportation of the mail, (and your petitioners conceive, the civilization of the age, and the development of new powers, render the Telegraph an indispensable means.)
To take provident care of the public domain, by increasing its value, by the best known modes of internal communication.
To cement the bonds of union, between greatly distant parts of this vast republic, which will be thus riveted together, by unceasing and instantaneous intercourse.
The construction of a Telegraph will furnish facilities for averting Indian wars, and suppressing Indian disturbances. It will afford the means of transmitting government orders, general news and private intelligence. It will enhance the value of the public domain, and expedite its sale and settlement. It will render the union of the remote frontier possessions of the United States in the North-West, with other parts of the republic, indissoluble.
And as the line of Telegraph will pass through an unsettled country, as the inhabitants of this Territory, engaged in a contest with unreclaimed nature, have not themselves the means to build it, as Government is the party most directly and most largely interested in its construction, your petitioners pray that an act may be passed by your honorable body, donating for the purpose a township of land to the Territory of Minnesota.
And your petitioners will ever pray &c.
