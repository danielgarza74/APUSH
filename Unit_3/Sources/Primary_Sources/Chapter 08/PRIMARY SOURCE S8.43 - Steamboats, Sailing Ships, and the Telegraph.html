Why Here Three pictures of the faster movement of goods and news: sailing ships crowding New York’s East River wharves, the paper tape of the first long-distance telegraph message, and a Mississippi packet steamboat. Students can compare how much each technology changed the speed of trade and information.
Treatment Public-domain images; printed titles transcribed
Historical Context
By the 1830s New York’s South Street, on the East River, was the busiest waterfront in the country, lined with packet ships that sailed to Liverpool on fixed schedules and coastal vessels carrying cotton north. On the western rivers, steamboats cut the trip from New Orleans to Louisville from months to days; by the 1850s large “high pressure” packets ran regular service between St. Louis and New Orleans.
On May 24, 1844, Samuel F. B. Morse sent the message “What hath God wrought” from the Supreme Court chamber in the Capitol to Alfred Vail in Baltimore over a line built with a $30,000 congressional appropriation. His early receiver embossed dots and dashes on a moving paper tape, which an operator then decoded. Within a decade private telegraph companies linked most American cities.
