Richmond Enquirer: Editorial on the Election Returns
Thomas Ritchie, editor · November 14, 1828 (Richmond Enquirer, p. 3)
 
TOC FIT Chapter 9 · Essay prompt 9.5 (Why Jackson Won in 1828)
WHY HERE The leading Jacksonian paper of the South reports the returns as they arrived by mail and stage. Students can see how a partisan editor followed the count state by state, and what the paper’s confidence and its sense of relief say about the size of Jackson’s victory and about the press network that announced it.
TREATMENT Public-domain text, selected excerpts (corrected from poor newspaper OCR; to be checked against the page image)
Historical Context

Thomas Ritchie edited the Richmond Enquirer from 1804 until 1845 and led the “Richmond Junto,” the group that dominated Virginia politics. After 1826 he joined Martin Van Buren in the alliance between Virginia and New York Republicans that became the backbone of the Jackson coalition. The Enquirer was read and reprinted across the South.

In 1828 states voted on different days between late October and mid-November, and news of the results traveled by mail coach. This editorial, written as returns from New York and Maryland were still arriving, predicts a large Jackson majority. Jackson won 178 electoral votes to Adams’s 83, carrying every state south of Maryland and west of the Appalachians, along with Pennsylvania and most of New York’s districts.

Document Text
RICHMOND, NOV. 14.

Figures are rapidly giving way to facts—calculations to certainty. For fifteen months past we have never entertained a moment’s doubt about the Election of Jackson. We avowed it on the 15th of Sept. 1827; and from that moment we have never faultered in our faith.

[. . .]

The vote in New York has excited the deepest interest.—Our whole city has looked to it with intense anxiety—the stage has been watched; its passengers interrogated; and estimates after estimates made, Ossa upon Pelion and Pelion upon Ossa, to arrive at the result.

[. . .]

We break off to announce the intelligence by last Evening’s Mail—It is conclusive—We get certain in N. York seventeen—and possibly 20—We need not weigh our cause by grains or scruples—Single votes are of no consequence—We shall carry also Kentucky—and we confidently infer from the returns, Ohio—We entertain not much doubt about Indiana—and it is possible, that Mr. Adams will not receive a vote beyond the mountains. Jackson’s majority may be 79 or 89 above Mr. A.

Maryland too, attracted great attention—The friends of the Administration confidently claimed 10 out of the 11 votes—How blows the wind in that quarter?—The Election came on, on Monday last. Baltimore county is one Electoral District, and gives Jackson 1—Baltimore City, Anne Arundel and Annapolis, form a double district and have chosen 2 Jackson Electors—The Upper District, we have no doubt, 2 more—5 in Maryland.

We will not distract our readers to-day by a variety of returns, and columns of figures.—In our next, they shall have enough to satisfy the most inquisitive Arithmetic!

We will not repeat with the N. Intelligencer of Monday last—This is the greatest and most glorious news we have received since the peace—but it is certainly very acceptable intelligence. It clears away an Administration, which has forfeited the confidence of their Country, by an overwhelming majority. Jackson will have 79 or 89 more than Mr. A.—The more decision now, the better. We may now have peace in Israel at least for two years.
