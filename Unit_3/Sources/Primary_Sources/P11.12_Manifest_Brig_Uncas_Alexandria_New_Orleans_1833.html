Manifest of the Brig Uncas, Alexandria to New Orleans (Franklin and Armfield shipment)
Franklin and Armfield; Joseph C. Moore, master; sworn before the collector of the port of Alexandria · October 30, 1833 (manuscript on printed form)
 
TOC FIT Chapter 11 · Essay prompt 11.1 (Slavery and Capitalism); Essay prompt 11.3 (The Nature of Slavery)
WHY HERE A federal customs form on which a slave-trading firm listed ninety-two people by name, age, height, and color before shipping them to New Orleans “for the purpose of being sold.” Students can use it to judge how routine, organized, and legally documented the interstate slave trade was, and to notice whose names and families appear on the page.
TREATMENT Public-domain manuscript; heading, opening entries, and oath transcribed; complete list in the image
Historical Context

The 1807 law that closed the Atlantic slave trade also regulated the coastwise trade in enslaved people. Any vessel carrying enslaved people from one U.S. port to another had to file a manifest, signed under oath by the shipper and the captain and certified by the customs collector, swearing that none had been brought into the country illegally after January 1, 1808. These manifests, filed at both the departure and arrival ports, are among the most detailed records of the domestic slave trade.

In the 1830s the Alexandria firm of Franklin and Armfield sent ships such as the Uncas, the Tribune, and the Isaac Franklin from the Potomac to New Orleans several times a year. John Armfield bought people in Virginia and Maryland and held them in the firm’s Duke Street pen; his partner Isaac Franklin sold them in New Orleans and Natchez. The ninety-two people on this voyage ranged from infants to adults of about forty, but most were in their teens and twenties, the ages planters paid most for.

Editorial note. The manifest is owned by the Smithsonian National Museum of African American History and Culture (object 2013.46.6), which marks it public domain (CC0); Encyclopedia Virginia reproduces the images and a transcription by Smithsonian Transcription Center volunteers. This reader prints the marginal heading, the first thirteen entries on page 1, and the oath on page 4 from that transcription; the table entries are set as single lines (number, name, age, height, color), and “D.” in nos. 2–4 is given as transcribed (its meaning is unclear; these children are bracketed with Clawey Ford). The transcription’s “known prior of service” has been corrected to “period,” the printed form’s wording. The full list of ninety-two names should be read from the page images. CAUTION: The manifest records what the traders and customs officer wrote; names, ages, and colors are the traders’ descriptions.
Document Text
Heading (written in the right margin of page 1)

Manifest of Negroes, Mulattoes and persons of Colour, taken on board the Brig Uncas, whereof Joseph C Moore is Master, burthen 155 1/25 Tons, to be transported from the Port of Alexandria, in the District of Columbia for the purpose of being sold or disposed of as slaves, or to be held to service or labour. shipped by Franklin & Armfield Alex. to Isaac Franklin New Orleans.

First entries on page 1 (No., name, age, height in feet and inches, “colour”)

1. Clawey Ford & 4 children—30—5 ft. 5 in.—Copper

2. John – D.—7—4 ft.—Copper

3. Matty. D—5—3 ft. 3¼ in.—Copper

4. Andrew D.—4—2 ft. 10 in.—Copper

5. Clowey Ann—9 month—2 ft. 10 in.—Copper

6. Eliza Smith—19—5 ft. 10¼ in.—Black

7. Ann Mariah Hawkins—20—5 ft. 3½ in.—Black

8. Ann Adison—19—5 ft. 1 in.—Black

9. Mary Clarke—18—5 ft. 3 in.—Black

10. Elizabeth Ducket—18—5 ft. 1 in.—Black

11. Louisa Fisher—15—5 ft. 1 in.—Black

12. Anny Digs & 1 Child—20—4 ft. 9 in.—Black

13. Henry—2—2 ft. 4½ in.—Black

[. . .]

Oath (page 4)

District of Alexandria Port of Alexandria the 30th day of October 1833.

I John Armfield owner of the persons named and particularly described in the within Manifest, and Joseph C Moore Master of the Brig Uncas do solemnly, sincerely, and truly swear, each of us, to the best of our knowledge and belief, that the Negroes within named, have not been imported into the United States, since the first day of January one thousand, eight hundred and eight; and that under the Laws of the United States are held to service or labor as slaves, and are not entitled to freedom under these Laws, at a certain time, and after a known period of service—So help us God.

Sworn to this thirtieth day of October 1833. before C T. Chapman Collector. John Armfield Joseph C Moore
