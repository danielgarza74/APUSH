Literary Cadet and Rhode-Island Statesman: “To the Freemen of Rhode-Island” and Election Returns
“One of the People” and the editors · November 12, 1828 (Literary Cadet and Rhode-Island Statesman, Providence, p. 2)
 
TOC FIT Chapter 9 · Essay prompt 9.5 (Why Jackson Won in 1828)
WHY HERE A pro-Adams appeal printed in Providence a week before Rhode Island voted, when most other states had already chosen. Students can compare its case for Adams, built on the debt, tariff, and internal improvements, with Jacksonian appeals, and test its confident predictions against the actual result.
TREATMENT Public-domain text, selected excerpts (corrected from newspaper OCR; to be checked against the page image)
Historical Context

In 1828 there was no single national election day. Each state set its own date for choosing presidential electors, and Rhode Island voted on November 19, after the results from many larger states were already known or rumored. Rhode Island’s editors therefore printed both returns from other states and appeals urging their own voters to turn out.

The Literary Cadet and Rhode-Island Statesman supported the administration. On its editorial page it ran the Adams electoral ticket, a long letter signed “One of the People,” and a column of returns. The letter lists the administration’s achievements, warns of Jacksonian handbills and rumors, and insists that Adams would win New York and perhaps part of Virginia. Adams did carry Rhode Island and the rest of New England except one Maine district, but Jackson won the election decisively.

LITERARY CADET. WEDNESDAY, NOVEMBER 12.
PEOPLE’S TICKET.

FOR PRESIDENT, JOHN QUINCY ADAMS.

FOR VICE PRESIDENT, RICHARD RUSH.

CANDIDATES FOR ELECTORS. [Election November 19th.] CALEB EARLE, PROVIDENCE. STEPHEN B. CORNELL, PORTSMOUTH. ELISHA WATSON, SOUTH-KINGSTOWN. CHARLES ELDRIDGE, EAST GREENWICH.

TO THE FREEMEN OF RHODE-ISLAND.

Fellow Citizens.—Your choice of Electors is at hand. Permit me to say a few words on a subject, always important to ourselves, which is now, by the peculiar character of the canvass, rendered important to the whole nation.

I will not say any thing to convince you of the superior claims of Mr. Adams to the first office in the country. You made up your minds, on that point, more than six years ago; and subsequent experience has satisfied you, that your preference of the Son of New England was a happy choice. More than FORTY MILLIONS OF DOLLARS, applied by Mr. Adams to the liquidation of the PUBLIC DEBT; TWELVE MILLION, expended on NATIONAL WORKS of the greatest utility; an improved tariff of duties on imports, for the protection of our Agriculture and Manufactures; The maintenance and vindication of our rights abroad, and full indemnity for their casual infraction, obtained by peaceful negotiation; The appointment to office of capable and worthy men, without regard to party distinctions; The prosperity of the great interests of the nation; Its advancement in power, in wealth, in population, in character—attest the patriotism, the ability, and the honesty, of the present Administration of the General Government.

You are satisfied, and have frequently expressed your satisfaction, at these results: You have perceived no useful reason for seeking, among other aspirants, a proper person to displace Mr. Adams, before the expiration of the usual period. In your calm and settled conviction and preference, you have spared yourselves the disagreeable sensations which an acrimonious and animated scrutiny of the characters of eminent public servants must always produce. But this same conviction requires you, at the coming choice of Electors, to ACT, with firmness and decision, but with moderation, on the sentiments which you entertain, and the feelings which you have, hitherto, so often expressed.

It would not comport with your duty to yourselves and your country to repose in confidence on the apparent unanimity which prevails among you. Such a course would give an undue advantage to a small but active and scheming minority, fruitful in expedients and untiring in exertions, to defeat and prevent a full expression of your will. I say nothing against the motives of these men. Presuming that they act from their own convictions of duty, it is, still, our part, to defeat their machinations. I am informed, but will not state from personal knowledge, that they are circulating pamphlets and handbills, containing unfounded accusations against Mr. Adams and his Cabinet. If such be the fact, the secresy of their manoeuvres should, at once, determine, in the minds of our enlightened Freemen, their true character. Every thing which has been publicly said against the Administration has been publicly met and refuted, by evidence of the most satisfactory nature.—Accusations which avoid the sight of day and the test of facts cannot be true. Such accusations you will not believe.

Another means of deterring the cautious and timid from voting has been the circulation, in an undertoned gossip, of assertions, got up for the occasion and adapted to peculiar localities and prejudices, which are intended to discourage us, as to the general result of the election. Statements of votes, in distant parts of the country, have been made by putting together those of towns, counties and districts, most favourable to General Jackson, and excluding, entirely, the votes most favorable to, and composing the actual and decisive majorities of Mr. Adams. As fast as one mistatement of this kind is exploded by the official returns, others are composed for other places, with equal industry and equal disregard of truth. If a few, only, are thus deceived, their object is in part obtained. But, fellow citizens, let not these things deceive you. The contest is, in some of the States, an animated and close one: but its general aspect is favorable to the re-election of Mr. Adams, and highly cheering to the friends of civil liberty. FOURTEEN STATES of the TWENTY FOUR have already expressed, by their latest elections, their decided preference of Mr. Adams. In the great commonwealth of New-York, which was thought, by all, to hold the balance of power, our preponderance, and the choice of from TWENTY ONE TO TWENTY FOUR ELECTORS of the 36 is PLACED BEYOND A DOUBT. The returns from Virginia, so long considered as certain for General Jackson, show an unexpected Adams force, displayed at her election, and justify the hope, that the Electoral vote of that State, or at least a part of it, will be given to the Administration. In the nine States which appear certainly against Mr. Adams, there is not an entire unanimity, as to the person for whom their votes should be cast. By some of the persons, there chosen Electors, insurmountable scruples are entertained as to the fitness of General Jackson for the Presidency; and by them some other person will, assuredly, be voted for—thus dividing the force of the Opposition, and insuring the defeat of its leading Candidate.

But, fellow citizens, it is our duty, and, I doubt not, it will be a pleasant one, as AMERICAN FREEMEN, rightfully possessed of a proportion of the sovereignty of the Nation, to give a FULL EXPRESSION of opinion, in the choice of our Chief Magistrate—whatever may be the prospects and promises of the people in other States. You have been calm and peaceful, amid the storm which raged around you.—Your peace was the result of your confidence in your own strength. Now is the appointed time for action, for energy, for decision. Our cause is just; our Candidate approved on trial, our prospect cheering. Come, THEN, TO THE POLLS, ON WEDNESDAY NEXT. Come, one and all. Devote the day to your Country, to the cause of Truth and Civil Order; to the support of Adams and Liberty!

ONE OF THE PEOPLE.

ELECTION RETURNS.

NEW HAMPSHIRE.—There can be no doubt of the election of Adams Electors in New Hampshire, by a majority of from 3000 to 4000 votes. The last returns are from 183 towns, which give a nett gain of 1002 for the Administration.

MAINE.—The returns from Maine are nearly complete, and it is unnecessary to give the detailed accounts. Mr. Adams has eight votes, and General Jackson one.

NEW-JERSEY.—The Administration Ticket for Electors and Members of Congress, has succeeded by a majority rising of 3000 votes.
