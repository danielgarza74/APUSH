John Ridge to Governor Wilson Lumpkin
John Ridge · September 22, 1833 (manuscript letter, Cassville, Georgia)
 
Field Detail
TOC Fit Chapter 10 · Essay prompt 10.3 (Explaining Indian Removal)
Why Here A leading Cherokee writes privately to Georgia’s governor that the pro-treaty faction is gaining ground, that Ross hopes to “outlive the Administration of Genl. Jackson,” and that the treaty party may organize its own chiefs and council to close a cession. Students can use it to see the divisions inside the Cherokee Nation and how one faction looked to the state that was pressing for removal.
Treatment Public-domain text, excerpt
Historical Context
John Ridge (1803–1839), son of the Cherokee leader Major Ridge, was educated at a mission school in Connecticut and had served as clerk of the Cherokee National Council. Like his cousin Elias Boudinot, he had fought removal through the courts, but after Georgia and the Jackson administration ignored Worcester v. Georgia (1832), he concluded that the nation would survive only by negotiating a removal treaty on the best terms available.
Ridge’s shift split the Cherokee leadership. Principal Chief John Ross and the National Council refused any cession, and in 1834–35 the council moved against the Ridges and their allies. The Treaty Party, as the Ridge faction became known, went on to sign the Treaty of New Echota in December 1835. Wilson Lumpkin, governor of Georgia from 1831 to 1835, was a strong advocate of removal and of the state land lottery that distributed Cherokee land.

Document Text
Cassville Ga 22d. Sept. 1833.
Dear Sir,
I have been intending to write to you on the Indian Affairs for some time, but the press of business in conducting the business of our nation at this interesting period, has prevented. I feel pleasure now to say that our cause prospers, & I believe will result in the general Cession of the Nation. The views taken in a communication by a Gentleman of the bar in the Georgia -- Pioneer on the Cherokee treaty, Should be republished with the correction of misprints in the paper alluded to. John Ross & his party will try -- to outlive the Administration of Genl. Jackson if they are not forced into the treaty, & it now -- depends upon the treaty party to -- take a bold and decided stand. We have gained so much now in Georgia & Alabama, that we shall soon organise head chiefs & a regular Council for those two states and close the -- treaty -- However this is conditioned upon the refusal of the Ross party to Join in a general treaty -- He has requested a Conference, and we have accepted it, & it is possible that we may agree to make a General Cession. This Conference will be held it is proposed on the 2d. Monday in Oct. next in the chartered limits of Tennessee -- If that Council passes by with our bringing the parties to an understanding, you may depend upon a vigorous course of measures on our part.
[. . .]
