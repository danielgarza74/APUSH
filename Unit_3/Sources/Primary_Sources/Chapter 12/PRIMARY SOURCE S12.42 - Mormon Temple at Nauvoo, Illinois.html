Historical Context
The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints, organized by Joseph Smith in western New York in 1830, was one of the most successful of the new religions born in the Second Great Awakening. Driven from Ohio and then from Missouri, where the governor ordered in 1838 that Mormons be “exterminated or driven from the state,” some 5,000 Saints settled in 1839 at a bend of the Mississippi in Illinois and named their town Nauvoo. Under a generous state charter it grew within a few years into one of the largest cities in Illinois.
Smith announced a revelation in 1841 calling for a temple, and church members donated money and every tenth day of labor to build it of local limestone. Its distinctive carved sunstones, moonstones, and starstones and its tall tower made it the most prominent building on the upper Mississippi. Smith was killed by a mob at Carthage, Illinois, in June 1844; the Saints finished the temple under Brigham Young, used it briefly in the winter of 1845–46, and then abandoned it as they began the trek west. An arsonist gutted it in 1848, and a tornado brought down most of its walls in 1850.
