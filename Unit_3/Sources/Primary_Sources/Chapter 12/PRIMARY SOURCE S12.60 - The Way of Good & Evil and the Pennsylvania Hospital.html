TOC Fit Chapter 12 · Essay prompt 12.2 (Why Reform?)
Why Here A Pennsylvania German folk lithograph maps two roads through life, one of piety, sobriety, and work leading to heaven, the other of drink, idleness, and crime leading to the gallows and hell, while two views of Philadelphia’s Pennsylvania Hospital show the institutions reformers built for the sick and the insane. Students can weigh whether such images express religious hopes of saving souls or a middle-class program for disciplining behavior.

Historical Context
Between the 1820s and the 1850s, reformers inspired by evangelical revivals and Enlightenment ideas of improvement campaigned against drinking, built penitentiaries and asylums, and promoted public schools. Popular prints spread their message. “Two roads” pictures, an old Protestant theme drawn from Christ’s image of the broad and narrow ways (Matthew 7:13–14), showed daily choices about alcohol, Sabbath-keeping, gambling, and work as steps toward salvation or damnation. John Hailer’s colored lithograph, made in Northampton County, Pennsylvania, belongs to this tradition.
The Pennsylvania Hospital, founded in Philadelphia in 1751 by Benjamin Franklin and Dr. Thomas Bond, was the first incorporated general hospital in the British colonies and long housed mentally ill patients in its Pine Street building. In 1841 it opened a separate Pennsylvania Hospital for the Insane in West Philadelphia under Dr. Thomas Story Kirkbride, whose plans for spacious, orderly asylums meant to cure through routine, work, and calm surroundings were copied across the country; a new building for men was under construction by the late 1850s.
Editorial note. The Norton Online Reader’s versions of these images could not be reached, so this reader uses public-domain copies held by the Library of Congress Prints and Photographs Division. The Hailer lithograph is the widely reproduced print of this title; the exact Pennsylvania Hospital view in the Norton set could not be identified, so this reader supplies a dated 1854 engraving of the Pine Street hospital and an ca. 1859 stereograph of the hospital’s new building for the insane. CAUTION: The Hailer print is dated c. 1862 and the stereograph ca. 1859, both after the 1825–1854 period of the reform movements; they show ideas and institutions that grew out of those movements rather than contemporary evidence of their origins.
