Historical Context
Camp meetings, which grew out of the Kentucky and Tennessee revivals of 1799–1801, became the signature event of American Methodism in the early nineteenth century. Families traveled to a forest clearing and camped for several days in tents and wagons arranged around a raised preachers’ stand, with benches for the congregation and often a fenced “altar” or “mourners’ bench” in front where those seeking conversion came forward. Preaching, singing, and prayer went on day and night, and meetings often drew thousands of people. Methodist leaders reported several hundred camp meetings a year by the 1810s.
Jacques Gérard Milbert (1766–1840) was a French artist and naturalist who lived in the United States from 1815 to 1823, collecting natural-history specimens for the French government and sketching landscapes and scenes of American life. His drawing of a camp meeting was engraved in aquatint by M. Dubourg and issued as a hand-colored print about 1819.

CAUTION: The print is an artist’s composed view, not a photograph of a particular meeting. Milbert was a foreign observer, and European audiences often found camp meetings exotic or disorderly; consider what the artist chose to show and what he left out.
