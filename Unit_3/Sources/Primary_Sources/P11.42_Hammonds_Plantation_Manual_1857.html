Hammond’s Plantation Manual (rules for “sucklers,” the elderly, and pregnant women)
James Henry Hammond · 1857–1858 (manuscript plantation manual, Redcliffe and Silver Bluff, South Carolina)
 
TOC FIT Chapter 11 · Essay prompt 11.1 (Slavery and Capitalism); Essay prompt 11.3 (The Nature of Slavery)
WHY HERE A page of rules a major South Carolina enslaver wrote to govern his plantations, setting the hours, work loads, and rations of nursing mothers, pregnant women, and elderly or disabled workers. Students can weigh whether these rules show fatherly “care” or the careful management of human property to protect future labor and births.
TREATMENT Public-domain manuscript page; reproduced as an image, text to be transcribed from the page
Historical Context

James Henry Hammond (1807–1864), a former governor of South Carolina who entered the U.S. Senate in 1857, held more than three hundred enslaved people on his Silver Bluff and Redcliffe plantations along the Savannah River. Like many large planters, he wrote a manual of standing rules for his overseers covering work hours, gang assignments, rations, clothing, medical care, punishment, and the birth and nursing of children.

Planters’ manuals and the agricultural journals that printed model rules presented plantation management as a rational, orderly system. Enslaved women’s childbearing mattered to enslavers because, after the closing of the Atlantic slave trade in 1808, the growth of their labor force and wealth depended on births. Rules about nursing time and lighter work for pregnant women therefore served both the enslaver’s claim to be a caring master and his calculations about future labor.

Editorial note. The Norton Source Collection prints a transcription of this page from the manuscript in the James Henry Hammond Papers, Library of Congress (reproduction LC-DIG-ppmsca-02942). No public-domain or openly licensed printed transcription of the page could be located, and Norton’s modern transcription is not reproduced here. This entry therefore reproduces the manuscript page itself as an image from the Library of Congress; the text (about 350 words, beginning “Sucklers are not required to leave their houses until sun-rise” and covering nursing mothers, the “suckler’s gang,” pregnant women, and the elderly and infirm) is transcribed below from the page image. The Library of Congress dates the manual 1857–1858. CAUTION: A planter’s rules record what he ordered, not what overseers actually did or what enslaved people experienced. Illegible words are marked [illegible].
Document Text
Transcription of the manuscript page

Sucklers.—

Sucklers are not required to leave their houses until sun-rise, when they leave their children at the children's house before going to field. The period of suckling is 12 mos. Their work lies always within ½ a mile of the quarter. They are required to be cool before commencing to suckle—to wait 15 minutes, at least, in summer, after reaching the children's house before nursing. It is the duty of the nurse to see that none are heated when nursing, as well as of the Overseer & his wife occasionally to do so. They are allowed 45 minutes at each nursing to be with their children. They return 3 times a day until their infants are 8 mos. old—in the middle of the forenoon, at noon, & in the middle of the afternoon: 'till the 12th mo. but twice a day, missing at noon during the 12th mo. at noon only. On weaning, the child is removed entirely from its Mother for 2 weeks, & placed in charge of some careful woman without a child, during which time the Mother is not to nurse it at all.

Remarks.—The amount of work done by a Suckler is about 3/5 of that done by a full-hand, a little increased toward the last.

Old & Infirm.—

Those, who from age & infirmities are unable to keep up with the prime hands are put in the sucklers' gang.

Pregnant.—

Pregnant women, at 5 mos., are put in the sucklers' gang. No plowing or lifting must be required of them.

[Added in lighter ink:] Sucklers, old, infirm & pregnant receive the same allowances as full-work hands.
