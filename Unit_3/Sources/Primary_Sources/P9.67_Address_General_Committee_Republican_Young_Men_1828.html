Historical Context

In New York City the Jackson cause was run through the Republican organization centered on Tammany Hall, which Martin Van Buren’s allies tied to his statewide network. Party men organized ward meetings, general committees, and separate “Young Men’s” committees that drafted addresses, adopted resolutions, and printed them as broadsides and in friendly newspapers. Such addresses were meant to be read aloud at meetings and posted in public places.

This address was issued by the city’s General Committee of Republican Young Men in 1828, ahead of elections in which Adams’s supporters had tried to make the presidential question a test of local loyalty. It repeats the main charges of the Jackson campaign: the 1825 “bargain” between Adams and Clay, Adams’s federalist past, and his broad reading of federal power in his first annual message. It answers Clay’s description of Jackson as a “military chieftain” with a list of Jackson’s civil offices.

Editorial note. Reproduced complete from the Library of Congress machine-readable transcription (American Memory, 1999) of the broadside in the Printed Ephemera Collection, checked against its stated wording. The broadside carries no exact date; its references to the coming election place it in 1828. The Norton Source Collection does not include this document. ADDRESS OF THE General Committee of Republican Young Men of the City of New-York, FRIENDLY TO THE ELECTION OF ANDREW JACKSON.

FELLOW CITIZENS,

As the representatives of the Republican Young Men of this City, it devolves on us unreservedly to give you our views of the approaching election, and of those questions which we consider connected with it. That it is our right to do so as individuals, we should have thought undoubted: but a novel doctrine has lately been promulgated by the submissive followers of the federal administration, that to express an opinion openly on public matters is improper and savours of dictation. Speaking the truth with boldness, or uttering opinions on public men may, in a despotic government, amount to high treason, and we know the time has been that martyrdom has sealed the fate of the ardent patriot for pleading the wrongs of his country, but we had yet to learn that in a country whose institutions guarantee the freedom of opinion and the liberty of speech, it would be rebellion or high treason to doubt the purity of the President and his Prime Minister. Such, however, is virtually the slavish doctrine lately broached by the indiscreet friends of Mr. Adams in this city—such the abject condition to which they would seek to reduce the freemen of this country. We must, however, claim the privilege of speaking our sentiments, even though it may prove offensive to a President and his Secretary of State, or criminate both as parties to a corrupt and dishonorable bargain for the two highest offices in this nation.

The approaching election, Fellow Citizens, derives an unusual interest from its connection with the next Presidential election. The precipitate and rash declaration of some of the friends of Mr. Adams that the line ought to be drawn, was a challenge to the Republicans of this City which they felt no disposition to evade or decline. It is characteristic of them to meet every political question without fear and without disguise. They promptly, at the call of their opponents, announced their determination and choice. Between Andrew Jackson and John Quincy Adams, they could not, as Republicans, hesitate. Their preference of the former was speedily formed and openly announced. This prompt acceptance of the challenge was unexpected to the very men who provoked it—among the more timid and prudent friends of Mr. Adams, it carried dismay. To decline the contest was now difficult—any thing, however, was preferable to defeat, and to escape this disaster, their only alternative seemed to be to shun the contest. Hence their late systematic effort to divert the attention of the people from the question, in the vain hope, by putting off the evil hour, to effect by management & deception what they cannot accomplish by an open and direct appeal to the people. To the latter it belongs to say whether these schemers shall be successful in their designs. For ourselves, we believe they are already prepared to decide the question, With the intelligence of a free people, they have marked the career and deliberately weighed the characters of these two candidates. They have seen the one born and bred in the aristocratic school of that elder Adams, the author of the alien and sedition laws, and all those high-handed measures which at last awoke the democratic spirit of the country, and ended in the political revolution of 1800. They saw this younger Adams, while yet a boy, introduced in his father's train, at the Courts of Princes, where his political principles were first moulded and fashioned.—Risen to manhood, his favorite station was abroad. Recalled thence by the fall of his father, we find him engaged with zeal in opposition to the Republican party, while his busy pen was secretly employed in composing coarse and filthy ballads to blacken the character of the illustrious Jefferson. This opposition proving fruitless, Mr. Adams, with the prudent foresight of the disciplined diplomatist, suddenly relaxed in his efforts, abandoned his party, went over to their opponents, and in the heat of his apostacy, betrayed his former friends to those whom he had recently joined. The next great act in the life of this gentleman, is his elevation by Congress, contrary to the declared will of the people, to the Presidential Chair. We need not advert to the means by which he obtained this consummation of his hopes. They are before the American people. Every reflecting man has passed upon them the sentence of condemnation, and though the high contracting parties stand on an elevated, it is not an enviable eminence. And what have been the measures of Mr. Adams and Mr. Clay since they surreptitiously seized on the reins of Government? Read the opening message of the President, in which, under a single clause in the preamble to the Constitution, he cancels that sacred instrument, and sets up the will of the President and Congress as the sole guide and limit of their power. Look at the miserable and ridiculous abortion of his Panama mission. See the gallant Porter driven from that navy, and an exile from that country, whose glory had been exalted by his deeds. See the mission of an inefficient minister to the Court of St. James, and the ruinous neglect of the Secretary of State, by which a valuable branch of commerce is lost. See the wasteful and extravagant expenditure of the public money, their reckless tampering with the provisions of the Constitution for electioneering purposes—see them running imaginary roads and canals through every section of our country, in the vain hope of purchasing the people's voices through an appeal to their interests. View the President himself and his travelling Cabinet, in the open day and under the eyes of an indignant people, scouring the country in every direction, in the fruitless chase after popularity. See this same Cabinet, with their myriad of underlings opening the sluices of calumny upon the gallant patriot whose name is identified with his country's glory. See them with remorseless fury, pursuing their object through the whole period of his existence, loading him with every epithet, accusing him of every crime, breaking in upon the peaceful retirement of his home, and dragging his aged and venerated partner before the public. But it has aroused the indignant spirit of a generous and grateful people. The waves of opposition are swelling high in Pennsylvania, Kentucky, Ohio, and New York, and will soon overwhelm these lost and ruined men.

And who is the man upon whose devoted head these minion of power have thus poured forth the streams of calumny? It is Andrew Jackson who at the age of fourteen entered the revolutionary army and at that early day consecrated his life to the service of his country—It is he who an orphan boy at the close of the revolution, devoted himself to the study of the law and by his own efforts unassisted but by the native strength of his mind, the energy of his character and the integrity of his heart, rose to the first rank in his profession. A Senator in the Congress of the United States, who recorded his vote against the alien and sedition laws of the elder Adams. The man who acted a leading part in the politics of that day as a firm and undeviating member of the Republican party and the main support of Mr. Jefferson in his favourite western country—he to whom Mr. Jefferson at once looked for succour when that western country was threatened with a severance from the Union, and of the security and safety of which Mr. Jefferson was only assured when he could write, “General Jackson is true to his country”—It is he who accepted a commission in the army at the commencement of the war of 1812—who being ordered to march to New Orleans and defend the southern country, obeyed the command—proceeded thither almost alone—collected the materiel of an army—pledged his own estates to raise the necessary funds—supplied his soldiers with arms, ammunition, provisions, clothing, organised and disciplined them—awed the disaffected and treacherous; cheered the droop'ng spirits of the people—inspired confidence wherever he appeared—and finally with his few but brave companions met the enemy—and the battle of the 8th of January ranks with the field of Marathon. Upon the restoration of peace we find him with his characteristic disregard of office, like the Roman patriot resigning his commission and returning to his plough. Such is Andrew Jackson—such the man whom Mr. Clay in an evil hour sought to stigmatise as a “Military Chieftain?” The Military Chieftain who, educated to the bar, was appointed by Washington the Attorney General of the District of Tennessee—who was a distinguished member of the Convention which formed the Constitution of that State; a member of the Senate of the United States; a Judge of the Supreme Court of Tennessee for six years; the Governor of Florida; again a member of the Senate of the United States. Such is this Military Chieftain. This Military Chieftain who when rebellion courted him interposed his arm between his country and the threatened danger; who while yet at New Orleans among his brave companions in arms, and in the midst of his triumph proceeded like the humblest citizen and in citizens attire to receive from an offended judge the sentence of the law; who protected that judge from the violence of an outraged people; and who when the extraordinary sentence was heard imposing on him a fine, gave an example of submission to the law, by declining the offering of his grateful countrymen. In his own language, the offence was his and by him should the penalty be borne.

But to enumerate every act of firmness of magnanimity; of virtue which distinguish this man would far exceed our limits. They are familiar to you all, and we call upon you with confidence, and particularly upon our youthful associates, whose hopes for the future safety of our country, must rest upon the purity of our public men, to vindicate the honor of your country in the defeat of a coalition originating in the shameless barter for office and attempted to be sustained on the ruins of the reputation of that tried patriot and incorruptible man— Andrew Jackson.

Resolved, That we view the corrupt means by which the present administration of the federal government came into power, and the measures they have pursued to perpetuate it, as fraught with danger to the future prosperity of our free institutions.

Resolved, That, with the fullest confidence in the virtues, talents, and firmness of Andrew Jackson, we will use all honorable means to secure his election to the next Presidency of the United States.

JOHN MONTGOMERY, Chairman.

RICHARD GRANT, Jr. Secretary.

JOHN E. WHITE, Assistant Secretary.
