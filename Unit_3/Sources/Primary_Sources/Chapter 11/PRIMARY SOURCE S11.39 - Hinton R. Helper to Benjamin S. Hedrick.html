Historical Context
Hinton Rowan Helper (1829–1909) grew up in a non-slaveholding family in Rowan County, North Carolina, and in 1856 was living in New York. That autumn he read the public letter in which Benjamin S. Hedrick, a professor at the University of North Carolina, defended his intention to vote for John C. Frémont, the Republican presidential candidate who opposed the expansion of slavery. Hedrick was being denounced in the state’s Democratic press, and within days the university’s trustees removed him from his post.
Helper sent this short note of encouragement to a man he had never met. The next year he published The Impending Crisis of the South, which used census figures to argue that slavery kept non-slaveholding white southerners poor. Southern states treated the book as incendiary, and circulating it became a crime in North Carolina. Helper’s attack on slavery was made on behalf of white southerners, and he held openly racist views.
Editorial note. This reader reproduces the complete letter as transcribed from the manuscript in the Benjamin S. Hedrick Papers, Southern Historical Collection, UNC-Chapel Hill, in the Documenting the American South edition The First Century of the First State University. The planner located the letter on the NC State “Civil War Era NC” site, which reprints the same DocSouth transcription; the DocSouth page is reproduced here because the NC State copy contains character-encoding errors. Page markers are omitted, and a space the online edition places before final punctuation after names has been closed up. Hedrick’s reply of October 27, 1856, appears elsewhere in this reader. CAUTION: Helper’s “tens of thousands” is a partisan guess, not a count; he was urging Hedrick on and had his own campaign against slavery’s hold on the South to promote.
Document Text
New York, Oct. 15, 1856
Prof. Hedrick,
Bravo! You are right. Stand firm, and friends will gather around you. I have not the pleasure of your acquaintance, but it would do me good to take you by the hand and tell you how glad I was to find that my dear old native State has at least one fearless patriot within her borders. Here are tens of thousands of men in the state, who entertain views similar to those expressed in your letter, but they dare not open their mouths. A remarkably free country! Fremont will probably get 50,000 majority in this state. "The work goes bravely on". If the election could be postponed six or eight months I have no doubt several of the Southern States would bring out an Electoral ticket in favor of something free — say, free speech, free soil, free labor, free presses, free schools, or Fremont.
In the faith, Very Truly, yours &c, H. R. Helper
