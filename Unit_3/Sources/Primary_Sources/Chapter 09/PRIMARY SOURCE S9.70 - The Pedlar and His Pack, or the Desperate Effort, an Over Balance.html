The Pedlar and His Pack, or the Desperate Effort, an Over Balance
Attributed to James Akin · 1828 (etching and aquatint, probably Philadelphia)
 
Field Detail
TOC Fit Chapter 9 · Essay prompt 9.5 (Why Jackson Won in 1828)
Why Here A pro-Jackson cartoon showing the anti-Jackson “coffin handbill” campaign collapsing under its own weight, with Adams clinging to the presidential chair. Students can read it as an argument by its maker about which side the “people” were on and about the role of personal attacks in the campaign.
Treatment Public-domain image; speech balloons transcribed from the repository’s description
Historical Context
By 1828 political cartoons were sold as single prints in eastern cities. This one targets John Binns, the Philadelphia editor whose “coffin handbills” accused Jackson of executing militiamen in 1815. Binns staggers under a pack of coffins, on top of which sit Henry Clay and President John Quincy Adams. Binns asks for more “Treasury-pap,” a jab at the government printing contracts that rewarded friendly editors.
Clay warns that “the people are too much for us,” and Adams vows to hang on to the chair despite the handbills, a letter by the Kentucky politician Harris, the Panama mission, and “the wishes of the People.” The cartoonist presents the attacks on Jackson as a desperate effort by a corrupt administration against the popular will. The Library of Congress attributes it to James Akin on the basis of style.

Document Text
[John Binns:] “I must have an extra dose of Treasury-pap, or down go the Coffins Harry, for I feel faint already.”
[Henry Clay:] “Hold on Jonny Q—for I find that the people are too much for us, and I’m sinking with Jack and his Coffins!”
[John Quincy Adams:] “I’ll hang on to the Chair Harry, in spite of Coffin hand-bills Harris’s letter Panama mission or the wishes of the People.”
[Title in the lower margin:] THE PEDLAR and his PACK or the Desperate EFFORT, an OVER BALANCE.
