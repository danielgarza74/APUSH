PRIMARY SOURCE S9.63
Jackson on the “Corrupt Bargain”: Letters to William B. Lewis and John Coffee
Andrew Jackson · February 7–19, 1825 (printed 1928)
 
Field Detail
TOC Fit Chapter 9 · Essay prompt 9.5 (Why Jackson Won in 1828)
Why Here Writing privately to his closest Tennessee allies in the days around the House election, Jackson moves from repeating rumors of a deal to treating Clay’s appointment as proof of corruption. Students can weigh how quickly the “corrupt bargain” charge took shape and how Jackson’s circle prepared to carry it to the public.
Treatment Public-domain text, two complete letters and one excerpt
Historical Context
In January 1825 an anonymous letter in a Philadelphia newspaper, the Columbian Observer, charged that Henry Clay had agreed to deliver his supporters’ votes to John Quincy Adams in exchange for the post of secretary of state. Clay denounced the author, who turned out to be George Kremer, a little-known Pennsylvania congressman friendly to Jackson, and asked the House to investigate. Kremer declined to appear before the committee, and the inquiry went nowhere.
On February 9 the House chose Adams, and within days Adams offered Clay the State Department. William B. Lewis, a Nashville planter, and John Coffee, Jackson’s old comrade from the Creek War and New Orleans, were among Jackson’s most trusted friends. Lewis later helped manage the newspaper campaign that pressed the bargain charge through the election of 1828.
Editorial note. The planned item, Jackson’s letter to Lewis of February 27, 1825 (Morgan Library & Museum, MA 811.12), is known here only from the Morgan’s catalog description and manuscript image; no published or open transcription was found, and the Morgan page could not be read online, so it is not reproduced. In its place this reader gives three letters from the same weeks, reproduced from John Spencer Bassett, ed., Correspondence of Andrew Jackson, vol. 3 (Washington, DC: Carnegie Institution of Washington, 1928), 275–78, transcribed from the Internet Archive OCR text layer with obvious OCR errors corrected. Bassett printed Jackson’s spelling and punctuation as written (“warrent,” “offerred,” “consumate”), and these are kept. The two letters to Lewis are complete as Bassett printed them; Bassett’s footnotes are omitted. The letter to Coffee is an excerpt: Jackson’s opening remarks on money matters are omitted, and Bassett printed only part of the letter. These are private letters to political allies and repeat rumor as fact; Jackson never produced evidence of an explicit bargain.
Document Text
Andrew Jackson to William B. Lewis, Senate Chamber, February 7, 1825
D'r Major, The Public Journals will have informed you of the letter to the Editor of the Columbian Observer from a member of Congress, the card of Mr. Clay, and the reply by another Card, and the proceedings of the house of Representatives thereon, who have raised a committee, and referred the vera dictum of Mr. Clay to that committee? This is thought here by many a very novel proceeding without any parliamentary precedent to warrent it. What the committee may do, I cannot conjecture. But you may prepare to hear a debate (if a report is made) of great excitement not very creditable to our country.
It is believed by some, that this course was taken to inveigle Mr. Kreamer into an apology, but if I am a Judge of human nature they have mistook the man, he would suffer Martyrdom before he would descend to such humiliation. I am told he has ample proof of the application of Mr. Clays friends to support his statement in his letter. If this is true, the committee will not open the case for this proof. Should it refuse, there will be no course left Mr. K. but to protest Vs. the proceedings and appeal to the nation. How the Election of President may result is impossible to tell. The rumor of Barter of office, intrigue and corruption still afloat, which I hope for the honor of our country there is no truth in.
I am in haste your friend
Andrew Jackson to William B. Lewis, Washington, February 14, 1825
Dear Major: I am informed this day by Colo. R. M. Johnston of the Senate that Mr. Clay has been offerred the office of Sec of State, and that he will accept it. So you see the Judas of the West has closed the contract and will receive the thirty pieces of silver. his end will be the same. Was there ever witnessed such a bare faced corruption in any country before? The Senate, (if this Nomination is sent to it) will do its duty. No imputation will be left at its door. We will soon be with you, farewell.
Andrew Jackson to John Coffee, Washington, February 19, 1825
[. . .]
Mr Donelson has wrote you at my request to day, and to his letter I refer you, for the result of the Presidential election. I weep for the liberty of my country when I see at this early day of its “successful experiment”, that corruption has been imputed to many members of the House of Representatives, and the rights of the people have been bartered for promises of office, Mr Clay their chief has recd by way of the fulfilment of the rumoured contract with him a tender of the office of sec of state, and it is said he has agreed to accept it. How far the senate will support his nomination and consumate this corruption a few days will determine. There are many of them I am sure will never do an act to cover themselves with the imputation of favouring such corruption.
[. . .]
