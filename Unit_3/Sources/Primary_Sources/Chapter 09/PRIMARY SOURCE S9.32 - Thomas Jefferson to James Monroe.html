Thomas Jefferson to James Monroe
Thomas Jefferson · March 3, 1820 (private letter)
 
Field Detail
TOC Fit Chapter 9 · Essay prompt 9.3 (The Missouri Crisis)
Why Here Writing as the compromise was being completed, Jefferson calls a Missouri settlement drawn “by a geographical line of division” the most ominous question he had seen and accuses northern Federalist leaders of using it to regain power. Students can weigh it as evidence that southern Republicans read the crisis as a struggle over party and sectional power.

Historical Context
In February 1820 President James Monroe wrote twice to Thomas Jefferson about the Missouri debates in Congress, reporting the Senate’s proposal to admit Missouri without restriction on slavery while barring slavery north of latitude 36°30′ in the rest of the Louisiana Purchase. The House and Senate accepted the bargain on March 2–3, 1820, and Monroe signed the bills on March 6.
Jefferson’s reply begins with a recommendation for an old Rhode Island ally, Christopher Ellery, before turning to Missouri. The two men he names were New Yorkers: Senator Rufus King, the Federalist leader of the restrictionists, who had been the Federalist presidential candidate in 1816, and Governor DeWitt Clinton, a Republican rival of the Virginia leadership who had run against Madison with Federalist support in 1812.
Editorial note. Reproduced from the transcription published on Founders Online (National Archives), from The Papers of Thomas Jefferson, Retirement Series, vol. 15 (2018), p. 441; the original is in the James Monroe Papers at the Library of Congress. Jefferson’s lowercase sentence openings and spelling are kept. The enclosed letter from Charles Collins and the modern editors’ notes are not reproduced. CAUTION: Jefferson’s charges against King and Clinton are partisan accusations made privately, not established facts about their motives.
Document Text
Monticello Mar. 3. 20.
Dear Sir
I am often placed under the dilemma of either alienating my old friends, or of giving you the trouble of reading a letter, and I have had too many proofs of your friendship not to know you will take that trouble to save me from so painful an alternative. I know your difficulties, and after giving my testimony, I pay no attention to the result, leaving that to yourself who alone have a view of the whole ground. Christopher Ellery, the subject of the inclosed letter from mr Collins was one of the most active of our friends in R.I. in the times of our trials whether our government should be republican in practice as well as profession. he came into the Senate when I came into the administration and assisted in giving us a majority in that body. nor was there a more zealous or active one in it. he is a good republican, a good man and of good understanding.
I am indebted to you for your two letters of Feb. 7. & 19. this Missouri question by a geographical line of division is the most portentous one I have ever contemplated. King is ready to risk the union for any chance of restoring his party to power and wriggling himself to the head of it. nor is Clinton without his hopes nor scrupulous as to the means of fulfilling them. I hope I shall be spared the pain of witnessing it either by the good sense of the people, or by the more certain reliance, the hand of death. on this or that side of the Styx I am ever and devotedly yours.
