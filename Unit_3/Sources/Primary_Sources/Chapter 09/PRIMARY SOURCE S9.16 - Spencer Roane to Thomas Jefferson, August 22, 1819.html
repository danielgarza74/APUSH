Why Here A short cover letter in which the author of the “Hampden” essays sends them to Jefferson. Students can see how Virginia Republicans ranked the McCulloch decision—“as cardinal” as independence itself—and how they sought the founders’ authority for their attack on the Court.
Treatment Public-domain text, complete
Historical Context
In March 1819 the Supreme Court upheld the Second Bank of the United States in McCulloch v. Maryland. Within weeks, Virginia Republicans answered in Thomas Ritchie’s Richmond Enquirer, the leading Republican newspaper in the South. Spencer Roane (1762–1822), a judge of Virginia’s highest court, Ritchie’s cousin, and a son-in-law of Patrick Henry, wrote four essays signed “Hampden” (June 11–22, 1819) charging that Marshall’s broad reading of the “necessary and proper” clause would leave the federal government without limits. Many Virginians believed Jefferson would have named Roane chief justice had the post fallen vacant during his presidency.
Roane sent printed copies of the essays to Jefferson and, the same day, to James Madison. Jefferson replied on September 6 with strong agreement. Roane’s letters show the essays circulating as a deliberate campaign to rally the party’s senior figures against the Court.
Editorial note. This reader reproduces the letter from Founders Online (National Archives), which prints it from The Papers of Thomas Jefferson, Retirement Series, vol. 14, 1 February to 31 August 1819 (pp. 626–27). Spelling, capitalization, and punctuation are as transcribed there; the modern editors’ annotations and note numbers are not reproduced. Roane’s “enclosed numbers” were the four “Hampden” essays. A nearly identical letter of the same date went to James Madison (also in this reader).
Document Text
Richmond, Augst 22d 1819. Dear Sir.
The enclosed numbers, written by me, were published a few weeks ago, in the Enquirer. They relate to a subject as cardinal, in my judgment, as that which involved our Independence. Mr Ritchie had some extra Copies struck, and has furnished me with a few, to be distributed among my particular, and my distinguished friends. I presume to ask your acceptance of a Copy.
The friends of liberty in our Country continue to regard you with veneration and gratitude, for your great & Eminent services:—and your opinion on such a question as this, would be considered as a great authority.
Be pleased to accept the renewed assurances of my high consideration, respect, and Esteem.
I am, Dear sir, yr obt St Spencer Roane
