Historical Context
John C. Calhoun (1782–1850) of South Carolina served as secretary of war from 1817 to 1825. In these years he was a strong nationalist who favored internal improvements, a protective tariff, and a larger army; only later did he become the leading theorist of states’ rights and nullification. Andrew Jackson, the hero of New Orleans, commanded the army’s Southern Division and had invaded Spanish Florida in 1818. In the spring of 1820 Jackson told Calhoun he planned to leave the army, and he wrote gloomily about the nation’s future.
Congress had passed the Missouri Compromise in March 1820, but Missouri’s formal admission still waited on its new state constitution. Calhoun also referred to the Adams-Onís Treaty, signed in 1819, by which Spain agreed to cede Florida to the United States; Spain delayed ratifying it until 1820–21, and some western members of Congress opposed it because it gave up American claims to Texas.
Editorial note. Reproduced from the text printed in John Spencer Bassett, ed., Correspondence of Andrew Jackson, vol. 3 (Washington, DC: Carnegie Institution of Washington, 1928), p. 24, as presented in the Library of Congress’s online text for the original letter in the Andrew Jackson Papers. Bassett’s printing ends with the sentence on Pensacola given here; the manuscript at the Library of Congress fills eight images, so readers wanting anything beyond the printed text should consult the manuscript. Calhoun’s spellings (“seperation,” “Pansacola”) are kept. The date of January 8 refers to the Battle of New Orleans (1815).
Document Text
Private
War Department, June 1, 1820.
Dear Sir, I have received your unofficial letters of the 15th April and 5th of May; and I regard your determination to retire from the Army with that regret, which I am sure a great majority of our people will feel on the occasion. To you and the army the seperation must be painful. Your name will forever be associated with the most brilliant pages of our history; and the memory of the 8th of January will, to the latest posterity, rouse the American Army to the highest feats of valour. To be so connected with our military institutions and history must render your adieu to the army painful indeed.
I perceive you have strong foreboding as to our future policy. The discussion on the Missouri question has undoubtedly contributed to weaken in some degree the attachment of our southern and western people to the Union; but the agitators of that question have, in my opinion, not only completely failed; but have destroyed to a great extent their capacity for future mischief. Should Missouri be admitted at the next session, as I think she will without difficulty, the evil effects of the discussion must gradually subside. As it regards the army and the acquisition of Florida, the greatest opposition has by no means been experienced to the east. However strange it may appear, the portion of our country, which appears to have the greatest interest in the acquisition, I mean the West, was probably that, in which the most opposition was experienced. So unnatural a state of things cannot exist long. The west must see, that the single Port of Pansacola is to it, invaluable. The protection of the commerce of the Mississippi must in a great measure depend on our acquisition of it
