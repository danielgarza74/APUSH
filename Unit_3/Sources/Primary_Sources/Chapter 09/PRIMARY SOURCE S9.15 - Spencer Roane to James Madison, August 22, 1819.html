Historical Context
In March 1819 the Supreme Court upheld the Second Bank of the United States in McCulloch v. Maryland. Within weeks, Virginia Republicans answered in Thomas Ritchie’s Richmond Enquirer, the leading Republican newspaper in the South. Spencer Roane (1762–1822), a judge of Virginia’s highest court, Ritchie’s cousin, and a son-in-law of Patrick Henry, wrote four essays signed “Hampden” (June 11–22, 1819) charging that Marshall’s broad reading of the “necessary and proper” clause would leave the federal government without limits. Many Virginians believed Jefferson would have named Roane chief justice had the post fallen vacant during his presidency.
Madison was a delicate ally for Roane. He had opposed the First Bank in 1791 but signed the bill chartering the Second Bank in 1816, accepting it as settled by long practice. He answered Roane on September 2, 1819, objecting to the Court’s reasoning while avoiding Roane’s more sweeping conclusions.
Editorial note. This reader reproduces the letter from Founders Online (National Archives), which prints it from The Papers of James Madison, Retirement Series, vol. 1, 4 March 1817–31 January 1820 (pp. 499–500). Spelling, capitalization, and punctuation are as transcribed there; the modern editors’ annotations and note numbers are not reproduced. Roane’s “enclosed Numbers” were the four “Hampden” essays published in the Richmond Enquirer, June 11–22, 1819. A nearly identical letter of the same date went to Thomas Jefferson (also in this reader); only the second paragraph differs.
Document Text
Richmond, august 22d. 1819. Dear Sir.
The enclosed Numbers, written by me, were published, a few weeks ago, in the Enquirer. They relate to a subject as cardinal, in my judgment, as that which involved our Independence. Mr. Ritchie had some extra Copies struck, & has furnished me with a few, to be distributed among my particular, & my distinguished friends. I presume to ask your acceptance of a Copy.
No man in our Country has done so much as you, in Establishing our present happy system of government, or can feel a greater interest in preserving it.
Be pleased to accept the renewed assurances of my high consideration, respect, and Esteem. I am, Dear Sir, yr obt. Servt.
Spencer Roane
