Historical Context
The Constitutional Whig, founded in Richmond in 1824 and edited by John Hampden Pleasants, was the main rival of Thomas Ritchie’s Richmond Enquirer. Pleasants supported Adams and Clay in 1828. His editorial, written on the morning of November 11 and printed the next day, concedes Virginia to Jackson and blames the “Junto in Richmond,” Ritchie’s circle, and national party agents such as Amos Kendall and Duff Green for the result.
The editorial’s list of rumors spread about Adams, its contempt for the “dupes” who believed them, and its claim that the educated favored Adams reveal how anti-Jacksonians understood the new mass politics. The New York report that follows, hopeful for Adams, also gives a glimpse of Tammany Hall’s “regular nominations” and of the split ticket that left New York’s electoral vote divided.

TUESDAY MORNING, NOV. 11, 1828.
“Blessed are they that expect little, for they shall not be disappointed.”
The returns already received, leave but little doubt that the vote of Virginia has gone for Jackson by a large majority. It is indeed still within the power of the western counties to bring up lee-way, but from the complexion of partial returns, that is not to be hoped for. In another place the reader will see all the returns that have yet reached us.
Had any man predicted this result four years ago, he would have met with the fate of Cassandra. Within two years, a revolution has been effected as remarkable, as unexpected, as unforeseen, as any on record. How this has been effected—what artifices have been resorted to, to delude and dupe the people of Virginia—what degradation certain politicians have incurred in accomplishing an event which they formerly bewailed as the most disastrous that the imagination could picture—it is useless now to consider. Suffice it to say, that mendacity the most unblushing, tricks the most scandalous, vigilance the most sleepless, bullying, boasting, vaunting and raving the most disgusting—every meanness that honour scorns, and unprincipled demagogues ever employed, has been called into use. In one place, the ignorant have been assured that the low price of produce was caused by the administration—in another that Mr. Adams had sold the Colonial trade for a price—in a third that he had declared no poor man should hold an office under him—in a fourth, that he had invited the King of England to pay him a visit. In short, lies have been every where invented to suit the grade of the understanding of those upon whom they were intended to operate—or that were calculated to enlist the feelings of self interest against the administration. We have scores of letters which we have never published, from all parts of Virginia, disclosing those base and infamous fabrications. We certainly never expected to see in our generation, so total an abandonment of principle, self-respect and shame; manifested too in many instances, by men who hold some station in society, and who have (or had) some character to lose. That such arts should be employed by the baser agents of party—by such men as Kendall and Green, we nothing admire—but that they should be invoked by the description of persons mentioned above, and that they should have met with such success is what we had believed impossible. We are persuaded that the Demagogues themselves who have wheedled the multitude in Virginia, are astonished at their own success, and feel their respect for their dupes sensibly abated.
We have never seriously hoped, much less claimed, the vote of Virginia for Mr. Adams. If momentarily, we have indulged such an expectation, it has speedily yielded to a calm survey of the host of southern and personal predilections arrayed in favor of Jackson, and the no less numerous prejudices, sectional and personal, enlisted against Mr. Adams. Upon this state of things, the Junto in Richmond speculated; using their utmost exertions to increase popular prejudice, to exasperate sectional feeling, to exclude correct information, and to force an appeal to the passions of the public, instead of its reason and judgment. They have succeeded in their speculations, but whether an honest pride, or a secret shame at the badness of their course, and the unworthy arts by which it has been carried, predominates in their feelings, we have no means of determining. We should imagine that the latter feeling, at least had the mastery with those who were wont to deprecate Jackson’s election as “a curse.” We should imagine that the very consistent Mr. Ritchie, the august Lt. Governor, who as late as the fall of 1827 viewed the election of Jackson with agitated nerves, and the worthy Jackson Elector for this District, who in 1824 could not speak of the probability of his success without piously invoking Heavenly interference—we should imagine, that these most consistent gentlemen in their secret souls, feel ashamed of themselves and their success.
The vote that Mr. Adams has received in Virginia, is to the last degree honourable and flattering to him. It has been given in opposition to the prejudices of the country. No man has voted for him, who had not strength of mind and generosity of character, sufficient to subdue the bias of education, the influence of localities, and to despise that love of a majority so common in little minds. The vote has been given not to the man, but from a persuasion of his capacity and honesty. It was as little personal, as a vote could be. The imagination, which did so much for Gen. Jackson, did nothing for Mr. Adams.
While we cheerfully acknowledge that thousands of honourable men voted for Gen. Jackson, it is unquestionable that the weight of the intelligence and character of the country has been opposed to him.
[. . .]
New York.—The promises of the friends of the Administration in this State are fulfilling. There is now, little cause to doubt the choice of 22 or 23 Administration Electors; and provided the Western votes calculated upon for Mr. Adams remain stedfast, his election will be certain.
The City of New York has chosen the Jackson Electors by a great majority. This was known beforehand. The interior, however, manifests a different feeling. The city and county of Albany, claimed by the Jacksonians, have chosen Mr. Van Vechten, the Administration Elector—so have Queens and Suffolk, Columbia, Rensselaer, Saratoga, Washington and Oneida: seven which are definitively heard from.
In the city of New York, the Tammany ticket for Congress, (Verplanck, Cambreleng and White,) has been elected. The Anti Auctionists also ran a ticket, but unsuccessfully. Noah, who received the regular nomination at Tammany Hall, for Sheriff of New York, has been defeated by a decisive majority. This is an infraction of the principle of regular nominations, singular enough. Of all men living, Noah has insisted most strongly upon the sacredness of regular nominations, and done most to sustain them. That he alone of the whole ticket regularly nominated, at Tammany Hall, should lose his election, is cruel. We expect to see him with his characteristic versatility, turn the battery of his wit and humour against what is really and intrinsically wrong—the system of regular nominations.
