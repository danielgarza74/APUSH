“The Lowell Offering” and the Literature of the Lowell Mill Girls
Patrick Hastings
 
AUTHOR Patrick Hastings (Library of Congress staff blogger)
WEBSITE Bibliomania, Library of Congress
PUBLICATION March 4, 2026
CHAPTER / PROMPTS Chapter 8 | 8.3
ARGUMENT ROLES 8.3.1 Support; 8.3.2 Support
Excerpt · 821 words (complete article)

After a twelve-hour day of grueling industrial labor, the young female workers of Lowell, Massachusetts spent the evening hours engaged in educational pursuits and cultural activities ranging from piano to poetry to political activism. The most enduring product of these activities is The Lowell Offering, a literary magazine “wholly written by females employed in the mills.”

Charles Dickens visited Lowell during his first tour through America in the early months of 1842. He toured a town built around integrated textile factories with a workforce of roughly 8,000 female textile workers known as the Lowell Mill Girls. These young women, typically between 15-30 years old, came from rural New England in search of paid work, independence, and educational opportunities.

“Lowell, Mass., Mills on Merrimack River.” c. 1900. Detroit Publishing Company Photograph Collection, Prints and Photographs Division.

Dickens, known for his criticism of the dehumanizing effects of the industrial system, compared the Lowell Mills favorably against the harsh conditions of the English factories when he published an account of his travels titled American Notes for General Circulation in October of 1842.

Charles Dickens wrote about the cultural enrichment opportunities available to the Lowell Mill Girls. He also promoted The Lowell Offering as a journal of high literary merit.

In American Notes, Dickens commented on the Lowell Mill workers’ comfortable living conditions, their access to good healthcare, and their opportunities for education, noting that “nearly all these young ladies subscribe to circulating libraries.” Dickens also noted that the Lowell Mill Girls “have got up among themselves a periodical called The Lowell Offering, a repository of original articles written exclusively by females actively employed in the mills.”

After reading “four hundred good solid pages” of The Lowell Offering, Dickens declared that “it will compare advantageously with a great many English Annuals.” This journal, published at irregular intervals between 1840-45, featured essays, poetry, and works of fiction written and edited by the Lowell Mill Girls.

The first essay in the first issue of The Lowell Offering, “Defence of Factory Girls,” responded to an article by Orestes Brownson, a prominent Transcendentalist writer and activist. In the Boston Quarterly Review, Brownson suggested a moral dark side to working in the mills; he wrote that to attach the label “‘She has worked in a factory’ is almost enough to damn to infamy the most worthy and virtuous girl.” The Lowell Offering published Harriet Farley’s blistering article in response, accusing Brownson of slander and asserting that “our well filled churches and lecture halls will testify that the state of morals and intelligence is not low.” Writing as an anonymous “Factory Girl,” Farley demanded that Brownson’s statement be “publicly retracted. If he still doubts upon the subject, let him come among us.”

The pieces presented in The Lowell Offering offer a range of attitudes toward the conditions and lived experience of the workers. The journal published dozens of authors; some are critical, satirical, or explicit in demands for improved conditions, and others celebrate the opportunities and freedoms available in Lowell that were otherwise rare to young females in this era. Yes, the company provided workers with a lecture series, piano lessons, literary meetings, and access to a lending library – but only in the evenings after they had worked 12-hour shifts in loud, poorly ventilated factories. The Lowell Offering presented different sides of the debate over working conditions, women’s rights, and the rise of the Lowell Female Labor Reform Association, the first trade union of industrial women in the U.S.

Mind Amongst the Spindles: A Miscellany Wholly Composed by the Factory Girls. Boston: Jordan, Swift, and Wiley, 1845.

The best and most influential pieces from The Lowell Offering were gathered for inclusion in an anthology titled Mind Amongst the Spindles: A Miscellany Wholly Composed by the Factory Girls (1845). Among the thirty-seven pieces selected for this anthology, we find works of realistic fiction depicting the lives of factory workers, literary criticism comparing poetic modes, Romantic essays depicting excursions into the New England countryside, and further varieties of writing. The book’s “Introduction” includes a lengthy and laudatory letter by the prominent English sociologist and feminist Harriet Martineau, who toured Lowell with Ralph Waldo Emerson during a lecture series he was delivering to the factory workers.

The Lowell Offering provided a model for publications in other mill towns, but not all readers or factory workers celebrated the magazine. Sarah G. Bagley, the first female labor leader in the U.S., for example, publicly condemned the magazine for refusing to publish her most critical pieces against the working conditions in the mills. Bagley would become editor of the Voice of Industry magazine that presented a more militant attitude toward labor issues.

Volumes of The Lowell Offering and other materials related to industrial working conditions are available in the Rare Book Reading Room for researchers and interested readers to explore, and the Library’s catalog record for this journal includes links to digitized copies available via HathiTrust.
