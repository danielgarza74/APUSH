HUMBOLDT JOURNAL OF SOCIAL RELATIONS Vol. 13:1 & 2 (Fall/Winter & Spring/Summer 1985/86) 374-386 THE INTERSECTION OF RACE AND GENDER: THE ANTEBELLUM PLANTATION MISTRESS AND HER SLAVES* Marli F. Weiner Department of History University of Vermont Burlington, VT 05405 ABSTRACT This paper examines plantation mistresses' efforts to improve the living conditions and enhance the well being of the slaves in their care. It explains those efforts in light of work patterns, the ideology of domesticity, paternalism, and southern efforts to explain slavery as a positive good. These forces generally led women to mediate the harshest aspects of slavery while their husbands devoted themselves to the business of agriculture. The efforts made by mid-nineteenth century white southerners to extol the virtues of their region could not disguise the harshness and brutality that lay just beneath the surface of its famed hospitable mansions and lush fields ripe with king cotton. Whites prided themselves on the virtue and order inherent in their institutions and congratulated one another for the advantages slavery provided when compared with the wage slavery of industrial areas, but their statements have a self serving quality about them. The defensiveness of the apologists for slavery, made necessary by growing criticism, imposed a unified view on southern public opinion. Slaves were very well treated; they were happy with their lot; slavery was uplifting, Christianizing, and civilizing them. Unable to convince others of the benefits of their peculiar institution, white southerners ultimately were willing to go to war to protect it. Slavery was too profitable and too central to their view of the world, and the alternatives too painful to contemplate, for southerners not to be anxious to de fend it. Slaveholders who believed slavery to be a positive good were convinced that their behavior justified their rhetoric. They took pride in the quality and quantity of the food, clothing, and shelter they provided, in the access slaves had to religion and medical care, and in the absence of harsh treatment. Slaveholders were convinced that slavery as they practiced it provided the most humane treatment possible for a dependent labor force. The reality, of course, was very different. Although historians' efforts to quantify beatings, sales, and other slave experiences have been quite controversial, there can be no doubt that slaves were not treated as their masters liked to think. Slaves clearly hated slavery and were eager for freedom. However, since freedom was rarely a realizable goal either for the individual or the group, slaves did what they could to make life tolerable for themselves. Their efforts to do so were simultaneously rejected by slaveholders determined to preserve their own authority and acknowledged as necessary to maintaining the harmony of plan tation life. The tension inherent in simultaneously rejecting and acknowl edging the demands made by the slaves was often resolved by assigning each task to different members of the slaveholding family. For the most part, men perceived their responsibility to be rejection of slaves' demands in order to further their own authority and the interests of their family fortunes. Women were given responsibility for acknowledging the claims slaves made for improved conditions, care, and concern. This division of respon sibility according to gender was never deliberately articulated within southern society. However, southern ideals about ap propriate behavior for women encouraged them to identify slaves as among their primary responsibilities, just as appropriate behavior for men urged them toward assertions of authority. Mistresses were effectively assigned responsibility for making slavery a positive good for slaves in daily practice as well as in the theory propounded by their husbands.
