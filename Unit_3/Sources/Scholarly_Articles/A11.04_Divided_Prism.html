A Divided Prism: Two Sources of Black Testimony on Slavery By DAVID THOMAS BAILEY MIRED IN A CYNICAL MOOD, A HISTORIAN MIGHT SUM UP HIS profession in a single thought: a source can be found to prove any- thing. No other group of scholars has tried so valiantly to prove this point as the students of American slavery. From an accessible and fairly small number of sources historians of slavery have produced a variety or, more appropriately, a boiling stew of contradictory writ- ings on all aspects of the peculiar institution. The publication of Robert W. Fogel and Stanley L. Engerman's Time on the Cross produced the most heated recent battle in the study of slavery. Using some well-known and a few less familiar collections of sources, Fogel and Engerman derived some startling and some obvious results. Reaction to their methods and conclu- sions came swiftly. Conventional and quantitative historians, after undertaking detailed examination of Fogel and Engerman's evi- dence, disputed the findings of Time on the Cross on every major point.' An older and less well focused debate has surrounded the use of a group of sources containing the recollections of slaves and former slaves. Included among such "black" sources are autobiographies of former slaves, autobiographical narratives dictated by escaped slaves to abolitionists, antebellum and immediate postwar inter- views, letters written by slaves and former slaves to their masters, an occasional narrative dictated by a black still in bondage, and several sets of twentieth-century interviews with former slaves.2 Recent his- I Fogel and Engerman, Time on the Cross: The Economics of American Negro Slavery (Boston and Toronto, 1974). 2 Various collections include Benjamin Drew, A North-Side View of Slavery. This concentration has come about for two major reasons. The two bodies of sources are by far the largest collections of black testimony about slavery. Moreover, due to republication they have become fairly accessible. Unfortu- nately, no single repository of the autobiographies exists, and al- though most have recently been republished, a few valuable mem- oirs remain relatively hard to find. The twentieth-century interviews have recently become particularly accessible. George P. Rawick col- lected the largest set, the Federal Writers' Project interviews, in his multivolume set, The American Slave. The Rawick collection also includes two volumes which contain part of the product of another study conducted under the direction of Charles Spurgeon Johnson at Fisk University in the late 1920s.3 In the first major twentieth-century study of slavery Ulrich Bon- nell Phillips dismissed autobiographies of former slaves as "of du- bious value."'4 Most historians in the first half of the twentieth cen- tury followed his lead and ignored black testimony about slavery. Although the work of the Writers' Project and the Fisk study pro- duced a small ripple of interest in the opinions of former slaves,5 black sources failed to receive concentrated attention from histo- rians until the 1960s. In 1963 Charles H. Nichols complained that "Nearly everyone concerned with American slavery has had his say, but in our time we have forgotten the testimony of its victims." In response to this problem, Nichols decided to write his account of slavery, Many Thousand Gone, "from the point of view of those whose unpaid labor maintained the plantation system of the Old South."6 Just as Nichols's decision reflected the civil rights move- ment of the late 1950s and early 1960s, John W. Blassingame's ap- proach in The Slave Community mirrored the academic concentra- tion on black studies and black culture that blossomed in American universities in the late sixties.
