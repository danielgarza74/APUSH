Not so well-known, however, is the similar, if less role that played in the origins of the Second spectacular, foreign policy American Party System. If, by the 1820s, the first generation of Americans had demonstrated the viability of the United States and secured the Revolution, they had failed to forge a consensus about how that republic should function and what principles it should exemplify. Nothing reveals this lack of agreement more than the prolonged debate over United States relations with the emerging nations of Latin America. Seeing growing markets and potential republics to the South, some Anglo-Americans sought close ties with the Hispanic Americans and emphasized the similarity of their experiences. Others, fearing the new nations as potentially powerfulrivals and critics, noted intense differences between Anglo and Spanish Americans as obstacles to friendly relations. Originating in economic and political interests, the question of how the United States should deal with the former Spanish colonies was part of a long process of definition for Americans. The debate testifies to how Americans their republic fragile thought was, even in the 1820s, and it demonstrates the tenacity of ideological perspectives formed during the American Revolution. Despite their increasingly partisan behavior, many American leaders in the 1820s saw themselves as engaged in a never-ending struggle to preserve the republic it from the ambitious schemes of apostates. What by protecting was at issue in the debate over how to deal with the Latin Americans was much more than diplomacy or politics—the basic concern of the participants was with defining the nature and responsibilities of the government of the United States.
