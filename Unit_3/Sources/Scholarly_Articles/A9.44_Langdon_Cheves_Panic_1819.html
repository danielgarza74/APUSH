Langdon Cheves and the Panic of 1819: A Reassessment EDWIN J. PERKINS Langdon Cheves's role as president of the Second Bank of the United States (1819-1822) warrants reassessment. First, he djl not save the bank from threat- ened failure, since it was, in truth, already sound. Second, he was irresponsible in accumulating huge excess reserves during the depression year of 1820; reserves estimated here as sufficient to support an increase of up to 17 percent in the volume of notes and deposits in the U.S. money supply. L ANGDON Cheves, when judged by his contemporaries for his performance as president of the Second Bank of the United States from 1819 to 1822, received only mixed reviews, but early twentieth- century historians significantly upgraded his rating and accorded him generally high marks for instituting sound management practices where they were allegedly lacking.' Cheves was summoned to Philadelphia to assume leadership of the bank when his predecessor, William Jones, resigned under a cloud soon after a special congressional committee, formed in 1818, released a lengthy report on its findings.2 Cheves's administration was free of scandal, but not totally exempt from criticism. Some charged that he was too conservative, even for a banker. The most serious accusation was that in his effort to build up the bank's reserves of specie, Cheves had precipitated a panic but then done little to relieve the distress associated with the depression that lasted until 1823. His most vocal critic was William Gouge, an ally of President Andrew Jackson, who asserted that under Cheves's adminis- tration, "the bank was saved and the people were ruined."3 The modern judgment of Cheves's performance has stressed the positive and largely ignored his contemporary critics. Meanwhile, other explanations for the underlying causes of the Panic of 1819 have tended to absolve Cheves of any blame for its occurrence. The traditional interpretation points mainly to Jones as the prepetrator of the panic because of his allegedly inflationary lending policies in 1817 and 1818. Recently, Richard Timberlake has stressed the culpability of the Treasury Department in placing the bank and economy in an extremely tenuous position in 1817 and 1819.6 Other accounts focused on changes in the international economy, particularly the sharp drop in prices for American exports.7 In all of these scenarios, Cheves has not been held accountable for the outcome. Instead, he has been portrayed as a man who carried out effectively a series of unpopular, but absolutely necessary, policies which were required to support the bank and eliminate the excesses that had crept into the American financial system. It is not surprising that Cheves has received such good press in historical accounts, since a close inspection of primary sources reveals that the present favorable assessment can be traced back largely to his own pen. Most of our knowledge of his presidency is found in a long, retrospective narrative covering his years in office, which Cheves presented to the bank's stockholders upon announcing his retirement in the fall of 1822.8 A lawyer by training, Cheves drafted a masterful brief in his own behalf. Taken as gospel by modern historians of the bank, who had high regard for conservatism, the myth of Cheves's heroics has been reinforced by constant repetition.9 The time for a reassessment is long overdue. The more equivocal judgment of nineteenth-century observers was closer to the mark. In truth, Cheves did not save the bank, despite the remarks in his self- serving narrative. An analysis of the bank's financial condition and a more critical reading of his narrative reveal that the Second Bank was not in any serious danger by the time he assumed office in March 1819. The only genuine crisis had passed six to nine months earlier in the summer and fall of 1818.
