THE JOURNAL OF ECONOMIC HISTORY VOLUME XXXVI DECEMBER 1976 NUMBER 4 The Antebellum Money Market and the Economic Impact of the Bank War 3 HIS paper examines the role of the Bank War in the economic events of the 1830's by developing and testing a structural economic model of the financial sector of the antebellum United States economy. By the Bank War I mean those political and economic events which occurred during the final years of the federal charter of the Second Bank of the United States (hereafter referred to as the Second National Bank). Four years before its charter was to expire, the subject of renewal became a major issue in the presidential campaign of 1832. In early 1832, Congress passed a recharter bill, but Jackson vetoed it, and his veto was sustained. Historians treating this subject have disagreed sharply in their interpretations of the subsequent economic events of the 1830's and of the economic significance of the failure to recharter the Bank. This general subject has been examined at great length by American historians, but basically, the literature can be classified into two groups, the traditional and the revisionist schools. Briefly, the members of the traditional school share the following general interpretation of the economic events of the 1830's.1 Jackson's veto of the bill to recharter the Second National Bank removed any effective restraints upon the behavior of the commercial banking sector. As a result, banks engaged in excessive note issue and specula- tive loan policies without proper specie backing for their liabilities. Journal of Economic History, Vol. XXXVI, No. 4 (December 1976). The resulting instability was further aggravated by Jackson's other economic policies, particularly the Specie Circular of 1836, which required payment in specie for all public land purchases. In the view of the traditional school, this combination of events culminated in the Panic of 1837. According to the revisionists, however, Jackson's veto and his subsequent economic policies had no discernible effects on the economic events of the 1830's.2 The commercial banking system was not affected by the removal of the federal authority vested in the Second National Bank. Furthermore, there is no quantitative evi- dence of the existence of wildcat banking practices. Thus, the re- visionist school rejects the idea that Jackson's veto and subsequent economic policies led the country into the Panic of 1837. Instead, they offer the hypothesis that the Panic of 1837 was precipitated by international events which were beyond domestic control. In addition to the differences in their interpretations of the economic impact of the Bank War, these two groups use quite differ- ent research methodologies. For the most part, the research of the traditional school concentrates on a subjective interpretation of events, personalities, and historical theses. The work of the revisionist school is characterized by the use of descriptive statistical tools in an attempt to analyze developments empirically. The objective of my study is to synthesize the hypotheses of these schools and to test them empirically by applying modern econometric techniques in an economic framework drawn from contemporary monetary and macroeconomic theory. More specifically, three ques- tions are drawn from the literature: One, did the Bank War have any economic repercussions? Two, were wildcat banking practices charac- teristic of this period? Three, what were the causes of the Panic of 1837? In my view the major problem with the previous research on this topic is not the conclusions that have been offered. The difficulty is with the methodology used to obtain these conclusions.
