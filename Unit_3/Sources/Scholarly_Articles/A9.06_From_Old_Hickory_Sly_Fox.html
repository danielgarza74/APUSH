Selection Overview

Brown examines the alliance between Andrew Jackson, heroic founder of the Democratic party, and Martin Van Buren, its masterful organization builder. He uses Max Weber to define charisma as a quality followers ascribe to a leader in times of crisis, and sets out to explain how Jackson's partisans tried to institutionalize that charisma in the national party, opening Van Buren's path to succession.

This gap in our knowledge exists, in good part, because Van Buren's accession was intricately related to the complex process whereby some of the partisan followers of Jackson attempted to institutionalize Jackson's personal charisma in the national Democratic party. Any mention of charisma, given the prevalent overuse and misuse of the word, is bound to arouse misgivings. In everyday discourse, charisma has become a commodity serviceable for enhancing the vendibility of mouthwash and toothpaste. Max Weber's own writings on the concept have contributed to the confusion and misunderstandings that mar most uses of it today. But recent scholarship on charisma, along with a close scrutiny of Weber's work on the subject, have given it a degree of rigor that makes it a valuable analytical tool in the study of political and social movements.3 Weber defined charisma as "a certain quality of an individual personality by virtue of which he is considered extraordinary and treated as endowed with supernatural, superhuman, or at least specifically exceptional qualities.1"4 The relevant point about this definition is that, contrary to prevailing belief, charisma is not a quality that inheres in the charismatic leader, independently of historical or social context. Rather, charisma is ascribed to the leader by his followers; as Weber himself observed, "it is recognition on the part of those subject to authority which is decisive for the validity of charisma.
