Selection Overview

Wigger traces American Methodism's dramatic growth from fewer than 1,000 members in 1770 to over 250,000 by 1820, and to more than 34 percent of church members by 1850. He argues that Methodism, with its demanding class meetings and wide circle of adherents, led the popularization of religion after the American Revolution and expressed the cultural ethos of the early republic.

In 1775 fewer than one out of every 800 Americans was a Methodist; by 1812 it was one out of every 36. While other denomi- nations expanded in absolute numbers, the Methodists gained an ever larger share of the religious market. At mid-century American Methodism was nearly half again as large as any other Protestant body and al- most ten times the size of the Congregationalists, America's largest denomination in 1776.3 These figures are even more impressive when one considers the church's wider influence. In the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, membership requirements were relatively strict and disci- pline, for the most part, was firmly adhered to. All members, for ex- ample, were required to attend a weekly "class meeting" where their spiritual lives and temporal dealings were open to examination by the class leader and the preacher, if one happened to be present, and where they might be asked to pray in public. This, no doubt, was a threatening proposition for many. Hence it is not surprising that many who had Methodist sympathies declined to enter into full "con- nection" with the church. In 1805 Francis Asbury estimated that membership stood at 100,000, but that up to one million people "reg- ularly attend our ministry."4 Using a more conservative ratio of eight adherents for every one member, it is still reasonable to assume that by 1790 Methodist adherents accounted for almost 15 percent of the aggregate American population, and by 1820 nearly 21 percent (see Graph 1). Clearly, American Methodism played a leading role in the popu- larization of religion following the American Revolution.
