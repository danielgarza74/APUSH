Selection Overview

Satz recounts the Cherokee departure on the Trail of Tears in October 1838 under the Treaty of 1835 and weighs estimates of the dead, from the long-cited four thousand, about a quarter of the tribe, to Thornton's higher figure and Prucha's lower one. He notes that removal drew wide sympathy in the 1830s and that historians have long stressed its moral weight.

Satz hundred and fifty years ago, during the first week of October 1838, the majority of the Cherokee people who had steadfastly resisted removal from their ancestral southern Appalachian homeland under the terms of the Treaty of 1835, began their westward trek to present-day Oklahoma. 432 Georgia Historical Quarterly though the actual population loss may never be known, the disruptions caused by the removal crisis and the hardships suf- fered by the Cherokees en route to the West have long been acknowledged by scholars.3 Efforts to evict the Cherokee people from their a ncestral lands elicited a great deal of sympathy for the Indians in the 1830s. The issue was debated in the press and on the floor of Congress. Francis Paul Prucha, a contemporary scholar of In- dian-white relations, argues that the removal question "agitated the nation to its very roots, . . . and [that] it touched the funda- mental conception of the United States as a Christian nation."4 Generations of historians and popular writers have r eminded the American people of the numerous indignities suffered by the Cherokees. Historian Carl Degler, for example, claimed that "in the confrontation between the American Indian and the white man over the course of the past three centuries, no single episode is more revealing or more devastating in its moral implications than the removal of the Cherokees."5 In a study of Indian removal aimed at a popular audience, Dale Van Every referred to the controversy over the forced removal of the Cherokees as "an issue impregnated with the most fun- damental bearings on the evolution of the American form of government under the Constitution."6 The historical evidence clearly supports the assertions of these authors. 3Francis Paul Prucha argues that scholars have been too prone "to accept uncriti- cally greatly exaggerated" mortality figures "because of the injustice involved in the removal and the hardships endured." Using statistics reported by Commissioner of Indian Affairs T. Hartley Crawford in a letter to Secretary of War Joel R. Poinsett, dated August 8, 1840, H. R. Doc. 1098, 27th Cong., 2d sess., August 27, 1842, 9-10, he asserts that fewer than 10 percent of the sixteen thousand emigrants died on the journey. Prucha also cites Donald R. Englund's 1973 University of Oklahoma Ph.D. dissertation, "A Demographic Study of the Cherokee Nation," which concludes that about two thousand Cherokees died as a result of removal. Although Prucha challenges the validity of higher morality figures, he concedes that "this Trail of Tears reaped a heavy harvest of misery and death."
