Historians over the last fifty years have largely done the same, treating the status of slavery in Arkansas as a footnote to the national debate that ultimately yielded the Missouri Compromise. However, Ar - kansas was no mere sideshow. Instead, discussions and decisions about its organization helped determine what the future held for relations between the states and federal government. Federal endorsement of slavery in Ar- kansas Territory reinforced the sectional tensions and tortuous relation- ship between slave and free states that precipitated the Civil War roughly forty years later. 1 Historians both of Arkansas and of the politics of slavery’s expansion have traditionally seen the decisions that congressmen made about slav- ery in Arkansas as the product of a compromising strategy intended to maintain the political balance of power between North and South. They have seemed content with caricatures of northern politicians seeking to restrict slavery’s expansion in order chiefly to maintain their section’s strength in the Senate and Electoral College. To understand the nuances of northerners’ posture toward the territory—why some northern congressmen accepted an enslaved Arkansas while others chafed at its existence—requires examinations of larger debates occurring in these de- cades over slavery’s expansion, the future of the Democratic-Republican Party, and the extent of federal power. 1819 marked a significant departure in discussions of slavery and free - dom. In the aftermath of that year’s landmark Supreme Court decision, McCulloch v. Maryland , which seemed to enshrine a broad construction of the Constitution, old-style republicans feared nationalistic approaches to domestic policy that expanded the power of the federal government all the more. These fears of federal expansion occurred at the same time North and South fought over the role of state and federal governments in the recapture of fugitive slaves—a debate that came to pervade antebellum America. Similarly, 1819 represented the culmination of northerners’ fun - damental turn away from slavery. Gradual abolition programs in northern states had by this time largely rid their states of slaves. The debate over Arkansas and Missouri was driven by northerners’ wish not only to limit slavery’s political power but to further insulate themselves from the in- creasingly undesirable institution. New York, the home of Congressman John Taylor, who led the fight to restrict slavery in Arkansas, had just en - acted a law in 1817 that provided freedom as of July 4, 1827 for all slaves unaffected by New York’s 1799 gradual abolition law.
