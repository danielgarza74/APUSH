Selection Overview

Bergeron explains why Tennessee did not rally behind South Carolina in the nullification crisis, rejecting Freehling's picture of a "passionately pro-Jackson" border state. He finds no evidence that Tennesseans tied slavery to nullification in 1832 and 1833, despite a slave population of 141,603 by 1830, and describes a state of warring political factions.

Tennessee's Response to the Nullification Crisis By PAUL H. BERGERON MANY STUDENTS OF SOUTHERN HISTORY AND OF THE JACKSON period have been impressed with Professor William W. Freeh- ling's stimulating study of the nullification controversy in South Carolina. His contention that the citizens of the Palmetto State in 1832 were disturbed by slavery agitation as well as the pro- tective tariff has provoked much thought about the character of the nullification conflict, most of which has centered around the abolitionist threat which Freehling maintains was felt in South Carolina.1 It is well known that Tennessee and other sister states of the South did not support South Carolina's nullification cause. Yet very little has been done toward making intensive studies of the reactions of these states. Perhaps Professor Freehling's observation that Tennessee in 1832 was "a mildly antitariff and passionately pro-Jackson border state" reflects a commonly held interpretation of that state's position,2 but such an expla- nation of Tennessee's failure to rally to South Carolina's side is at best a gross oversimplification. The slavery question simply was not an issue in Tennessee in 1832 and 1833. There seems to be no evidence of concern about slavery in relationship to the nullification controversy.3 The 1830 census reveals that the state had a sizable slave popu- lation, though only about half that of South Carolina. In the latter year the slave pop- ulation accounted for slightly over 20 percent of the state's total population, while slaves constituted approximately 54 per- cent of South Carolina's population.4 These figures suggest that Tennessee was very much involved with the peculiar institu- tion, though clearly not to the same extent as South Carolina. If Professor Freehling's estimate that fear for the possible fate of slavery helped lead South Carolina to nullification is correct, the smaller concentration of blacks in Tennessee might explain why a majority of its citizens were not as sensitive on this score as were the nullifiers of the Palmetto State and therefore did not perceive a threat to slavery in 1832. But, again, if Freehling's analysis is right, it is more difficult to explain why at least some Tennesseans did not share the nullifiers' apprehensions and attempt to unite public support behind South Carolina to combat the abolitionist threat. Professor Freehling seems equally mistaken with regard to Tennessee politics. Far from being "passionately pro-Jackson," it was a state of warring and complicated factions. On the eve of the nullification crisis there were three recognizable political groups. The old faction headed by John Overton of Nashville was still furnishing leadership for the Jackson cause in Tennes- see. Two of its principal lieutenants, John H. Eaton and William B. Lewis, had been a part of the national administration. In opposition to the Overton-Lewis-Eaton clique was its traditional rival, the Andrew Erwin group. This anti-Jackson alliance had been competing for power in Tennessee for slightly more than a decade. It had enjoyed at one time or another the support of such men as Senator John Williams, Newton Cannon, William Carroll, and others.' But by 1830 a new pro-Jackson faction, composed of men resentful of the Overton-Lewis-Eaton domination, had developed. Included within its ranks were Hugh Lawson White, who had defected from the Overton group, Felix Grundy, James K. Polk, Cave Johnson, and Andrew J. Donelson.
