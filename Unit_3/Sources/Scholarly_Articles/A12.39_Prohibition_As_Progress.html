Selection Overview

Rorabaugh argues that by the 1840s total abstinence had become a popular idea reaching beyond temperance societies and their middle-class leaders. To measure ordinary opinion, he examines New York's 1846 license elections, in which each township voted on whether to permit retail liquor sales, and compares the mostly rural results with census data and partisan returns.

PROHIBITION AS PROGRESS: NEW YORK STATE'S LICENSE ELECTIONS, 1846 During the second quarter of the nineteenth century many Americans quit drinking, denounced alcoholic beverages, and became advocates of prohibition. This anti-liquor movement has received a great deal of attention from historians, but until recently most of that attention has focused on the development of temperance organizations, on the leaders of those organizations and on the rhetoric that these leaders used to exhort Americans to forego alcoholic beverages.l At times this focus has caused a popular broad-based movement to be viewed in an overly narrow fashion. For example, the fact that most temperance leadersr such as Theodore Weld, Justin Edwards, and Lyman Beecher, were middle-class ministers has led to the conclusion that the entire movement was middle-class.2 The middle class certainly did embrace temperance, but only recently have historians defined more precisely the social groups from which the movement derived its greatest support. A few historians who have examined temperance society rosters and newspaper lists of prominent supporters have found temperance to be a concern of manufacturerst commercial farmersl and artisan mechanics who were seeking wealth and respectability.3 These studies have two limitations. First, they focus on citiess at a time when most Americans did not live in cities, and, second, they focus on temperance societies, at a time when most Americans were not members of such organizations.4 By the 1840s total abstinence from alcohol was a popular idea that had transcended both the appeal of membership in a temperance society and the vision and rhetoric of the movement's traditional middle-class leaders. To understand this popular phase of the mid-nineteenth-century temperance movement, it is necessary to examine the movement in some other manner 5 One way to gain a fresh perspective on the temperance movement is to examine the election returns for elections in which ordinary Americans voters, were asked to express their views concerning alcoholic beverages.6 In 1845 the New York state legislature passed a law requiring each of the 858 townships in the state- except for New York City, which was voted an exemption in February, 1846-to hold a paper ballot election in May, 1846, for the purpose of deciding whether or not the officials of a town (or township: in New York they are the same) should issue licenses for the retail sale of strong drinks. If a majority of the voters favored iLicense,' then the town commissioners were authorized to issue permits to taverns and public houses to serve liquor. If the voters balloted for iNo License?5 then the officials were prohibited from issuing such licenses, and no liquor could be sold for consumption on the premises. The law was quite limited, for in neither case was there a prohibition of possession, manufacturing, wholesaling, or retailing of alcohol for consumption off premises. Nor was the statute entirely clear as to the particular beverages being prohibited for retail sale. It was only after the eleceions that the New York state attorney general, John Van Buren, ruled that the law barred ale, beer, and alcoholic hard cider as well as wine and distilled spirits.7 Although few anti-liquor leaders were fully satisfied with the 1845 laws

What these leaders found particularly galling about liquor licenses was the implication that society and the government, through licenses, gave tacit approval to alcohol. Few temperance leaders were so naive as to believe that the end of licensing would mean the end of liquor sales; rather, they wanted to drive the sellers underground and destroy their legitimacy within the community.8 Despite the prohibition law's murkiness and limitations, and despite the fact that some temperance men opposed prohibition because it substituted legal coercion for moral suasion, the New York license elections of 1846 are as good a gauge of the public's opinion about alcohol in the mid-nineteenth-century United States as we are likely to obtain. New York was a populous, significant, and diverse state, as near a microcosm of the entire nation as a single state could be, and it was the only highly populated state during the 1840s to hold license elections at one time throughout the state. Furthermore, these particular elections were held in each town both independently and simultaneously. That isl unlike a statewide election, such as Pennsylvania's liquor referendum of 1854, the outcome was determined at the local, township level. And since the elections were held in all towns at the same time, they offer a good measure of public opinion operating upon all localities under the same general influences. Townships are also convenient units for examination. They are not arbitrary clumps of individuals brought together for the purpose of a study; rathert towns in New York were masses of people living, workingl and praying together. There was, as so many historians have recently reminded us a sense ot community and a resultant community sense.9 Further, historians who have been studying the temperance movement at the local level thus far have written about urban localities.l° The examination of mostly rural New York townships will give us a broader perspective. Then, too, in 1845 the state of New York took a detailed census of population, agriculturel and manufacturing. The rich social and economic data from this census can be compared with the data fronl the 1846 license elections. Finally, the license returns can be compared to partisan political returns that are available for townships. The campaign surrounding the license elections was rather dull. Ten days before the vote the Poughkeepsie Journal & Eagle commentedt "At present we see very little said on the subject throughout the state by the papers, many scarcely allude to it, and among the people themselves there appears to be no excitement." There were many reasons for the lack of 'ipopular excitement.'nll For one thing, because the license issue had been around for several years, all the arguments had been heard before. Then, too, in many parts of the state it had become common for local officials to run for office pledged not to grant licenses. As early as 1842 the Ulster Republican reported that temperance had i'exercised a controlling influence upon the electionl' in the village of Kingston, New York. That village's board of directors met in a temperance hotel (where no liquor was served), refused to license any retail groceriesb and only grudgingly licensed five taverns for the convenience of travellers. In Penn-Yan villagen just a month before the 1846 license elections, the Yates County Whig reported that a village government pledged to prohibition had been elected without opposition. 12 The lackluster campaign came to an end amid a torrent of other events.
