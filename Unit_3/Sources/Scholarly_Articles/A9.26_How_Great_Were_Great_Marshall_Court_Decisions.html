Madison,5 McCulloch v. Maryland,6 and Dartmouth College v. Woodward7-were of enor- mous significance to the history of the early republic. A closer look at these rulings in their historical context, however, suggests that such assumptions are in need of serious revision. While I do not mean to suggest that these famous Marshall Court decisions were completely inconsequential, the prevalent assumption that they fundamentally shaped the course of American national develop- ment is almost certainly wrong. This Article will reconsider the consequences of three categories of Marshall Court decisions. Part I will examine the most famous Marshall opinion of all, Marbury v. Madison, and will question the importance of its proclamation of the judicial review power. Part II will reevaluate the importance of McCulloch v. Maryland and Gib- bons v. Ogden8-decisions that approved extremely broad conceptions of national legislative power. Part III will turn to some of the famous Contract Clause decisions of the Marshall Court- specifically, Dartmouth College v. Woodward, Sturges v. Crownin- shield,9 and Green v.
