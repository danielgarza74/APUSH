Damnifying Growth in the Antebellum South THOMAS F. HUERTAS The South's failure to industrialize more rapidly in the antebellum period did not lead to its immiserization. The South's terms of trade improved over the antebellum period so that its consumption potential expanded more rapidly than did produc- tion. Specialization in cotton production promoted a favorable movement of rela- tive prices facing Southern producers. A customs union analysis shows that the South might have industrialized to a greater extent and might have raised its con- sumption potential further had it been able to impose an independent tariff on im- ports from all areas, including the rest of the United States. THE analysis of antebellum U.S. economic growth and development owes much to Douglass North's model of regional specialization and export-led growth based on a simple and traditional division of the nation into three regions.' North emphasized the positive benefits for the nation that emanated from regional specialization, but both he and a number of other scholars have stressed the adverse consequences for the South of its concentration on the production of export staples, particularly cotton. This paper ex- amines in some detail one of the principal distortions: the difference be- tween domestic and international relative prices due to the antebellum tariff. It distinguishes between the effects of southern relative prices on the structure of production in the South and the welfare effects of changes in international relative prices. In so doing, it links the debate concerning the failure of the South to industrialize with the discussion of the effects of the antebellum tariff. In conclusion it offers some perspectives on the relation- ship between relative prices and economic growth. Although the South is frequently described as the Cotton Economy, the extent and development of its specialization on export staples is still not completely known. Census data have furnished scholars with the raw ma- terial for decadal snapshots of the southern economy, and the nation's foreign trade statistics have helped fill in the void between the census years. But the overall picture of southern trade and production remains incomplete. One of the most important missing elements has been data on interregional trade flows. Some estimates for selected years exist, but they provide neither a continuous series nor information concerning the real value of trade. Table I begins to fill in this gap.
