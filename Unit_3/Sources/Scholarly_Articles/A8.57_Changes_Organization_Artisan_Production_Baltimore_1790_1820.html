Reverse the picture," declared the journeymen tailors, "what a melancholy contrast view a journeyman, in the prime of youth, through intense application to a sedantary [sic] employment, almost deprived of eye sight, his constitution impaired, and his circumstances precisely as they were years before.''1 The address of Baltimore's journeymen tailors illuminates the ordeal of journeymen clothiers in the major urban centers of the new American republic. Beginning in I794 with the creation of the Federal Society of Journeymen Cordwainers of Philadelphia, journeymen shoemakers, tailors, and printers organized societies to protect their status as highly skilled laborers. These associations utilized a variety of measures to compel master artisans to come to terms with their employees. They presented their case to the public in the newspapers and in broadsides, hoping that an upsurge of community indignation would make the employers more pliable. They established separate journeymen's workshops that competed with the mas- ters' shops. They attempted to organize general boycotts of employers who violated the provisions of journeymen constitutions. I02 WILLIAM AND MARY QUARTERLY called for strikes in which all members of a particular craft would partici- pate.' This outburst of organizational activity has attracted the attention of labor historians, but few scholars have examined the structural changes in artisan production that generated the unrest.3 Indeed, the best analysis of the handicraft system in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries remains that of John R. Commons, published nearly seventy years ago. Concentrating on shoemakers because they were the first craftsmen to break from the traditional handicraft system and because they organized the earli- est and most militant journeymen associations, Commons attempted to con- struct a general model of artisan production prior to the emergence of factory system. The shoemaking craft, according to Commons, passed through three stages of development before the Industrial Revolution; these stages corre- sponded with the size of the market for manufactured goods. During most of the colonial period the master shoemaker produced for a "custom-order market"; beginning in the Revolutionary years, he also sold his goods in a 'retail market''; by the early national era he began to operate in a "whole- sale market." With each successive phase, the master shoemaker's social identity shifted from the producer to the merchant class. By the early nineteenth century he had been transformed into a "wholesale merchant- employer," an acquisitive capitalist who expanded the productive capacity of his workshop by hiring more employees and who turned a profit by limiting his workers' wages. At the same time, the journeymen cordwainers, who could no longer reasonably aspire to master status, fell into the ranks of permanent wage workers. It was Commons's contention that the origins of modern industrial classes reached back to the early national period, several decades before the appearance of the factory system.' While Commons provided a useful model for understanding alterations in the handicraft system, he was chiefly concerned with the rise of trade unionism and consequently offered only general impressions of the evolving structure of the artisan shop and the composition of the work force.
