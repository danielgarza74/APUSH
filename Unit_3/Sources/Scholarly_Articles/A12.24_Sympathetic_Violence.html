Sympathetic Violence: Maria Stewart's Antebellum Vision of African American Resistance Christina Henderson University of Connecticut Between January 7, 1832 and May 4, 1833, William Lloyd Garrison's newspaper, The Liberator, published six articles by abolitionist and black nationalist Maria W. Stewart.1 In these articles, Stewart spoke in two seemingly contradictory registers as she described God's interactions with humanity. On the one hand, she portrayed a gentle God who directed his angels to carry oppressed individuals "into Abraham's bosom [where] they shall be comforted" ("Address: Delivered before" 66); on the other hand, she warned sinners—specifically white American sinners—of a wrathful and violent God who was on the verge of sending "horror and devastation" to the world ("Address, Delivered in"). While these two images may seem paradoxical to contemporary readers, they reflect the connection between sympathy and violence that permeated Stewart's theology and structured her concept of Christian community. She believed God's compassion for suffering believers would motivate him to punish their tormenters and that African American Christians should follow his example by protecting one another with force if necessary. This juxtaposition of Christian mercy and retributive violence also points to the crucial but often minimized role of African American women such as Stewart who were uniquely situated to collaborate with black nationalists and white abolition ists. As an important figure in radical political action, Stewart helps us to better understand the multivalent forces that shaped resistance movements in the early nineteenth century. Stewart, a free African American widow who lived in Boston during the 1830s, was one of the first American women to lecture before mixed race and mixed-gender audiences (Richardson, Preface xiii-xiv).2 She passionately and publicly advocated for abolition and racial equality, and many of her speeches were printed in The Liberator. Sympathetic Violence Mott and have underscored feminine appeals to sympathy and nonviolent forms of protest.4 But Stewart—who considered Walker a mentor and attended meetings with Mott and other women abolitionists—fits neither model, at times causing her to be overlooked in scholarship.5 Both black nationalism and women's abolition have received considerable critical attention, but they largely have been regarded as distinct movements marked by conflicting philosophies and strategies. Few recent studies have examined moments of collaboration between these two nineteenth-century groups or even considered the possibility that a link exists between them. Stewart's writing, however, makes this link visible. Urging readers to cultivate both Christian love and violent resistance against slavery, Stewart stands with a foot in each camp and draws on the traditions of both. This essay argues that Stewart's articles reflect her intellectual collaboration with black nationalists and white pacifist abolitionists, offering contemporary scholars important insight into the ways these larger movements inflected one another. Recent critics have added Stewart to the traditionally male-dominated list of antebellum black nationalists by concentrating on how she adapts African American rhetorical traditions.6 Most scholarship, though, minimizes her collaboration with white female activists. Lena Ampadu and Ebony A. Utley, for instance, focus on Stewart's contributions to black nationalist and black jeremiad traditions.7 Evidence suggests, however, that Stewart was very much a part of the women's anti-slavery movement; for example, she attended the Women's Anti-Slavery Convention in 1837, which Mott chaired (Richardson, Introduction 27; Faulkner 72). Stewart's speeches reflect her inten tion to engage with this audience; in one address, she explicitly speaks to her "fairer sisters" ("Lecture"). Stewart's addresses point to her interactions and intellectual exchanges with these various voices within abolitionist and black nationalist groups. Martha S. Jones has argued that Stewart's pamphlet Religion and the Pure Principles of Morality, the Sure Foundation on which We Must Build (1831) "was a collaboration born out of radical abolitionism's early cross-racial alliances" among Stewart, Garrison, and Walker (24). This assertion can be extended to Stewart's articles in The Liberator, which evince her associations with Walker as well as with women abolitionists such as Mott and the writers whose columns appeared alongside Stewart's in the "Ladies' Department" of Garrison's paper. Critics such as Jennifer Rycenga, Cedrick May, Willie J. Harrell, Jr., and Joycelyn Moody have noted that Stewart emphasizes God's wrath and—like Walker— incorporates the black jeremiad tradition into her speeches by condemning sin and prophesying retribution for slavery and prejudice.
