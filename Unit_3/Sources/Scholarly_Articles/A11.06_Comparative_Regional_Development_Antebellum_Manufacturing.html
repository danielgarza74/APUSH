Comparative Regional Development in Antebellum Manufacturing A reconsideration of regional economic development during the antebellum period is underway. Emphasis thus far has tended to center on economic aggregates, on one region (the South) or on a single sector (agriculture). While the results of this work have altered our understanding of the antebellum South's relative eco- nomic position-or at least have sharpened the debate on the issues -comprehension of the role and position of the period's industrial sector remains faulty, largely because it has been derived from in- direct evidence. Our analysis of the nature of southern manufacturing activity has demanded knowledge of industrial development elsewhere, partic- ularly when dealing with questions of why the South evolved as it did. Such questions involving alternate paths of development can be analyzed fruitfully in a comparative context. Discussion of such issues as slavery and comparative advantage that underlies much of the existing body of thought regarding southern industrial levels frequently becomes entangled in debates regarding the reasons why southern manufacturing developed as it did, and what might have happened if certain, presumably retardant, conditions had been absent. Comparison of southern industrial development with that of the other major agricultural but not slave-based economy, the antebellum West, and comparison of both South and West with the major nonagricultural economy in the East do provide answers based on empirical evidence rather than on hypothetical constructs alone. Comparative Regional Development 183 held the Northeast to be the developing industrial area, the West the food-supplying region, and the South primarily an agricultural region supplying raw materials for industrial use outside the area. Reinforcing this was a frequent tendency to blend the East and West into- an "industrial North," where the economy differed dra- matically from the non-industrialized South. It was thus tempting to generalize this "laggard" industrial condition into southern eco- nomic "backwardness." Only recently has this perception come under serious challenge in studies suggesting that economic development and welfare in the South had not suffered in comparison to the rest of the United States, particularly to the West. The southern econ- omy in the aggregate appears to have been prosperous despite its industrial condition. While the traditional generalizations regarding overall economic conditions among the regions seem obviously suspect given recent empirical evidence, were those regarding the manufacturing sectors specifically also off the mark? Examination of several indicators of regional industrial development (Table 1) reveals not large differ- ences between the West and South, but rather similarity of the two regions when they are compared to the eastern states. The amounts of capital and labor per firm in the western and southern states are similar, but far below that of either New England or the Middle States. Even output per firm in the West was considerably closer to that of the South than of these eastern areas. By any measure New England clearly was the most advanced industrial region, with the Middle Atlantic region a somewhat dis- tant second. From this perspective, both the western and the south- ern industrial sectors were "laggard." But is that an appropriate perspective? The West, a developed and richly-endowed agricul- tural region, was already establishing a pervasive and diversified manufacturing sector, one that after the Civil War would blossom into one of the world's major industrial regions. And the South's industrial condition, when set in contrast to this other primarily agricultural region, seems neither so backward nor so underdevel- oped. What appears are three different regions-each responding to existing factor endowments and specializing according to these dif- ferences in input availability.
