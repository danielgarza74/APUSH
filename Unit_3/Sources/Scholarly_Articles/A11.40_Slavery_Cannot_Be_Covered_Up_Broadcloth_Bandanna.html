PIERSON Beginning in the early 1830s, white abolitionists attempted to prove that American slaves suffered physically, emotionally, and spiritu- ally at the hands of those who claimed ownership over them. They felt that they needed to convince the American public that slaves were badly treated. Time and again they argued the basic facts of southern planta- tion life: slaveholders divided families, legalized rape, and did not recog- nize slave marriages as legitimate.' Masters beat slaves, denied them food and rest, and kept them from schools and churches. The most famous works of American abolitionist literature argue these ideas with passion, Michael D. Elizabeth "Lizzie" Jones's speech at the 1860 anniversary meeting of the American Anti-Slavery Society indicates a new trend in abolitionist rhetoric. Rather than rehash how poorly slaves were treated, Jones proclaimed that such discus- sions-indeed the entire argument of the previous thirty years over whether slaves were ill or kindly treated by their masters-mattered not one bit. "We are told [by pro-slavery people] that the slaves are well fed and well clothed," she began. That is nothing to our argument. But they are happy! So much the worse for the master. If he has obliterated the line between the man and the brute, I should rather not stand in his place when he goes to judgment. Slavery cannot be covered up with broadcloth or a bandanna. It cannot be made respectable, not even with the cast-off clothing of the master. Gilt buttons and gold chains don't help it. Jones continued with a philosophical statement about what she saw as slavery's true crime: "The question is not what he eats, or how much money shall be spent upon him," she said, "but it is this, Shall he be recognized as a man?" According to Jones, slavery "tears away the crown of manhood, and brands the human being a brute." Her dismissal of the debate about the material conditions of slave life is dramatic and com- plete. Ultimately she argued that all the representations of slaves as "fat and sleek, contented and happy, fall to the ground. They have no bearing upon the question."2 With this claim she signaled a willingness to aban- don an established line of abolitionist attack in favor of the philosophical proposition that human beings need independence and freedom in order to retain their claim to the favored place above animals granted by God. Jones was not alone in labeling any form of dependence as unacceptable. By the late 1850s, many abolitionists attacked slavery because it enforced dependence upon slaves, not for the material conditions in which the slaves lived. This essay explores the reasons for this shift in abolitionist rhetoric, paying particular attention to how white abolitionists wrote about gender 2. Jones's speech appears in the New York National Anti-Slavery Standard, May 26, 1860.
