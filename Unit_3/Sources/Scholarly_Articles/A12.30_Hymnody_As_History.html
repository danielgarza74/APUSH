Hymnody as History: Early Evangelical Hymns and the Recovery of American Popular Religion STEPHEN MARINI The hymns of evangelical Protestantism are the most widely used spiritual texts in American history. Sacred lyrics like "All hail the power of Jesus' name," "Jesus, lover of my soul," "How firm a foundation," and "When I survey the wondrous cross" have been sung, preached, and prayed by millions of Americans since the eigh- teenth century. At worship, revivals, youth services, conferences, conventions, and colleges, and in the family circle, these hymns have been ceaselessly repeated in an unending round of living oral tradi- tion. Since the Great Awakening two and a half centuries ago, the churches of the evangelical tradition have published tens of thou- sands of hymn texts and tunes. This continuous popularity since colonial times establishes hymnody as a crucial expression of Amer- ican evangelical religiousness. Hymns have performed vital functions in evangelical religious culture. The experience of singing is central to revivalism, the char- acteristic evangelical ritual mode, and hearing or meditating upon hymns often mediates the new birth, the movement's classic form of spiritual experience. For all evangelicals, hymn-singing has been a primary vehicle of transcendence, the very wind of the Spirit itself. Hymns have also served as educational media for children, aids to prayer for adults, homiletic devices for preachers, and literary mate- rial for poets and novelists. They played a crucial role in the trans- mission of Christianity to African Americans and Native Americans. Above all, hymns have articulated the everyday beliefs and practices of generations of American evangelicals. This essay is an initial attempt to use the most widely circulated evangelical hymns of the colonial, federal, and antebellum periods as an interpretive lens through which to explore early Amer- ican popular religion. I. DEFINING POPULAR RELIGION AND AMERICAN HYMNODY What can we learn from hymn texts that amplifies our understand- ing of evangelicalism as a cultural reality in colonial, revolutionary, and antebellum America? Several recent studies have created a new discourse on American popular religion that supplies a promising approach to this question. All of them agree that a significant dimen- sion of religion exists alongside and outside the institutional and cultural control of ecclesiastical bodies and that it has played a far more important role in American religiousness than historians have generally granted. These studies of popular religion complement each other and should inform any study of hymnody. On the other hand, the content of early evangelical hymns also challenges some of their central conclusions. Peter Williams's now classic study Popular Religion in America: Symbolic Change and the Modernization Process in Historical Perspective suggests three conditions that define popular religion. It is "usually 1) found outside formal church structures, 2) transmitted outside the established channels of religious instruction and communication em- ployed by these structures, and 3) preoccupied with concrete mani- festations of the supernatural in the midst of the secular world."1 These conditions generally obtained for early American evangelical hymnody. Despite the strenuous efforts of many denominations to control their hymnody, early American evangelicals of all theological stripes persistently borrowed hymns from one another. The hymn texts and tunes they used were in this sense "found outside formal church structures," and before 1860 their hymn collections and tune books were most often provided by entrepreneurial editors like William Billings, Isaiah Thomas, Jesse Mercer, Samuel Worcester, John Wyeth, and William Walker rather than authorized denomina- tional "channels of religious instruction and communication."2 1.
