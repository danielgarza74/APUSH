ANTE-BELLUM LABOR: VIOLENCE, STRIKE, AND COMMUNAL ARBITRATION Ante-bellum United States labor history, the offspring of the concern about worker- capital relations that came to the fore at the turn of this century, corresponds to a pattern that occurs in almost all new intellectual fields where current interests encourage the development of fresh historical perspectives. The events, concerns, attitudes, and even terminology of the times not only stimulated new awareness, but also directed and limited its scope. In this sense, much American labor history has been written backwards, with the major clashes and developments around 1900 focusing what has been looked for, and found, concerning pre-Civil War American workers. The stress of John Commons and his school on unions as the core of labor history most obviously grew from early twentieth-century events, but the intense and often bloody worker struggles around 1900 have ties with more recent attempts to find and define ante-bellum worker radicalism, class consciousness, or class culture. From this perspective, ante-bellum labor history involves a record of failure or at best a pale shadowing of later developments. The central explanatory work explores labor's limitations before the Civil War: why workers failed to establish permanent unions, or why one finds only glimpses of that class consciousness or radicalism seemingly etched in the outbursts of 1877 and the drawn-out struggles of the years 1885-1915 and immediately after World War I. Even the strongest stimulant to recent American labor history, Herbert Gutman's Americanization of E.P. Thompson's classic study of labor's traumatic movement from a pre-industrial to an industrial world, has reinforced this somewhat transtemporal view of ante-bellum labor history. For the process of industrial adjustment which Thompson presents as a single transition, Gutman sees as a reality repeating itself in all major American eras.l The degree of looking backward has varied widely among labor historians. Helen Sumner, one of the four authors who wrote the history of ante-bellum labor in the Commons series, suggested positive accomplishments more than did her male co- authors or than Norman Ware who more intellectually shaped this tradition.2 Several examples of the new labor history have been concerned primarily with description of the work, mobility, ethnic, and familial patterns of laborers. Yet where schematic interpretation has been strongest and most interesting, the emphasis on halting beginnings has remained largely intact.3 The tacit assumption that ante-bellum labor history formed a somewhat shaky first stage in a straight linear development toward what happened at the end of the nineteenth century has distracted attention from some of the distinguishing features of work life in the years of pre-Civil War industrialization. This essay explores some of the peculiarities of the era's capital-labor relations by considering three central areas where prevalent early patterns differed substantially from later ones: (1) the role of violence in disputes over workplace issues; (2) the form and structure of the period's strikes; and (3) the mechanisms of communal appeal through which worker grievances or disputes were commonly adjusted. If one considers these three crucial aspects of capital- labor dealings chronologically from the industrial "take off stage in the 1830s, ante- bellum worker efforts become a somewhat successful and quite distinctive way of handling labor-management tensions and arguments. Continuities, of course, remain, as they always do in history, but the labor-capital violence, the pragmatic unionism, and the forms of worker consciousness of the later era stand less as culmination of faint or failed ante-bellum beginnings than as new struggles, strategies, and orientations arising from management's discarding an earlier form of labor-capital accommodation in the United States. The essay's structure involves first establishing the contours of violence related to specific work demands, to explore why this method of responding to or handling labor problems was so seldom employed, especially compared to the later period when the United States "had the bloodiest and most violent labor history of any nation in the world."4 Some crucial incidents ofthe mid-1830s will be examined which suggest the rough ground rules worked out, regarding labor organization and tactics, between labor, capital, courts, and community in these years. A descriptive look will follow at the common non-violent protest or pressure tactic of both craft and industrial workers before the Civil War, the strike or, as it was more commonly and accurately called, the "turn-out," which essentially involved an appeal to the community about the fairness of particular actions. Finally some suggestions will be made about the sources of this "system" of labor-capital adjustment and its connections with the labor-management violence and battle for pragmatic power around 1900, which slowly led to the type of industrial-union organizations and attitudes that have prevailed in the twentieth century. There are, of course, limitations in this general description. The ante-bellum pattern of capital-labor adjustment had little that was formal about it, although legal decisions after the mid4830s fell in line with the communally accepted principles that upheld the system. And it operated loosely, without predictable results in specific cases, much like its twentieth-century successor. Success, failure or compromise depended on a multitude of local and personal variables: specific conditions, worker options, general economic realities, and the degree of worker-manager trust and flexibility. An additional variable seemingly mattered much more in ante-bellum conflicts than in later ones: the degree of communal sympathy or support for worker demands. This discussion of broad ante-bellum labor patterns, of course, does not deny variations related to different types of employment, employers, employees and communities. Presumably distinctions might be drawn among craft, skilled factory and unskilled industrial work, as well as subtler ones based on the innumerable variations within and between each craft or type of workplace. Worker tactics may have changed somewhat with the religion, sex or ethnicity of groups as well as their skills or familial situation. Managers probably crafted responses partly in relation to the size or diversity of their operation, their faith or ancestry, their situations as owners or hired agents, and their personal ties to the locale of conflict. Community response was possibly influenced by size, diversity, region and socio-religious composition. Yet workers Catholic and Protestant, foreign and native, craft and industrial, skilled and unskilled, in homes, small shops and factories, in cities, towns and rural mill settings all took part in some general practices of protection. And clearer understanding of this general pattern is necessary for more precise eventual delineation of what caused variations in it.5 This commonality of situation is perhaps best illustrated by an example drawn from the division where broadest variation in pattern might be expected: between unskilled labor in large factories and skilled craftsmen. For the ante-bellum years the available evidence suggests that these groups followed roughly similar tactics and patterns in defending their interests, with roughly equivalent success and failure.
