LOUISA MAXWELL COCKE: AN EVANGELICAL PLANTATION MISTRESS IN THE ANTEBELLUM SOUTH Louis B. Gimelli in recent years, scholars have turned their attention to many previ- ously neglected topics dealing with the roles of women in American history. The results have been enlightening, adding new dimensions to our perceptions of traditional subjects. This is certainly true of re- cent research dealing with the antebellum South. Anne Firor Scott, in her study of the southern lady, has made us aware of both the myth and the reality of the lives of upper- and middle-class antebellum southern white women. Catherine Clinton has presented us with a general study of the plantation mistress that contains absorbing ac- counts of the subordination of the mistress to the master, the tedium of her work routine, and the infirmities she endured. Both works pro- vide us with a better grasp of slavery in terms of its impact on the interpersonal relations among white women, masters, black men, and black women.1 While much has been accomplished, more remains to be done. Neither of the above mentioned works deals directly with the interac- tion between evangelical white women and slavery. These and other subjects pertaining to women, slavery, and evangelical religion beg to be confronted. Some will be addressed in this essay, which deals with one evangelical woman: Louisa Maxwell Cocke.2 Louisa and John Hartwell Cocke were mistress and master of Bremo plantation in Fluvanna County, Virginia, during the antebel- lum years.3 At a time when slave codes were becoming more rigid and many southerners were defending slavery on racial, religious, and sociological grounds, the Cockes were deploring its existence, re- garding slaveholding as a sin, and criticizing their fellow slaveholders' defense of the institution.4 They worked to ameliorate the lot of their slaves by providing for their education, offering them religious in- struction, urging them to abstain from using alcohol, and allowing a few to settle in Liberia under the auspices of the American Coloniza- tion Society.5 Divorced from their associations with Evangelical Prot- 2 Jean E.
