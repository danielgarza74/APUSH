The Monroe Doctrine: Domestic Politics or National Decision? HARRY AMMON With the publication in 1949 of Samuel Flagg Bemis's John Quincy Adams and the Foundations of American Foreign Policy it seemed that any possible controversy among historians over the origins of the Monroe Doctrine had been resolved.1 As the first modern scholar with access to the Adams family papers, Bemis confirmed the conclusions reached by earlier scholars, notably Dexter Perkins and Arthur P. Whitaker.2 Except for the noncolonization principle, clearly Adams's contribution, Bemis regarded the principles in President James Monroe's December 1823 message as a joint work of the president and the secretary of state. He saw the doctrine as the result of a great national decision. Domestic considerations, he believed, were relevant to the cabinet discussions of November 1823. However, probing extensively into the domestic aspects of the policy decision, he saw no reason to alter the conclusion reached by Perkins that the rejection of the British proposal of a joint policy statement was based on concepts of national interest and foreign policy objectives shared by Monroe and his advisers.3 Bemis and other scholars have noted that Monroe and his secretaries were unwilling, to use John Quincy Adams's words, to appear as a "cockboat in the wake of the British man-of-war." They knew that the American people might not appreciate such close cooperation with the nation's recent enemy. Bemis and his scholarly predecessors considered these circumstances not as determinants but merely as factors relevant to the decision-making process.4 This view of the Monroe Doctrine as a national decision, seemingly so firmly established, recently has been challenged in Ernest R. May's The Making of the Monroe Doctrine,5 May does not question the conclusions of earlier scholarship about the authorship of the Monroe Doctrine; his concern is with the motives underlying the decision to reject the British proposal for a joint policy statement. He contends that the reaction of the president and his cabinet was shaped by the domestic political interests of each participant. As he states in his introduction, "the positions of the policy makers were determined less by conviction than by ambition. They had different stakes riding on the outcomes and Adams had a greater stake than others." According to May the key for understanding why the foreign policy debates came out as they did lies in the struggle for the presidency, for the Monroe Doctrine was "actually the by-product of an election camnaien."6 Scholars writing in the late nineteenth century were preoccupied with issues arising from contemporary policies. At the turn of the century in the age of Big Stick Diplomacy, questions were asked regarding the intent of the doctrine, and the answers frequently reinforced existing national policies. In recent decades historians have become increasingly aware of the manipulation of foreign policy in the interest of political ambition, notably on the presidential level; and in the light of modern political behavior it is not surprising that scholars should raise questions about similar issues in an earlier age. However, historians prior to May have weighed this issue. Their failure to see domestic politics as the origin of the doctrine cannot be attributed to a reluctance to face up to such a possibility, for historians have long recognized the role personal rivalries played in the foreign policy of the Washington administration. 1 ne reason tnat previous students or tne doctrine nave not adopted the theme of domestic politics for the Monroe Doctrine is that the direct evidence suggesting that personal ambition was a determinant is lacking.
