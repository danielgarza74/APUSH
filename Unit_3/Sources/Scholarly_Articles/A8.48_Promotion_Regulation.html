Selection Overview

Pisani argues that in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries the Constitution drove economic growth by accepting the private market while expecting government to promote enterprise. Looking beyond the document's explicit powers, he stresses an "unwritten constitution" of common law, statutes, and state constitutions that balanced individual opportunity against the common good, creating wealth as well as protecting it.

Promotion and Regulation: Constitutionalism and the American Economy Donald J. Pisani The United States Constitution is much more than the formal document ratified in 1789. It is the preeminent symbol of the American preference for continuity over radical change, a collection of myths that provides a common faith, and a compli- cated dialogue between written and unwritten rules of law. As such it reflects and embodies many of the conflicting values at the core of American culture. The law itself, as an extended commentary on the Constitution, has perpetuated many of those conflicts by seeking to balance essentially irreconciliable objectives. It has always attempted, with mixed success, to reconcile elitism and democracy, in- dividual opportunity and community, enterprise and equity, growth and stability, competition and cooperation, and freedom and responsibility. In the late eigh- teenth and early nineteenth centuries, the Constitution served as a powerful engine of economic growth. The nation's greatest perceived need was to encourage en- trepreneurs to pool and to invest capital. The Constitution accepted the private market as the chief instrument of economic growth, and it recognized the concomi- tant responsibility that government do everything possible to promote that growth. But by the end of the nineteenth century, big business, not the grasping central government so deeply feared in the period from 1789 to 1860, became the dominant concern of reform-minded Americans. Most of the special privileges granted to busi- nesses before the Civil War no longer made sense. The promotional impulse did not disappear, but regulation became a more visible expression of constitution- alism. The federal and state governments were too weak to regulate the economy effectively until the 1930s. Ironically, when government did become strong enough, Americans lost faith that it could or should reorder the economic system. The Con- stitution's influence on economic development has ranged from its tendency to en- courage economic decentralization to its preference for judicial and quasi-judicial institutions as arbiters of economic conflicts, to its assumption that law must em- body "higher" values than majority rule or than "the greatest good for the greatest number." Overall, the Constitution has played an enormous, though often indirect, part in the development of state and local, as well as federal, economic legislation. Donald J. Pisani is associate professor of history at Texas A&M University. 740

Constitutionalism and the Economy 741 This essay is more concerned with constitutionalism's influence on economic de- velopment than with the formal economic powers and responsibilities enumerated in the Constitution.1 Granted those explicit powers are vast. They include the power to define the national economic interest in relations with other nations, to regulate interstate trade, to tax and to spend, to create a reliable money supply, to secure copyright and patent rights, to enact uniform bankruptcy statutes, to grant cor- porate chatters, to dispose of the public lands, and to protect the sanctity of private contracts. Nevertheless, the Constitution is as important for the implicit as for the explicit economic powers it vests in government. Among the many definitions of constitutionalism, the most common emphasizes a philosophy of government built on the principles of limited government, restraint, and the accountability of rulers to the ruled. That definition squares with the thesis -popularized by the famous constitutional historian Edward S. Corwin - that the law's basic purpose is to protect vested rights.2 There is much truth to that thesis, but it obscures the extent to which the law provides rules and referees to supervise the acquisition of new property as well as to protect established rights, that is, to create wealth as well as to defend it. Cer- tainly, our "unwritten constitution" including common law traditions, statutes enacted by Congress and by state legislatures, and the state constitutions themselves (which, in effect, complete the federal charter) -reflects the deep conviction that those who hold power have a duty to allocate scarce resources in a way that balances a maximum of individual opportunity against some expression of the common good. The essence of constitutionalism, then, is an attempt to order essentially in- consistent values, an attempt to balance differences rather than to eliminate them entirely. The tension between promotional and regulative objectives has profoundly influenced the relationship of government to the economy. The Constitution's Framers could not anticipate the dramatic economic changes that occurred in the United States during the first half of the nineteenth century. After all, they drafted a document to serve an economy in which most farmers prac- ticed subsistence agriculture and in which factories were rare, transportation limited, banking and credit primitive, and business transactions simple and direct.
