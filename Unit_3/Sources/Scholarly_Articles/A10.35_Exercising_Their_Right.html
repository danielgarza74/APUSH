Exercising Their Right: African American Voter Turnout in Antebellum Newport, Rhode Island RICHARD C. ROHRS DURING the first half of the nineteenth century, just as the franchise was being extended to more and more white males in the United States, it was being withdrawn from African Americans, even in the North. In four northern states— New Jersey (1807), Connecticut (1818), Rhode Island (1822), and Pennsylvania (1838)—the right to vote was taken away outright. In 1821, the New York state legislature established a more stringent property requirement for African Americans than for whites.1 As late as i860, only Maine, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, Rhode Island, and Vermont allowed African American males to vote without restriction beyond the qualifi cations required of all voters. In three other states—Michigan, New York, and Ohio—African Americans could vote in some types of elections or if they met certain conditions.2 Leon Litwack estimates that only about 6 percent of the free black population of the northern states was admitted at the polls during the antebellum period.3 It is not surprising, then, as 'James Oliver Horton and Lois E. EXERCISING THEIR RIGHT 403 one historian recently lamented, that because of the scarcity of sources, "northern black voting . . . remains a marginalized topic."4 One important source, however, is extant, and it yields valu able insights into African American voting behavior in one New England town.5 At the mid-nineteenth-century town meetings of Newport, Rhode Island, occasions for conducting the town's business, residents also voted annually for state officers, bien nially to elect a member of the United States House of Rep resentatives, and every four years to elect a president. During the 1840s, town meeting minutes often recorded the names of those men, black and white, who voted, although not for whom they voted.6 When analyzed in conjunction with the 1850 census and other sources, these voter lists yield suf ficient information to determine certain patterns in African American voting and to compare them to those of white voters.
