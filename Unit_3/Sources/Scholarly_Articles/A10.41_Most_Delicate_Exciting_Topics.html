"THE MOST DELICATE AND EXCITING TOPICS": MARTIN VAN BUREN, SLAVERY, AND THE ELECTION OF 1836 William G. Shade The presidential election of 1836 has not drawn a great deal of scholarly interest. For some historians of the early republic Martin Van Buren's election signaled the end of the era of Jacksonian Democracy. Consequently, textbook writers and those students of American political parties who deal with the longue duree barely acknowledge its existence, while specialized studies of the Jacksonian era routinely ignore its importance. Only scholars studying party development during these years have suggested the significance of 1836 as a transitional election between phases of the second party system.' Political scientist Gerald Pomper categorized 1836 as a "converting" election-that is, one "in which the majority party retains its position, but there is considerable change in its voter base."2 As such, it bore characteristics of both tradition and modernity.
