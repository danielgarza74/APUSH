The Reorientation of American Protestantism, 1835-1845 JAMES D. BRATT In March 1835 Charles Finney told a gathering in New York City: "If the church will do all her duty, the millennium may come in this country in three years."' This statement has often served as an epi- gram for the era, the motto of that movement for revivalism and social reform that, having already swept the churches, was to so infuse the culture with its moral imperatives as to make a Civil War against slavery inevitable and the hegemony of evangelical Protestantism secure. On this reading Finney's declaration marks the midpoint in a story of triumph-triumph for revival religion, and triumph for a nation that aspired to righteousness.2 In Finney's own career, however, the statement turned out to be more of a swan song. He did not settle in to fight for the nation's urban future but soon left New York for a pure Yankee enclave in the Ohio countryside. With that he also pulled back from his career as revivalist to join, of all things, the theological professoriate he had long derided. The three years he scheduled for the millennium instead brought the evangelical cause trial and disaster-personal, financial, organiza- tional, ideological. REORIENTATION OF AMERICAN PROTESTANTISM 53 evangelicalism's logic than as the launching ground of new depar- tures. More broadly yet, it divides two centuries of American Protes- tant history. The premises that had prevailed since the rise of Edwards, Wesley, and Whitefield in the 1730s were running out; the new formulas about to be devised would last through the Great Depression. Tellingly, Finney was lecturing that March night on why revivals fail.3 Setting dates and "turning points" might seem the most outmoded of historical labors, yet the chronological markers in any narrative are key indicators of its logic and purpose. The standard texts of American religious history, for instance, still divide their parts according to the landmarks of nation building and political development: Revolution to Civil War, Civil War to World War I, World War I to World War II. The pattern is bald in Winthrop Hudson's account, more nuanced in Sydney Ahlstrom's; it is evident in Robert Handy's textbook, even though he incorporates Canada as well as the United States; and it persists, with variations, in more recent offerings by Martin Marty, George Marsden, and (incorporating Canada again) Mark Noll.4 The logic of nation building has commonly fit Finney's work into a cycle of "great awakenings" which in turn serves as the plot line of American Protestant history and dictates the particular interpretation each part of the cycle receives. Thus, the First Great Awakening helped, and ultimately was important because it helped, prepare the way for Independence. The Second Great Awakening mattered because it both invigorated and stabilized the early republic, then redeemed the nation by launching crusades to extend liberty to all God's children. Under such expectations Finney's momentum had to carry into the Civil War, and the 1850s had to be "the high tide of humanitarian reform."5 Dissonance and reformulation already twenty years before could not register. 3.
