WOMAN'S PIETY WITHIN PATRIARCHY The Religious Life of Martha Hancock Wheat of Bedford County by Cynthia A. Kierner* Martha Hancock Wheat was one of many antebellum women who heeded the call of evangelical preachers and found in their own religious faith a source of autonomy and power. Nineteenth-century Americans esteemed piety as a mark of true womanhood, and, like many of her contemporaries, Wheat exploited conventional ideas about woman's religious devotion to assert her own moral authority both at home and within the larger community.1 Unlike their urban counterparts, however, rural women like Martha Wheat pursued their Christian agendas as individuals, without the organizational and moral support provided by women's associations and collective activities. Dispersed settlement inhibited the development of an autonomous women's culture in the rural South, though the extent to which men controlled women's lives is a matter of scholarly controversy. Although patriar- chal values also recognized a gender-based division of labor, patriarchy, unlike the northern ideology of separate spheres, gave men absolute authority. According to this interpretation of antebellum society and culture, southern men enjoyed dominion both inside and outside their households. Thus, patriarchy idealized women as utterly subservient, powerless, and devoid of personal authority.3 Although some antebellum southerners clearly embraced this patri- archal ideal, for many southern women?especially for those outside the planter elite?that ideal did not dictate social reality. Scholars have shown that some southern women enjoyed authority in their domestic sphere and that domestic relations in these families resembled those of their northern contemporaries.4 In the urban South, women founded benevolent and reform associations similar to those established by women in northern cities.
