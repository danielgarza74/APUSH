The Nullification Crisis and Republican Subversion By RICHARD B. LATNER HISTORIANS ARE NOW FULLY AWARE OF THE ENORMOUS SIGNIFICANCE of the nullification controversy. South Carolina's protest against the tariffs of 1828 and 1832 was only a surface manifestation of pro- found planter fears, real and imaginary, that a hostile northern majority would subvert their slave system. The crisis laid bare southern anxieties about maintaining slavery and evidenced a de- termination to devise barriers against encroachments on southern rights.' The Jackson administration's response to nullification has not received similar attention. The standard view holds that Andrew Jackson successfully dealt with the nullifiers by adopting a carrot- and-stick strategy. Alternately offering the rebels soothing words and tariff reform and threats of military force and summary execu- tion, he brilliantly outmaneuvered his foes and compelled them to stand down. But a further inquiry into nullification from the perspective of the White House indicates a more complex story. Inspired by the image of nullification as a conspiracy of ambitious demagogues, Jackson distorted the nature of the conflict he con- fronted. Determined to save the republic, he devised a policy that successfully isolated and overawed South Carolina but strained the loyalties of his party's southern wing. An assessment of Jackson's performance, then, defines more precisely the nature of this mo- mentous event and provides additional insight into the structure and ideology of the early Democratic party. Andrew Jackson's contempt for nullification is well known. "This abominable doctrine . .. strikes at the root of our Government and the social compact, and reduces every thing to anarchy . . . ," he contended. Although he considered himself an adherent of Jefferso- nianism, he distinguished between an advocate of states' rights and a nullifier. "One will preserve the union of the states," he ex- plained. "The other will dissolve the Union by destroying the Constitution by acts unauthorized by it." He often likened the Union under nullifier rule to a bag of sand with both ends open, observing that "the least pressure and it runs out at both ends.'" Jackson publicly broadcast his opposition to nullification in the administration's newspaper, the Washington Globe. Its directors, Amos Kendall and Francis Preston Blair, shared his principles and served as key members of his Kitchen Cabinet.4 Indeed, the Globe had been established in part to counter the radical antitariff lean- ings of Duff Green's United States Telegraph. Shortly after its birth, the newspaper accused John C. Calhoun of driving "the people of South Carolina, and of the whole South, into a league of revolt," and when the nullifiers persisted in their activities, it stepped up the attack, signaling Jackson's identification of nullification with "CIVIL WAR AND DISUNION" and proclaiming his intention to "PRESERVE THE UNION AT EVERY HAZARD."5 Jackson's aversion to nullification owed something to his dispute with Calhoun, but his opposition by no means hinged on a conflict of personalities.
