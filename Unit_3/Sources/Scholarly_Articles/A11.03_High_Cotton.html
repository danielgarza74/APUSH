High Cotton: The Antebellum Alabama Plantation Mistress and the Cotton Culture LEAH RAWLS ATKINS Traveling on the Herald from Montgomery to Mobile in 1828, Captain Basil Hall found his ears "wearied with the sound of Cotton! Cotton! Cotton!" At each landing the river steamer took on more cotton and new passengers, whose first question was always "What's cotton at?" Hall was so tired of all the cotton talk that he "wished all the cotton in the country was at the bottom of the Alabama [River]/'1 When a British actor took the steamboat Carolina from Montgomery to Mobile in 1835, he complained that the side galleries were filled with bales of cotton, the windows were blocked up, and the "Carolina became, in fact, a floating mass of cotton."2 Other travelers noted that the "whole soul" ofthe Alabama planter was "wrapped up in the pursuit" of growing more cotton and that he devoted "every rod of his land to cotton- growing."3 In 1858 a British visitor to Mobile described it as a city "where the people live in cotton houses and ride in cotton carriages. They buy cotton, sell cotton, think cotton, eat cotton, drink cotton, and dream cot? ton. They marry cotton wives, and unto them are born cotton children. In enumerating the charms of a fair widow, they begin by saying she makes so many bales of cotton. It is the great staple-the sum and substance of Alabama."4 LEAH RAWLS ATKINS is Director of the Center for the Arts and Humanities, Auburn Uni? versity. 1. 93 Plantation Mistress "Cotton wives" played a significant role in the cotton kingdom of Al? abama, but the reality of their lives was hidden under a canopy of ro? mance. The mythology of the southern belle, whose waist was cinched tight by laced corset, whose complexion was as white as moonlight on magnolia blossoms, and who fainted at the least crisis, began as the Romantic movement swept Europe. "Beautiful, graceful, accomplished in social charm, bewitching in coquetry, yet strangely steadfast in soul," she remains, Francis Pendleton Gaines has written, "perhaps the most win- some figure in the whole field of our fancy."5 Sir Walter Scott's tales of medieval aristocracy influenced the South. While the rest of the nation only "read Scott with enthusiasm, the South assimilated his works into its very being," and soon the Old South came to regard itself "as the epitome of the chivalric ideal," including its cult of womanhood.6 John Pendleton Kennedy's novel Swallow Barn appeared in 1832 and marked the beginning of a glamorous treatment in American fiction of southern rural life. Kennedy painted with a Romantic palette a pictur- esque Virginia plantation and introduced Prudence Meriwether, a "high authority in matter of dress," with an "exaggerated thoughtlessness," and "sometimes, in her tone of conversation, a little too much girlish- ness." Bel Tracy is the spirited neighbor girl with "too rigorous [an] es? timate of the proprieties and privileges of her sex." The stage was set for the plantation myth and the mystique of the southern belle.7 Such mythology received a boost at the end of the century when rev- erence for the South was entwined with the "Lost Cause" and the image ofthe "grand and beautiful women" of the South standing behind their men in gray.8 In 1896 an Alabama division ofthe United Daughters ofthe Confederacy (UDC) was organized, and here as across the nation, the UDC perpetuated a romantic image of the Old South and of southern womanhood.9 In the fictional and literary representations of plantation life the mis? tress has often been a stock character, a reliable stereotype.
