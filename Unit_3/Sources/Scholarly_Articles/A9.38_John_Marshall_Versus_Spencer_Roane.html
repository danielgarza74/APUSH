Selection Overview

Miller argues that Martin v. Hunter's Lessee, often seen as a nationalist victory for the Marshall Court, had mixed results. Arising from disputes over Virginia's Fairfax lands, the litigation let Spencer Roane's Court of Appeals reject the Supreme Court's appellate authority and showed how federal rulings could be nullified through weak local enforcement, feeding a states' rights movement Marshall saw as revived antifederalism.

JOHN MARSHALL VERSUS SPENCER ROANE A R??valuation of Martin v. Hunter's Lessee by F. Thornton Miller* The federalists won in 1787-88, but the antifederalists' vision of the country was more fully realized because, for much of the nineteenth century, America remained more a mixture of regions and states than a nation. Even the proper way to interpret the great federalist creation, the Constitution, heralded as the bond of the nation, was open to debate, and there was no consensus that the Supreme Court had the final word. Despite federalist warnings about the need for uniformity, an opposition emerged in different places, at different times, that would ignore, check, undermine, and nullify the federal law of the land. A states' rights movement developed by the 1820s?led in Virginia by Spencer Roane, of the Court of Appeals?that John Marshall believed was nothing more than antifederalism revived. By the Jacksonian period it appeared that the nationalism of the Marshall Court was defeated. The failure of the Court to establish a uniformity in the law was evident much earlier, however, at the dawn of the high Marshall Court era. On closer examination even the apparent nationalist victory of Martin v. Hunters Lessee, a case arising from a dispute over the title to the Fairfax lands in Virginia, had mixed results. This case not only demonstrated the ability of Virginia's Court of Appeals to reject the appellate authority of the Supreme Court but also gave another example of how a legal principle established by the highest court could be thwarted and made practically null and void through lack of enforcement at the lowest level. This circumvention was troublesome to Marshall because he was personally involved in the litigation that climaxed in, but continued long after, Martin v. Hunter's Lessee. The antagonism that developed between him and Roane during this litigation compounded his problems, and the two men's differences eventually took them beyond the courtroom to a duel in the press. *F. Thornton Miller is an instructor at the University of Alabama. This paper was presented at the 1987 meeting, in Philadelphia, of the Society for Historians of the Early American Republic. 298 The Virginia Magazine The principal issue raised in the Fairfax cases concerned federal appellate jurisdiction over state judiciaries. The Constitution allowed Congress to give state courts concurrent jurisdiction over issues arising under federal law. The federal judiciary would be smaller, and less expensive, if state courts were used in this way. The First Congress established this mixed legal system with the Judiciary Act of 1789. If, however, there was no system of appeals to a single court, there would be as many federal laws of the land as there were states. Section Twenty-five of the act, therefore, allowed appeals from the highest court of each state to the Supreme Court when the questions in a case involved the Constitution, treaties, or federal law. This process of appeal was carried out only a few times before the appeals in the Fairfax litigation (1813-16), when its validity was challenged because it was seen as a threat to state sovereignty. The issue over which the litigation began concerned the status of British and Tory property after the Revolution. In the treaty of 1783 ending the war, the British secured a promise from Congress that confiscations would end and that the states would respect British property rights. Those who became antifederalists in the 1780s opposed giving the British the legal basis to press their claims in spite of the treaty and, after 1788, the Constitution, which made treaties the supreme law of the land. In the Fairfax cases the antifederalists continued to argue that Virginia had acted, during and after the Revolution, as a sovereign nation and that its actions could not then or later be challenged by the federal government. Another issue in the litigation involved republicanism versus feudal- ism, or democracy versus elitism. About a third of the populated area of Virginia?including the Northern Neck, the area between the Rap- pahannock and Potomac Rivers, and the Upper Valley?had been practically a proprietary colony within a royal colony. Virginians believed that this feudal remnant had ended with the Revolution. Yet a federal treaty, the ratification of the Constitution, and the establishment of a federal judiciary assured that, years after independence was won, this proprietary domain could be reestablished through the Fairfax litigation. The cause of the suits was the ambiguity of the legal status of the proprietary land after the Revolution and after the death of Thomas, Lord Fairfax. The litigation followed several tracks related to Fairfax's own division of his property.
