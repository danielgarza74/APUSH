Selection Overview

The Peases explain how Charleston shifted from a Unionist bastion to a nullification stronghold between 1830 and 1832 as a two-party structure emerged. Anxiety over a stagnant economy, they argue, intensified the crisis: imports fell, the 1825 collapse of the cotton market brought depression, and many feared the 1828 tariff would leave the city ruined.

The Economics and Politics of Charleston's Nullification Crisis By JANE H. PEASE AND WILLIAM H. PEASE EVEN BEFORE THE 1828 TARIFF BECAME LAW, SOUTH CAROLINA had flirted openly with nullification.' Robert James Turnbull's 1827 "Brutus" letters, first published in the Charleston Mercury, had popularized the doctrine in Carolina's chief port. Then, a year later, the South Carolina Exposition endorsed interposition, and the state legislature seemed ready to dissolve the Union. When, in January 1830, the Webster-Hayne debate revealed an impending constitutional crisis to a shocked North, nullification had already become a practical political issue in South Carolina. Historians, long fascinated by the subsequent drama, have plumbed the economic and political implications inherent in An- drew Jackson's militant nationalism and John Caldwell Calhoun's brilliant assertion of states' rights. Some have found in the event as little as a personal clash between ambitious men; others, as much as a prelude to civil war. 336 THE JOURNAL OF SOUTHERN HIS TOR Y Much examined as both national and state phenomenon, the nul- lification crisis has been largely ignored as a force shaping partisan politics in one of the South's major cities. Nonetheless, between 1830 and 1832, as a clear-cut two-party structure emerged in the city, Charleston shifted dramatically from a Unionist bastion to a nullification stronghold. Concomitant with that change, which shook the city to its core and laid bare a complex political culture, was a redistribution of economic as well as political power. True it was that fears generated by the intense partisanship which the crisis had engendered ultimately destroyed the party structure created by it. Nonetheless, the apparent relapse to traditional, deferential poli- tics occurred only after the nullification controversy had provided an opportunity for recent immigrants to seek community consider- ation for their distinctive ethnocultural values, had offered a means for businessmen to readjust competing economic interests, and, of most lasting significance, had served as a major channel through which socially prestigious and economically privileged men fought for power. Intensifying Charleston's nullification experience was anxiety over the city's stagnant economy in the 1820s. Between 1820 and 1830 her imports had diminished every year but one. In 1825 the international cotton market collapsed, and the dramatic growth in the city's cotton exports earlier in the decade leveled off. In the wake of that collapse, general economic depression followed. Un- employment and inflation plagued Charleston's inhabitants. Up- country legislators threatened to reduce the city's representation in a state government which already taxed city property and commer- cial income more heavily than agricultural land and produce. Charleston's appeals for aid to internal improvements went un- heard in the rest of the state. Then, in 1828, her languishing economy seemed further threatened by the new federal tariff. Many feared that commerce would soon "take its departure," leaving the city "deserted," its prospects "ruined."3 Any change, therefore, promised improvement.
