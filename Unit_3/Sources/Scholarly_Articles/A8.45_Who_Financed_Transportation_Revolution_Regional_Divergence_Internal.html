THE JOURNAL OF ECONOMIC HISTORY VOLUME 56 DECEMBER 1996 NUMBER 4 Who Financed the Transportation Revolution? Regional Divergence and Internal Improvements in Antebellum Pennsylvania and Virginia JOHN MAJEWSKI Comparing investment in transportation companies in Albemarle county, Virginia, and Cumberland county, Pennsylvania, I analyze why nineteenth-century internal improvements revolutionized the northern economy, but only seemed to increase the South's dependence upon plantation agriculture. In both counties local investors and state governments financed early improvements. Beginning in the 1830s, however, urban capitalists financed most Pennsylvania railroads, leading to the eventual integration of railroads into coherent systems. Local investors and the state government continued to dominate Virginia's railroads, which made it difficult to build improvements to pierce the Appalachians and link the Old Dominion to midwestern markets. Economic historians now explain early American industrialization with a story that takes its plot from Adam Smith and its supporting characters from George Rogers Taylor's The Transportation Revolution. Like Smith, recent scholarship stresses the importance of larger markets in providing incentives to increase specialization, spur incremental innova- tions, and raise productivity. In antebellum America, markets grew larger through urbanization, population growth, and, most importantly, improved transportation. The construction of turnpikes, river improvements, canals, and railroads helped connect farm families and small towns with mer- chants and manufactures in larger cities, setting the stage for Smithian growth. Whereas improved transportation in the North set the stage for a vibrant, diversified economy, transportation projects in the South seemed only to foster an unhealthy reliance on staple crops. More than 80 years ago U. B. Phillips sternly pronounced that "the building of railroads [in the South] led to little else but the extension and intensifying of the plantation system and the increase of the staple output."2 Considerable evidence suggests that Phillips's assessment, however exaggerated, has a large measure of truth to it. Despite constructing thousands of miles of railroads in the 1850s-the slave states more than quadrupled their railroad mileage during the decade-the South remained well behind the North in key measures of economic development: inventive activity, industrial output, manufacturing productivity, and urbanization.3 In light of the long debate over the profitability of slavery, few historians would deny that slavehold- ers displayed considerable entrepreneurial vigor in pursuing the profits of staple agriculture. Yet as the antebellum period came to a close, even some slaveowners were dismayed that the boom in southern railroad construction had failed to replicate the results of northern transportation improvements. Charles Bruce, a state senator from Virginia, spoke for many when he complained that his state's motley collection of local lines "have built up no town, and hardly a single village."4 I try to explain the differing impact of transportation improvements through a comparison of infrastructure projects in two rural localities- Albemarle county, Virginia, and Cumberland county, Pennsylvania. While hardly representative of the diverse American landscape, Albemarle and Cumberland provide many clues about the diverging regional paths of the transportation revolution. Known for their rich soils and productive farms, both counties were obvious candidates for new transportation projects.
