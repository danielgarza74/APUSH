PATRIOTS AND CHRISTIANS: A REASSESSMENT OF NINETEENTH-CENTURY SCHOOL REFORMERS As anyone interested in the history of education knows, the publication in 1968 of The Irony of Early School Reform, by Michael Katz, initiated a vigorous reappraisal of the origins of public education in 19th-century America. Katz examined the pioneering school reform movement in antebellum Massa- chusetts and sharply questioned the standard historical interpretation. To earlier educational historians, the school reformers were humanitarians responding to popular demands, and the common-school crusade in Massachusetts was the vanguard of a national movement to establish public, secular schooling. Katz, however, saw the reformers as anxious, upper-class citizens who imposed bureau- cratic school systems on a reluctant community, in order to control the children of a changing society. The school reform movement, he concluded, was a response not to democracy but to the problems of an urban industrial state. His argument has been bolstered by subsequent studies which concentrate on city schools and relate educational reform to urban growth and fear of social disorder. This revisionist interpretation has demolished the familiar and comforting myths of traditional educational history. But it leaves a number of problems unresolved. First, the thesis that "public education in nineteenth-century America grew with the nation's cities" fails to explain the establishment of similar school systems in rural America where most of the people lived.2 Second, recent preoccupation with urban school reform understates the historic relation- ship between education and nationalism. Based on examination of the formative years of reform in Massachusetts between 1830 and 1837, this essay advances another interpretation: the educa- tional awakening was a Protestant crusade to establish a culture that became the dominant system of values in the new nation. This interpretation does not reject the work of revisionist historians; on the contrary it accommodates the urban thesis. The early school reformers assuredly were conservative, upper-class citi- zens disturbed about the disruptive effects of urban and industrial growth. But revisionist historians, frankly disillusioned with the bureaucratic school systems of contemporary urban America, project the present back into the past when they isolate urban growth as the catalyst of change.3 Examination of the early reform movement reveals that reformers were impelled by a driving anxiety about the progress and character of republican society - not just urban republi- can society. They believed that the republic would survive only if all children - from the smallest villages and farms as well as the largest cities - were trained in the values inherited from the Puritan past. PATRIOTS AND CHRISTIANS 555 The role of religion has received short shrift from the two leading schools of educational history, despite striking evidence of its importance not only in school reform but in the many-faceted reform activity of antebellum America. Yet religious influence in the growth of public education has been recognized by other scholars. In 1964, for example, in her study of 19th-century school- books, Ruth Elson observed that they were written mainly by New Englanders, many of whom were clergymen. Elson states that the authors saw the function of their books as the creation of American nationality, and that the "national truths" of their texts were based on Christian ethics and the Puritan heritage. Elson significantly entitled her book Guardians of Tradition. Two years later, an essay by David Tyack on the growth of public education in the Far West before the Civil War emphasized the crucial role of the Protestant clergy. And in another important article, published in 1967, Timothy Smith explored school reform movements in the Middle West and New York City between 1800 and 1850. He concluded that they were vigorous attempts to establish systems of Protestant schooling (a purpose clearly understood by the Catholic hierarchy), and were vitally connected with the wider antebellum crusade to establish "a national identity that was not simply religious but distinctively Protestant." The crusade was so successful, Smith declared, that by 1850 Protestantism had become synonymous with Americanism.4 More recently two other scholars have continued this theme. In Americans and the California Dream, Kevin Starr asserts that in California education went hand in hand with the Protestant churches in a determined attempt to found a Christian state on the western sea. Before the Civil War, New Englanders, led by the clergy, established the California common-school system, modeled on that of Massachusetts. California, one clergyman proclaimed in 1850, must become "the Massachusetts of the Pacific," a society founded on religion and self-rule.5 In another study - on the emergence of the teaching profession in the 19th century - Paul Mattingly stresses the primary role of religion in educational reform. He takes the religious convictions of school reformers seriously and maintains the importance of their belief that only Christian teachers could shape a national character. Mattingly expresses dissatisfaction with the fashionable thesis of revisionist educational history, centered on urban industrialism. Its basic flaw, he writes, "is the failure to account for the moral dynamic and religious underpinnings which set the context for all discussions of educational and social reform before the Civil War."6 The example of Massachusetts stimulated and inspired school reformers throughout the nation, and it is essential that we get at the heart of its common-school crusade. An understanding of its intrinsic religious impulse, coupled with the insights of historians who detect a similar impulse behind the movement in other parts of the country, should lead to the proper placement of common-school reform in Massachusetts within the wider antebellum Protestant crusade to forge a Christian nation.7 The educational reform movement in Massachusetts emerged as a purposeful, organized force in 1830 with the founding in Boston of the American Institute of Instruction.
