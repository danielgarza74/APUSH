Social Reform and the Divided Conscience of Antebellum Protestantism JAMES H. MOORHEAD It is a commonplace of antebellum historiography that the numerous reforms of the age often bore an intimate connection with Protestant evangelicalism, and Charles Grandison Finney is often portrayed as a symbol of this link. In addition to endorsing such causes as antislavery and temperance, the great evangelist inspired numerous converts to work out their salvation through useful service, including reform; and the areas swept by his revivals provided fertile soil for every manner of ultraism. Both as theological innovator and religious activist, he seemed to epitomize a tide of perfectionist optimism surging with great force against institutional restraints.1 Yet there was a very cautious side to Finney. He seldom committed himself unreservedly to any cause other than revivalism and generally eschewed the most controversial approaches to reform. Viewing this aspect of his career, one scholar has recently argued that "the basic thrust of Finney's thought and activity was conservative, status conscious, and pessimistic about human nature."2 Because of these two faces, the historian is tempted to fix on one or the other as the "real" Finney, but it is more profitable to probe his ambiguities than to mitigate them. An examination of Finney offers fruitful insight into nineteenth-century evan- gelicalism's explosive potential for reform and its equally powerful tendency to limit and contain that impulse. SOCIAL REFORM AND PROTESTANTISM 417 explained: "The law of God requires us to will, or intend the promotion of every interest in the universe, according to its perceived value, for its own sake; in other words, that all our powers shall be supremely and disinter- estedly devoted to the glory of God, and the good of the universe."3 By thus defining virtue, Finney was espousing a theory which had been popularized in the eighteenth century and which many American theologians, most notably Jonathan Edwards, had made an important feature of their thought. In Finney's hands, as in those of most antebellum moralists, the concept lost the richly aesthetic and ontological overtones it had possessed for Edwards and became little more than a summons to do good for others.4 Disinterested benevolence was the keynote in a practical, task-oriented theology ready- made for the adherents of the various voluntary societies. Although he rejected the term, Finney tended toward utilitarianism. He strenuously objected to the thesis that certain acts were in themselves right or wrong, irrespective of the end promoted. This "rightarian" approach was "godless, Christless, and loveless," for it set persons "in chase of a philosophic abstraction" and ignored "the concrete reality of the highest well-being of the universe."5 Morality was contextual for the evangelist; that is, the rightness of a deed could not be determined a priori, but only by looking at specific cases and asking whether they were expressions of "selfish purposes" or "benevolent design."6 This ethic provided a critical standard by which to judge all practices, no matter how venerable or well-entrenched; and even when it did not lead directly to reform, it narrowed the grounds on which institutions could be justified and placed upon them a burden of proof: were they in fact useful to the well-being of God and humanity? That latent subversiveness was heightened by the enlarged boundaries to which Finney stretched benevolence. The goal was to attain the greatest good for the entire universe, and efforts to achieve less were not virtue.
