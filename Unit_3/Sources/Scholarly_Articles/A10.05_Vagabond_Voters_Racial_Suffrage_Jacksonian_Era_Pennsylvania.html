Selection Overview

Blair explains how Pennsylvania eliminated Black suffrage at its constitutional convention of 1837-1838, even as voting rights for white men generally expanded. Democrats, he argues, joined racism to class fears that Black voters would unite with transient white migrants, the vagabond voters set loose by Jacksonian-era economic change, against the interests of propertied white men.

william a. blair Vagabond Voters and Racial Suffrage in Jacksonian-Era Pennsylvania Pennsylvania eliminated black suffrage during its constitutional convention of 1837-1838. Although racism was a motivating force, white supremacists also reacted against the flood of so-called vagabond voters: migrants and transients unleashed by the economic changes of the Jacksonian era. Transient voters who had to move to find work became more typical in this period of expanding white suffrage. Bias against immigrants—including native-born men from other states—thus merged with hatred of African Americans as concerns for Democrats, as racial exclusion became one of the means by which to handle a constellation of elements that were viewed as having the potential to create disorder in power arrangements. In 1837, delegates in Harrisburg argued over African American suffrage as they amended the Pennsylvania constitution. Democrats wanted to change the provision that allowed for black voting. The arguments for such an act involved more than racism, overlapping with class-based worries of the harm that could come if a coalition formed of black and white indigent and transient voters. Some hoped to overturn a constitutional provision requiring taxes to be paid in order to vote, but a delegate from Lancaster County warned against this move. He declared that the loss of an economic restriction would attract what one scholar has termed indigent transients and who he called “the vicious vagrant, the wandering Arabs, the Tartar hordes .” Eliminating the tax qualification for voting, it was argued, opened the state to a potential flood of vagabond voters. As long as black people could vote—people who were often stereotyped as indigents themselves— it was feared that they would join with the transient newcomers to contest the interests of propertied white men. Bias against immigrants from other countries, as well as from other states, merged with stereotypes of black people to support exclusionary measures in an era otherwise characterized as expanding male suffrage. This included a wide range of people, from hucksters to ragpickers, sailors to canal-builders, railroad workers to day laborers. One scholar has described this phenom- enon as a human column that moved through the antebellum regions of North or South, rural or urban, which typically lost from one-half to three-quarters of adult residents from one census to the next. In the case of Springfield, Illinois, the result meant that in every single election over the last decade before the Civil War, one-time voters outnumbered more permanent residents by seven to one. By 1850, 30 percent of Americans everywhere were either native-born migrants traveling between states or foreign-born immigrants. 2 The influx of these migrants to Pennsylvania became more of a concern during the Panic of 1837, which contracted employment opportunities, and as fugitive slaves and free blacks fled the slave states, which increased the African American population. Migrants who were considered poor, a drain on local charity, and potentially violent were met, as Kate Masur shows in her article in this issue, by expanding police powers at the municipal level. 3 In the case of Pennsylvania, these concerns over indigent transients were also handled by using racial and economic measures to exclude peo- ple from voting. Racial proscription would take care of one set of individu- als. The tax provision would work against transients. Requiring payment of taxes had the additional benefit of solving the problem of voter fraud. In an era without forms of voter identification, paying one’s taxes provided proof of residency and of a person’s intent to establish permanent roots in a community. In other words, permanent residents could be considered worthy electors with an interest in local issues and who bore the conse- quences of the result. An increasingly restless population corresponded with what more than one scholar has described as a high-water mark for state constitution- making in the United States. The last thirty years before the Civil War saw the codifying and constitutionalizing of racial suffrage even as voting rights for white men generally expanded. It was an era in which American democracy was redefined, based on the exclusion of black people, women, and Native Americans. This was not taking place at the national level but within states, whose actions would inform the ruling made by Roger B. Taney in 1857 that black people were not citizens.
