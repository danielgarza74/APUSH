486 THE JOURNAL OF SOUTHERN HIS TOR Y The contribution of the fear of abolitionism to the muzzling of temperance in the South still remains to be assessed by historians. What is most striking is not the absence of temperance agitation, but its attenuated character and its inability to command broad community support. Despite the fears of abolitionism, some south- erners did campaign for temperance and prohibition, and any as- sessment of the role of antislavery in shaping the character of southern reform must involve a close study of those who dissented from the dominant southern attitude of indifference or hostility toward temperance. How did southern reformers differ from other southerners, and, equally important, how did they compare with the groups supporting temperance in the northern states? Answers to these questions throw light both on the course of temperance reform in the South and on the relationship among industrializa- tion, social change, and reform activity throughout the nation. Though the pattern of southern temperance would soon diverge sharply from that in the North, the South's early participation in temperance reform closely paralleled the northern in timing and inspiration. In October 1826, just a few months after the organiza- tion of the American Temperance Society in Boston, the Virginia Society for the Promotion of Temperance was established. By 1831 the temperance movement in the South had strong support in Virginia and Georgia, and there were societies in every state except Louisiana. As in the North, temperance sentiment developed most strongly among the adherents of evangelical religion. Methodists and Baptists, especially, carried the brunt of the early missionary work in the southern states. In both Virginia and Georgia the state temperance societies actually grew out of the active concerns of members of the states' Baptist conventions.5 Yet the parallels between northern and southern temperance were superficial and short-lived. By the mid-1830s it was clear to northern temperance leaders that the antiliquor crusade in the South gar- nered fewer recruits and provoked more open hostility than in any other section of the country. The first official admission that tem- perance lagged badly in the South came at the National Temperance Convention held in Philadelphia in 1833. Important organizational changes effected at the convention revealed the fears of temperance reformers that too close an association with New England abolition- ism had damaged the progress of temperance in the South.
