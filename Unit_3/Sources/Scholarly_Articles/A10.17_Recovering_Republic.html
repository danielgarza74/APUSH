148 SOUTH CAROLINA HISTORICAL MAGAZINE time that his white manhood suffrage amendment was officially ratified in December 1810, Calhoun had already parlayed his family name, his repu tation as a champion of "white men's democracy," and his skill on the stump into an easy victory over Laurens planter John Elmore in a race for a seat in the United States Congress.2 Once white manhood suffrage was established in South Carolina, a grassroots political culture, which was as doggedly and aggressively democratic as that in any other part of the young republic, emerged in most areas of the state. Between 1810 and 1850, voter turnouts exceeding seventy percent of all eligible voters were commonplace, and in hotly contested elections, such as the one held at the peak of the nullification crisis in 1832, voter participation approached the ninety percent level in many portions of the state. Moreover, the rate of turnover among elected officials was quite high. Seldom did as many as forty percent of the state legislators from any given General Assembly return to serve in the next General Assembly, and on at least one occasion, 1818, nearly eighty percent of all incumbent legislators either refused to stand for re-election or were defeated at the polls. In many areas, candidates for the legislature had literally spoken to every eligible voter in their districts and in all probability had visited with most voters either in their homes or at their churches.3 It was a furiously political age. Candidates wooed voters, praising liberty and denouncing aristocracy and privilege. Voters reponded with extraordinarily high turnouts. Incumbents held few advantages and every new General Assembly was lavishly sprinkled with new faces. The politi cian's power, no matter how wisely used, and his ambition, no matter how noble or civic-minded, were always constrained by the reckoning of elec tion day.4 Nurtured in this political milieu, one open and competitive and demo cratic by the standards of the age, John C. Calhoun matured into the most sophisticated political thinker of the Jacksonian era and arguably the most original political theorist in all of American history. Yet for all of Calhoun's importance as a political actor as well as a thinker, the political and intellectual context of his contributions to American constitutional thought has often been misunderstood. In The Liberal Tradition in America, Hartz depicted Calhoun as a wayward Lockean liberal trying to fashion a systematic defense of slavery in a society which lacked the materials needed to erect a genuine conservative tradition. Hartz pictured Calhoun as the chief architect of a so-called "Reactionary Enlightenment," during which the slave-holding South self-consciously repudiated its Jef fersonian origins and retreated behind a hard but brittle protective facade of constitutional literalism. This strategic intellectual retreat, designed to facilitate a more effective defense of slavery, ended in failure, Hartz main tained, because neither Calhoun nor other southern leaders could deny their own Revolutionary heritage.6 As a quasi-feudal conservative, Calhoun cut a sorry figure. If Hartz judged Calhoun a failed Tory, Hofstadter saw him as a prophet of reaction. In his influential "Marx of the Master Class" essay, Hofstadter portrayed Calhoun as Marx standing on his head, a visionary who saw impending class conflict but who identified with capital rather than labor. To Hofstadter, Calhoun was not a nostalgic Burkean trying to resurrect a mythical feudal past but an aggressive, perceptive conservative trying to construct an alliance between southern planters and northern capitalists that would serve to protect the prerogatives of men of property from radical assault.7 Yet both Hartz and Hofstadter passed lightly over Calhoun's constitutional thought, focusing instead on Calhoun as an apologist for what they perceived as the hierarchical social order of the Old South. 5On the transatlantic republican tradition, see especially J.
