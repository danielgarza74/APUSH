Selection Overview

Ashworth questions Hofstadter's standard view that Jacksonian Democrats sought only equal opportunity and equality before the law, the creed of the self-made entrepreneur. He suggests instead that Democrats saw men as essentially the same, making the Jacksonian a leveller, though he notes that they denied equality to Black people, Indians, women, and children.

The Jacksonian as Leveller JOHN ASHWORTH Over the past two decades Jacksonian historiography has been enriched by the publication of a large number of scholarly articles and monographs. Legislative voting in the states and in Congress has been analysed, the social composition of the two major parties has been studied, the mechanics of the political system have been scrutinized and political ideas have been re examined.1 The present article will be concerned with ideology, the belief system of the Jacksonian Democratic party. It will deal specifically with the Jacksonian conception of equality. Surprisingly, this is a facet of Democratic ideology to which relatively little attention has been paid.2 In The American Political Tradition, published in 1948, Richard Hofstadter presented what is perhaps the standard view of Jacksonian equality. He cited Andrew Jackson's Bank Veto Message of 1832. Jackson here affirmed that Distinctions in society will always exist under every just government. Equality of talents, of education, or of wealth cannot be produced by human institutions. In the full enjoyment of the gifts of Heaven and the fruits of superior industry, economy, and virtue, every man is equally entitled to protection by law. It consequently opposed and denounced the far more radical demand for equality of outcome; indeed the necessary and desired result of equal opportunity was an inequality of outcome. This outlook was indeed, as Hofstadter argued, that of a rising middle class. It was the philosophy of the liberated capitalist, of the dynamic entrepreneur, of the self-made man.3 But was it the philosophy of the Jacksonians? While it is true that this view of Jacksonian Democracy as a movement of middle class entrepreneurs has been severely criticized in recent years it is surely noteworthy that the view of Jacksonian egalitarianism which Hofstadter expressed has not been revised. The Jacksonian as embattled agrarian has now replaced, in many analyses, the Jacksonian as entrepreneur.4 But historians still note that by espousing only the limited ideal of equal opportunity and equality before the law, the Democrat was powerless to prevent the emergence of an economy dominated by ever larger units of industrial and financial capital. As an ideology Jacksonian Democracy was shallow and incoherent; as a radical force it was impotent. It can be suggested, however, that the radicalism of the Democrats was undermined not by an inadequate commitment to egalitarianism but by the zeal with which they clung to their faith in equality. For at the core of Democratic ideology was a notion that men were, as social and political units, essentially the same. The Jacksonian was a leveller.5 It would of course be unwise to press this idea too far. No Democrat in the Jacksonian era envisaged an absolute equality of wealth; no Democrat believed that men were precisely the same. Moreover, the Jacksonian was the first to insist that blacks and Indians could not be considered the equals of whites. Nor were women or children the equals of adult males. Thus some Americans remained more equal than others. It must also be admitted that not all Jacksonians subscribed to the view of equality which I have chosen to term "levelling." The Democratic party in the 1820s, 30s and 40s was always internally divided. In the years following the Panic of 1837 tne struggles within the party came to be predominantly between radical and conservative elements.
