PROMOTIONAL WIZARDRY: RHETORIC AND RAILROAD ORIGINS, 1820-1860 James A. Ward Railroads permeate American culture. Old train depots on the National Register of Historic Places now serve fancy dinners, steam excursions are packed with enthusiastic throngs, railway books sell well, railroad organizations flourish, trains magazines crowd newsstand shelves, model trains chug across countless cellar empires, and even railroad stamps find eager collectors. The popularity of railroads illustrates the substantial contributions they have made to national life. They created our time zones, infused our language with countless phrases such as "on time" and "highball," enriched our country's music with unforgettable melodies such as "The Chattanooga Choo-Choo," "The Wabash Cannonball," and the immortal "Casey Jones, " created unique environments along our rail right-of-ways, raised public architecture to new heights with "union" stations, provided jobs for generations of unskilled immigrants, and used their corporate power and wealth to change American democracy. Yet historians have evinced little interest in railway origins, and those who have dealt in any depth with the topic usually concluded that the rails grew out of ferocious trade rivalries among Atlantic port cities. An examination of the contemporary railroad promotional literature, however, reveals that urban competition was only one tile in the rhetorical mosaic railroad boosters carefully designed to convince fellow countrymen to embrace the new transport system. Railroad enthusiasts preferred language fraught with symbolism to bond their readers emotionally to the new industry. Their constant references to the fabled West, for example, stirred public passions for railways as a means to tap the unbounded wealth they believed was hidden in the woods. At quite another level, these appeals hinted at a chance for adventure, pushing back the limits of the unknown, and the pleasant surprises that awaited those ambitious enough to spike iron rails into the wilderness. The conquering technology of steam, promoters promised, would also use its manifest power and speed to hasten the nation's destiny across the continent. The enthusiasts' public rhetoric was, like the country's attitude towards its own future, always upbeat and optimistic-even in the face of a shortage of construction funds and a multitude of geographic obstacles that had to be overcome. Rails, they claimed, would create their own wealth and business, raise everyone's land values, lessen sectional and urban rivalries, and benefit all classes. At the same time the promoters' rhetoric promised not only individual pecuniary returns, but the more indirect monetary returns that would benefit society. Tax revenues would increase when home property values rose, all cities would prosper as rail hubs, and the whole citizenry would be propelled upwards towards a higher level of general prosperity. Boosters hammered at the notion that the public weal, railroad companies, and personal welfare were inextricably joined by link and pin couplers. Rather than elevating their new industry above common concerns, they preached the gospel of railways in a language that was comforting-and ironic. In an age moving rapidly towards a virulent individualism, railway promoters pitched their projects with rhetoric that promised to knit the nation together through communities of interest, interwoven to enhance the country's power, wealth, and unity. The boosters argued that their projects were absolutely vital to America's general interests and, in fact, asserted that every mile of rail laid was a patriotic manifestation. With such rhetoric, enthusiasts injected railways deep into the American psyche and in so doing created a new business that almost immediately became a boom industry.
