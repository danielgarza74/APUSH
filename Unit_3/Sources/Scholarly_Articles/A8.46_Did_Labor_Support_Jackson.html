DID LABOR SUPPORT JACKSON?: THE BOSTON STORY IT has lately come to be accepted gospel that eastern workingmen supported Andrew Jackson and his party during the period of his presidency, and that " he could not have been elected presi- dent if the votes of the laboring men of the Northeast had not been added to those of his followers in the Southeast and the West." 1 A recent study has indicated that for the city of Philadelphia, at least, this thesis is not tenable.2 This paper's concern is with the workingmen of Boston and their participation in the political arena during Jackson's two terms. As a key urban center in that period Boston merits examination. So unprecedented had been the expansion of industry in that city that by 1837 over ten thousand workers were engaged in some form of manufacturing enterprise.3 Hardships and inequalities bearing on workingmen throughout the seaboard area found classic expression in Boston; 4 and the level of political and economic activity of the work- ing class there made it one of the cities most prominent in the struggles of organized labor.5 A study of the attitudes of Boston workers toward the party of Jackson, then, should furnish us with extremely relevant data as to the validity of the thesis that an important share of Jackson's success was due to the support he received from labor. It would seem that in part the problem could best be solved by studying the positions and platforms of the workingmen's parties, if any, and noting their relationship with the Democratic party, and their support or opposition on specific and general issues. In the first place, " The Workingmen's Party " of Boston was an extremely weak political organization, never repre- senting more than a tiny fraction of the workers.6 Since many of its candidates were Whigs and among the wealthiest men in the com- munity, a good case might be made for the startling thesis that the party of Boston's workingmen was a front organization for the Whigs. Actually, in so far as organizational activity is concerned, the main energies of the workers were directed along paths somewhat removed from politics. They organized to strike for a ten-hour day in 1832 and again in 1835; they served as the nucleus of a New England organization that combined political with economic action, The New England Association of Farmers, Mechanics, and Other Working Men,7 which lasted from 1832 to 1834; and in the year of the Asso- ciation's demise, 1834, they formed a trades union which at its height numbered more than 4,000 workers, representing sixteen trades, in its membership.8 It was in these movements rather than in political parties that outlet was found for the expression of their widely rang- ing demands. Boston's workingmen remained in large part outside the short-lived party of 1830-1831. Let us look at the voting record of labor for the entire period 1828-1836. The ideal data for this study would consist of lists of all the voters, precise descriptions of their occupa- tions and social position-that is, whether they were wealthy or poor, journeymen or masters, small retail dealers or large merchants-and, finally, a tabulation of their votes. In the absence of such definitive material, a less satisfactory technique must be utilized, but one which has a common-sense validity and, in practice, has been effectively employed. The wards of the city have been broken down ac- cording to population and assessed valuation; these wards with the lowest assessment per person have been assumed to be the working- class wards.9 Other statistics substantiate these findings.
