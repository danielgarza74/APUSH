THE JOURNAL OF ECONOMIC HISTORY VOLUME 66 SEPTEMBER 2006 NUMBER 3 Rethinking the Jacksonian Economy. The Impact of the 1832 Bank Veto on Commercial Banking JANE KNODELL Revisiting an old debate between "traditional" historians and "New" economic historians about the short-run monetary effects of President Jackson's war on the Second Bank, I offer new arguments for the traditionalists. I develop a frame- work accounting for growth in commercial bank credit based on the "investment club" characteristics of antebellum state-chartered banking and apply it to state bank balance sheet data for 1830-1836. The removal of the Second Bank indeed magnified the boom in commercial banking, and the replacement of Second Bank branches with state-chartered banks as lenders and federal government de- positories amplified the boom in frontier lands and state debt. T he 1830s were a period of rapid financial expansion in the United States; the money supply grew, capital inflows increased, and asset prices rose significantly. Historians traditionally thought that the boom in both the banking system and the market for public lands at the west- ern frontier of settlement occurred because of President Andrew Jack- son's veto of the federal rechartering of the Second Bank in July 1832, but the work of Peter Temin, Hugh Rockoff, and George Macesich overturned this interpretation and established a new conventional wis- dom.' In the three decades since the contributions of Temin, Rockoff, and Macesich, however, we have learned more about the unique capital structure and lending strategies of antebellum banks and about the sym- biotic relationship between state governments and state-chartered banks.2 Recent scholars have shown renewed interest in the financial crises of the late 1830s and early 1840s, which were marked by falling asset prices, commercial bank failures and state debt defaults. For ex- ample, Peter Rousseau has uncovered a new culprit in the Panic of 1837, the "supplemental" interbank transfers ordered by the U.S. Treas- ury in June 1836, and John Wallis has produced a detailed study of the long depression of 1839-1843, in which state defaults play a leading role in the commercial banking crisis of 1841-1842.3 Both studies show that federal and state governments' management of their finances had powerful monetary effects in this period, effects that were ignored by the New historians' version of the story. Given these developments in the literature, it is time to take a fresh look at how President Jackson's war on the Second Bank affected do- mestic finance in the 1830s. After reviewing the traditional hypothesis about the Bank Veto and the now-conventional story about monetary expansion in the 1830s, I present an alternative account of the sources of growth in commercial bank credit in the 1830s. Then I apply this al- ternative approach to an analysis of the monetary effects of removing the Second Bank. I conclude that the removal of the Second Bank am- plified the boom in state banking, state borrowing, and land develop- ment, thereby contributing to the financial distress of the early 1840s, particularly in the South and West-but that it did so through monetary mechanisms different from those posited by either the traditional histo- rians or the New economic historians. AN OLD DEBATE AND A NEW FRAMEWORK After President Jackson's veto and his October 1833 executive or- der forbidding further deposits of Treasury receipts in the Second Bank, money supply growth accelerated. From 1834 to 1836 the money supply grew at an average annual rate of 30 percent, compared to 2.7 percent between 1831 and 1834.
