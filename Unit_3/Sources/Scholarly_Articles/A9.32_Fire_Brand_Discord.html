"The Fire-Brand of Discord": The North, the South, and the Savannah Fire of 1820 By Matthew Mason January 11, 1820, a fire broke out in Savannah, Georgia. It began at two in the morning in a livery stable in the most densely populated part of the city. Several factors combined to make it a massive conflagration. Savannah's weather had been dry for the preceding four months and a stiff wind blew that night. Furthermore, in short order the blaze spread to the town's Baptist church square, where for some reason several of the shops contained large amounts of gunpowder. One report stated that the explosion of this powder, together with the wind (made more powerful by the fire) created an inferno that "bid defiance to all human controul intense," continued the account, "that several large buildings consid- ered as fireproof, went with the rest. Many of the houses were cleared of their contents, but these were generally burnt in the streets, from the impossibility of removing them out of the reach of the element!" 444 Georgia Historical Quarterly This disaster devastated Savannah more than psychologically. Because residents quickly abandoned their fruitless efforts to douse the flames, and especially because inhabitants of the mar- ket square area fled, only two of Savannah's seven thousand peo- ple died and just two were injured. But the fire wiped out whole industries; one observer noted there was "not a barbers nor apoth- ecaries shop, a hardware, dry goods, stationary, crockery or cloth- ing store left!" Besides these businesses, the blaze destroyed 463 houses and their outbuildings. Cold and rainy weather in the fol- lowing weeks only exacerbated the suffering of the people ren- dered homeless. Residents estimated the direct property damage at four million dollars and the indirect losses at six million more. They calculated that the powder explosion alone ruined one mil- lion dollars' worth. Perhaps the best measure of the catastrophe was that almost half of the city lay in ashes.2 This calamity claimed nationwide attention. At first glance it seemed to do so at a propitious time, as a debate raged in Con- gress and throughout the nation over whether to restrict slavery in the prospective state of Missouri. Given that this already months- long dispute had threatened national unity, many Northerners embraced calls for aid to Savannah as an opportunity to show goodwill to their southern countrymen. But in the end the Savan- nah fire, far from the hoped-for balm for the Union's wounds, be- came yet another sectional irritant, since other Northerners were not prepared to set aside their antagonism toward the South. The conditions that some northern philanthropists attached to their donations set offa chain of recriminations that proved the nation could not come together in the face of disaster. Neither was it surprising that citizens of the Mid-Atlantic states led both columns in the North's divided response. The Mid-Atlan- tic supplied not only the largest donors and the most prominent voices for attaching stipulations to relief for this slave-state city, but also leadership of the northern fight against extending slavery to Missouri. Thus, the particulars of this sectional flare-up over aid to Savannah illustrate the spin-off effects of the Missouri crisis specif- ically and of sectionalism more generally.
