Selection Overview

Carwardine argues that historians' focus on Charles Grandison Finney, whose New Measures revivals swept western New York in the mid-1820s and reached eastern cities, has obscured other developments in urban revivalism. He questions the view that Finney almost single-handedly created the city revival style of the mid-nineteenth century and examines what was happening in the major cities before and during his movement.

The Second Great Awakening in the Urban Centers: An Examination of Methodism and the 'New Measures' RICHARD CARWARDINE ALMOST every discussion of the shaping of religious revivalism in early and mid-nineteenth century America has been centered on and dominated by the revival movement of Charles Grandison Finney., Finney's revivals in the mid- 1820s swept the "Burned-over District" of western New York and then spread eastward to challenge the bastions of conservative Presbyterianism in urban centers. He threw aside the restraints and caution of an older generation of ministers sympathetic to revivalism and employed a variety of "New Measures" to promote revivals-measures which, when supported by the equally contro- versial New Haven theology, helped provoke an ultimately schismatic conflict. Challenging what he termed "hyper-Calvinism"-the unrelenting and long- standing Calvinistic beliefs on the subject of human inability, and of limited atonement and election-by arguing that the individual need not passively wait to see if he were one of the saved, Finney used a style of revivalism that gave the sinner and the minister a positive and active role in the conversion process.2 Although checked momentarily in 1827 at New Lebanon by a convention called to discuss his New Measures, Finney ultimately found his way into Philadel- phia, New York City, Boston, and other cities of the East., Richard Carwardine teaches history in the University of Sheffield. 328 The Journal of American History As significant as this Finneyite movement undoubtedly was, the emphasis that has been given to it has tended to obscure other developments in religious revivalism at this time. The extravagant claims of Gilbert H. Barnes-that the New Measures were Finney's "invention" -are no longer given credence., How- ever, the two most scholarly treatments of American revivalism, those of William G. McLoughlin, Jr., and Bernard A. Weisberger, give such prominence to Finney's role in the development of a mass urban evangelism that equally significant developments contemporaneous with Finney have been passed by. Looking backward from the revivalism of the twentieth century and of Dwight L. Moody, McLoughlin gives Finney a near-revolutionary role in the birth of a "Modern Revivalism" and an urban revival style; while Weisberger takes as his starting-point the contribution of the individual revivalist to a developing revival pattern, and sees Finney as bringing "the lusty breath of the Western revival into the East."' Such an emphasis has served to reinforce the long-held view that, virtually single-handed, Finney created the mid-nineteenth century revival style of the cities. To counterbalance this interpretation it is necessary to see more clearly what was happening in the major cities immediately prior to and during the Finneyite movement. This was not, after all, the first time that the urban centers had been visited by revivals.6 George Whitefield's itinerancy during the eighteenth-cen- tury Great Awakening included Philadelphia, where "commerce was sus- pended," New York, and the principal cities and towns of New England; and in the early nineteenth century the Second Great Awakening brought revivals to many of the main cities.7 To be sure, the latter revivals often conformed to a 4Gilbert Hobbs Barnes, The Antislavery Impulse 1830-1844 (New York, 1933), 7. ,McLoughlin, Modern Revivalism, 11; Weisberger, They Gathered at the River, 86. 6The place of revivalism in the religious life of the cities in the early and middle decades of the nineteenth century has received relatively little consideration from historians.
