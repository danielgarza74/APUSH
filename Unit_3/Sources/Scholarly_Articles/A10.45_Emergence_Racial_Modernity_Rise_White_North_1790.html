JLL, MAM) The Emergence of Racial Modernity and the Rise of the White North, 1790-1840 James Brewer Stewart In April 1831, a white abolitionist contributor to William Lloyd Garrison's Liberator, identifying himself as "T.T.," reported two highly contrasting "dreams" he had experienced. Each foretold the nation's future in the starkest of racial terms. In the first fantasy, set a generation ahead in time, Americans of every pigmentation had dispensed with racial differences as meaningful designations and were living together, placidly intermarried in unprecedented harmony. In the second, played out in the immediate future, racial hatreds had plunged a degraded nation into bloodlust with black insurrectionists enslaving their former masters and tyrannizing the white population.1 A brief consideration of "T.T"'s predictions allows us to begin to understand a compounding crisis in racial definitions and race relations that began developing in the North during the 1820s and which then finally exploded during the early 1830s. But this unprecedented plunge into violence also had far more substantial immediate origins in developments underway in the North since the 1820s, which were deeply inflaming northern race relations just as activists like "T.T." launched their crusade. These included heightened aspirations, accomplishments, and ideological disagreements among African-American activists; increasing conflict as well as collaboration within and between various segments of black and white communities; deepening allegiances to a volatile identity of "whiteness" among Euro- Americans generally; rapid colonizationist mobilization; and new forms of democratic politics that set "white" against "black" as never before. Considering the explosive legacies of the 1820s, the abolitionist demand for racial equality could not have been issued in a more hostile environment. Despite the inaccuracy of "T.T."'s predictions, he was certainly correct when insisting that the North in 1831 stood on the cusp of an unprecedented transformation of relations between races. When the mobs finally subsided by 1838, nearly everyone within the free states, whatever their pigmentation or political outlook, found them- selves responding to a world "T.T." had not foreseen. The North was rapidly evolving into a modern white supremacist political culture as people in the free states found themselves insolubly divided by pigmentation, invariably governed by the claims of biological inequality, and almost exclusively informed by racially self-referential approaches to politics and social reform. The abolitionists' initial crusade for racial equality had instead spawned an interclass upheaval among the vast majority of whites in the North that solidified into an immovable consensus of highly organized white supremacy, a polity premised on the uniform assumption that nature had always divided black and white as superior and inferior-and always must. By 1840 this spirit of essentialism pervaded nearly every element of northern society, politics, and cultural life, even affecting those who continued to struggle for racial justice. The white North, in short, had emerged into racial modernity, an age much more resembling the racial tyranny of the late nineteenth century than the racial contestation of the decades between 1776 and 1830.
