The Decline of Artisan Republicanism in Portland, Maine, 1825-1850 ROBERT H. BABCOCK ON a crisp autumn day in 1841, hundreds of craftsmen paraded through the streets of Portland, Maine, in a fervent display of solidarity. Marching were housewrights, masons, plasterers, and stonecutters from the building trades, and blacksmiths, machinists, coppersmiths, brass founders, and tinplate workers from among the sons of Vul- can. Shipbuilders, joiners, caulkers, pump and block mak- ers, mast and rope makers, riggers, and sailmakers left their waterfront workshops to join the procession. Printers, hat- ters, tailors, tanners, curriers, and shoemakers rounded out the ranks of Portland's sizable and self-confident artisan class. As they moved along their route, master craftsmen, journeymen, and apprentices proudly held aloft colorfully decorated silk banners, each measuring roughly 3 by 31/2 feet and all emblazoned with ancient craft symbols, mot- toes, and slogans, many painted by William Capen, one of the city's best-known sign makers.' Exhibiting a pride of craft and faith in democracy charac- teristic of artisan republicanism, Portland's hatters boasted on their banner, "We Crown the Sovereign People." "When History's page / no date can give / Of Empires overthrown," masons, plasterers, and stonecutters declared, "Our Coun- try's deeds / and ours shall live / In monumental stone." The printers identified themselves to bystanders as "The Tyrant's Foe, the People's Friend." Lest anyone think that merchant capitalists had alone built Portland's prosperity, shipyard craftsmen proclaimed, "We lay the foundations of commer- cial enterprise." Artisans engaged in construction trades an- nounced that their "Labour and Skill are indispensable for the advancement of civilization." The ancient symbol of a bared and upraised arm on the blacksmiths' banner was prepared to "strike while the iron is hot." Such slogans reflected an ideology shared by masters and craftsmen in the larger American cities during the Revolu- tion and early national period, an ideology that blended the traditional craft ideals of virtue, independence, and equal- ity with a core of patriotic values associated with the found- ing of the new republic. The manner in which that ideology was publicly proclaimed had its roots in ancient craft tradi- tions. For instance, the arm-and-hammer sign on the Port- land blacksmiths' banner had been adopted by several Lon- don artisans in the fifteenth century.
