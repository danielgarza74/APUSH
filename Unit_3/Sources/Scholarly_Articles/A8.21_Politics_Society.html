He contrasts the two older frameworks: Turner's stress on sectional interests and rivalries, and Schlesinger's 1945 claim that Jacksonian democracy was a struggle between a business community and the working classes.

POLITICS AND SOCIETY: TOWARD A JACKSONIAN SYNTHESIS Daniel Feller Thirty years have now passed since the appearance of the last narrative synthesis of the Jacksonian period. In 1959 Harper & Row published Glyndon G. Van Deusen's The Jacksonian Era, a volume in the New American Nation Series designed "to present, fairly and impartially, the history of the twenty years, 1828-1848, in American life . . . years full of color and movement, years full of significance for the American future." Van Deusen treated many subjects outside politics, but the centerpiece of his survey was a narrative of national political history, centering on issues and policies, campaigns and elections, politicians and parties.I In 1959 a decision to structure a comprehensive history of two decades in American history around a conventional political narrative required neither explanation nor apology. But the last three decades have witnessed a revolution in our understanding of all aspects of American life-political, economic, social, cultural. So it is not surprising that portions of Van Deusen's book have been superseded. What is surprising--indeed, astonishing-is that no new narrative synthesis has appeared to replace it. While several distinctive portraits of Jacksonian society have since appeared, none has attempted to incorporate the recent discoveries and new interpretations under the traditional umbrella of a political narrative.2 Daniel Feller is a member of the Department of History at the University of New Mexico. Formerly associated with the Andrew Jackson Papers Project, Mr. Feller is the author of The Public Lands in Jacksonian Politics (1984). He would like to thank Daniel Walker Howe, Thomas E. Jeffrey, Richard John, Richard P. McCormick, Gregory H. Nobles, Howard N. Rabinowitz, and Johanna Shields for their helpful comments on an earlier version of this article. Glyndon G. 136 JOURNAL OF THE EARLY REPUBLIC This extraordinary lacuna can hardly be attributed to the decline of narrative (a genre which continues to flourish in other periods of American history, notably the American Revolution and Civil War), nor to a general loss of interest in politics. Political narrative once dominated Jacksonian historiography because historians assumed that social change and cultural evolution gave impetus to, and were in turn encompassed within, political events. But that assumption has cracked under the impact of new knowledge and new interpretations. Historians of the last generation could no longer assume that in writing political history, they were also writing the history of society. No new synthesis appeared because one no longer seemed possible. Humpty Dumpty had fallen off the wall, and historians could not reassemble him. By 1959, two comprehensive narrative frameworks for Jacksonian history had already been propounded and debated. In two books, The Rise of the New West and The United States, 1830-1850, Frederick Jackson Turner had codified his argument for the primacy of sectionalism in American history. In Turner's view, the sectional maneuvering that drove Jacksonian politics grew out of contrasts embracing all aspects of American life: "Inevitable in such a changing country were sectional interests, sectional antagonisms, and sectional combinations. Each great area was evolving in its own way. Each had its own type of people, its own geographic and economic basis, its own particular economic and social interests." National politics was the stage on which these contrasts and rivalries were played out. Logically Turner began both books with descriptions of each section and then, having identified the players, went on to narrate the plot.3 In 1945 Arthur Schlesinger, Jr., countered that Jacksonian democracy was "a problem not of sections but of classes." Schlesinger presented a new formulation of the Jacksonian dialectic: a struggle for power between a Hamiltonian "business community" and "the working classes," the latter supported (as in the 1930s) by the Democratic party and a sympathetic executive. Our unfortunate habit of lumping together all the so-called "progressive" historians has obscured the sharpness of Schlesinger's dissent from Turner, and the extent to which his Age of Jackson was itself a work of calculated revisionism.
