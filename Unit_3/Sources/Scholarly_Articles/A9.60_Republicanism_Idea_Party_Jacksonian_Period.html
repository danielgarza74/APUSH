Selection Overview

Wilson questions accounts, such as Wallace's and Hofstadter's, in which Van Buren's Albany Regency won acceptance for a modern idea of permanent, competing parties. He argues that the eighteenth-century Country outlook still shaped Jacksonian views of party: Whigs kept invoking antiparty ideals, and Democrats, especially Van Buren, worked to tie party organization to the old republican faith.

REPUBLICANISM AND THE IDEA OF PARTY IN THE JACKSONIAN PERIOD Major Wilson There are many useful ways to think about party development in the United States during the Jacksonian period. In broadest terms party can be seen as a product of "modernization," a process characterized by an expanding market, the growing pluralism of interests, and the triumph of equalitarian ideals in the political sphere. As family and partnership ventures gave way to corporate forms of business, and as a myriad of new social organizations evinced the principle of volun- tary association, new party organization arose to mobilize a mass electorate, isolate public issues, and choose government officials. Driven by what Richard Hofstadter called a "passion for organiza- tion," a new breed of professional politicians began to displace the older patrician leadership with a political machine of interchangeable parts and to embrace party in some ways as a good or end in itself. Successive presidential elections provided more immediate stimuli for party formation and served, by 1840, to draw rather clearly the lines of the second party system. There were elements of continuity with the earlier parties, but the new party system was deemed by many students to be different and distinctively modern.' Along with new organization came the formulation of the modern "idea of a party system." 420 JOURNAL OF THE EARLY REPUBLIC Van Buren and key members of his Albany Regency contributed most to "the modern concept of a political party." In place of the old antiparty ideal, with its emphasis on a politics of consensus, they sought to justify a politics of conflict carried on through the competi- tion of permanent party organizations. As in the economic market- place, they seemed to suppose, the general interest or good of society would emerge in the political arena by the check and balance of sepa- rate and competing interests. Here was to be found, Hofstadter agreed, "a complete, articulate, quite sophisticated, quite modern de- fense of the political party' '-one that was thenceforth to order politi- cal life in democratic America.2 It is easy, however, to overstate the modernity of the second party system. One recent historian has found many elements of an older, deferential style of politics mingled with new party organization. Nor, as the present essay argues, did the modern "idea" of party enjoy an unqualified triumph. To the contrary, recent scholarship on the con- cept of republicanism invites a closer look at the way the eighteenth- century Country outlook helped to shape the perceptions of party held by those involved in the process." It has long been recognized that many Whigs continued to invoke the antiparty ideals of an earlier age, even as they were driven toward more effective party organiza- tion. Much less obvious, because relatively neglected by students of the period, was the effort of Democratic spokesmen to identify party organization with the old republican faith. Van Buren in particular attached great importance to this effort and devoted considerable at- tention to it.
