Selection Overview

Sheidley revisits Webster's "Second Reply to Hayne" of January 1830, long praised for refuting nullification and giving Americans a new idea of a single democratic union. She argues that the terms of Webster's nationalism were largely set by the sectional interests and culture of the conservative Massachusetts elite on whose behalf he spoke.

The Webster-Hayne Debate: Recasting New England's Sectionalism HARLOW W. SHEIDLEY HE excitement in Washington was palpable on the morning of 26 January 1830. The previous day Senator Robert Y. Hayne of South Carolina had concluded a compelling two-day attack on Daniel Webster and New England in which he ques- tioned not only the statesmanship of the senator from Massachu- setts but the loyalty of the region's Federalist leadership during the War of 1812. Word quickly went forth that Hayne had delib- erately "abused Mass. & Mr W.,"' and all of Washington antici- pated a reply. Webster did not disappoint his eager audience, nor did he dis- appoint posterity. The "Second Reply to Hayne" is generally as- cribed first rank among nineteenth-century American congres- sional addresses.? 6 THE NEW ENGLAND QUARTERLY the Union.3 More important, Webster's nationalist argument has been credited with giving Americans a new way of conceiving of their collective identity. In the words of Robert A. Ferguson, the speech "resonates with a common nationality at a time when most republicans still saw their country as a compact between the states"; thus Webster formalized "a shift in the American mind toward the idea of a single, democratic union." Or, as Mer- rill D. Peterson argues, "Webster... raised the idea of Union above contract or expediency and enshrined it in the American heart.'"4 The nationalism ofWebster's "Second Reply to Hayne" cannot and should not be denied, of course. But the specific terms of that nationalism and the manner in which Webster proclaimed it to the Senate and the country at large were in great measure determined by the sectional interests and culture of the conser- vative elite community of Massachusetts that had shaped Web- ster's vision and on whose behalf he spoke. Many historians have recognized that Webster was a sectional leader who successfully blocked a potential alliance between the West and the South against the Northeast by shifting the terms of debate from issues of public policy to constitutional interpretations.
