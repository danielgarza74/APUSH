Selection Overview

Pierce asks why the Virginia Convention moved from swearing allegiance to George III in August 1775 to instructing its delegates to propose independence in May 1776. He distinguishes the causes of resistance from the arguments for independence and concludes that pride in being English and constitutionalism were the two major forces behind the independence movement in Virginia.

THE INDEPENDENCE MOVEMENT IN VIRGINIA, 1775-1776 by MICHAEL D. PIERCE * ON August 23, I775, the Virginia Convention, extralegal governing body of the colony, approved a resolution which swore "before God and the World, that we do bear faith and true allegiance to His Majesty George the Third, our only lawful and rightful King."' Nine months later, in an apparently complete reversal, the Virginia Convention instructed its dele- gates to the Continental Congress "to propose to that respectable body to declare the United Colonies free and independent States, absolved from all allegiance to, or dependence upon, the crown or Parliament of Great Britain."2 Virginia thus became the first colony to actually propose a resolu- tion for separation from the British Empire. An understanding of the forces behind this shift in allegiance is valuable for an assessment of the meaning of the American Revolution, both in Virginia and the colonies as a whole. Some distinction must be made between the causes of resistance to the British government and the arguments for independence. The causes which set off resistance to imperial authority were not necessarily strong enouglh to generate a desire for independence. In the initial phase of the revolutionary movement the colonists denied any intention of seeking to establish an independent nation. It was expected that an accommodation with England could be achieved without resort to the ultimate device of separation. In the end the arguments for the Revolution would become those for inde- pendence, but some change had to take place in the minds of Virginians before this union of ideas could occur. The distinction between revolution and independence loses its meaning if independence had been an underlying goal from the beginning of the Revolution. Some contemporary observers thought they saw a desire for independence early in the struggle. Lord Dunmore, Virginia's last royal governor, "no longer doubted" that the Virginia Patriots were seeking separation in May I775. Thomas Jefferson, foremost among the radicals, had urged in 1774 that the King restore peace because no one in America wanted to separate from England.4 Jefferson's statement could be taken as a veiled threat of independence if colonial demands were not met. Even the Virginia Convention's pledge of loyalty ended by hinting that the relation between crown and colony had changed. This evidence is circumstantial and some of it hostile, and the fact remains that few prominent colonists publicly advocated independence until 1776.5 As late as February I776 a contract for supplies for the Virginia militia provided that "in case of a discontinuance of the war" Virginia would still take the provisions, indicat- ing that the colony's leaders had not yet abandoned all hope of a com- promise.6 It is therefore reasonably safe to assume that the movement for separation can be explained to some degree in terms other than those used to explain the Revolution. A major theme running through all the arguments for independence was that the colonists were forced to that extreme by England. In one way or another, almost every argument for separation was geared to showing that independence was the result of British, not American, actions. Many vari- ations of the "force" idea were used by Virginians to convince others and, perhaps, themselves of the necessity of severing the English connection. The most common variation was that Great Britain either must recognize the rights of Americans, or those rights would have to be preserved by an independent America. A hint of such a thought was expressed by the Fincastle Committee of Safety in January of I775. After stressing its loyalty to the King, the committee stated that it would surrender its liberties onl5y "at the expense of our lives."' Jefferson was more explicit in August.
