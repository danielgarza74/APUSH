England, the Caribbean, and the Settlement of Carolina
Richard Waterhouse

JOURNAL Journal of American Studies 9, no. 3 (1975)
EXCERPT Original journal pages 259-60
JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/27553189
Selection Overview

Waterhouse notes that historians have traced Barbadian plans to settle Carolina but have neglected the social backgrounds of settlers who came directly from England in the colony's first decades, and he examines both groups, including the Barbadian-born leaders of the early elite.

Alfred D. Chandler, Wesley Frank Craven, Charles M. Andrews, Hope Frances Kane, Adelaide B. Helwig, Edward McCrady and John P. Thomas have described how Barbadians initiated plans to settle in Carolina as early as 1663, plans which culminated in the! short lived settlement at Cape Fear in 1665.3 Both Chandler and Richard S. Dunn have identified the social classes in Barbados from which some of South Carolina's earliest settlers were drawn.4 However, although the aforemen tioned studies have added to our knowledge of some aspects of the Barba dians' role in the colonization of Carolina none of them has provided a com prehensive account of that role. It is the purpose of this paper, first, to examine the social backgrounds of settlers who emigrated directly from England to South Carolina during the first two decades of the colony's settlement; second, to suggest reasons that explain the Barbadians' attempts to organize settlements in Carolina during the course of the 1660s; third, to determine the motives of the Barbadians, not only from the wealthy planter classes but from other Caribbean social backgrounds as well, in settling in Carolina after 1670; fourth, to propose the implications which the nature of the early immigration into South Carolina, both from England and the Caribbean, held for the later social development of the colony, especially as it related to the formation of a colonial ?lite.
