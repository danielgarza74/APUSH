Selection Overview

Sosin notes that twentieth-century historians reject the old charge that Lord Dunmore and British Indian department officers conspired to embroil Virginians in an Indian war, seeing it instead as an effort by Virginia land speculators and frontier leaders to force the Shawnee to accept white occupation of the Ohio’s left bank. He argues that the Indian department did play a role, but worked to prevent a general Indian confederation and so helped the Virginians succeed against the Shawnee.

But they did not conspire with Dunmore to embroil the colonists in a war; rather they worked to save them from an expanded conflict against a general Indian confederation. Indeed, their efforts were indispensable to the very success of the Virginians against the Shawnees and vital for the protection of th Virginia frontier and the frontiers of neighboring colonies to the north and south. To understand and appreciate fully the service rendered by British officials to the frontiersmen, it is necessary to consider the expansion of the frontier from the Susquehanna River in Pennsylvania south to the Savannah River in Georgia; and the reaction of the Indians, including the Six Nations of New York, the Mingo, Delaware, and Shawnee of the upper Ohio Valley, and the Cherokee and Creek of the South. The British officials of the Indian department were themselves operating under a policy regulating expansion of the frontier and white-Indian relations adopted by the royal government during and immediately after the French and Indian War. To maintain the peace and security of the frontier and provide orderly expansion, the government had sought to guarantee the Indian lands against white encroachment until the lands had been legally purchased and the natives fully compensated. The Proclamation of I763 temporarily set aside the region west of the Appalachian Mountains as an Indian reserve. White settlers, particularly from Virginia, violated the boundary line, however, by establishing themselves along the Monongahela River and its tributaries. The resentment of the Indians was aroused. Recognizing the need to placate the natives and to accommodate the expanding white settle- ments, the superintendents of the Indian department negotiated new boundary lines. In November I 768 Sir William Johnson obtained from the Six Nations a cession of land lying east and south of the Mohawk and Ohio rivers and extending as far west as the Tennessee River. That same year Johnson's counterpart in the southern district, John Stuart, at Hard Labor in South Carolina negotiated a boundary line with the Cherokee and Creek tribes. This boundary ran from Tryon's Mountain in South Carolina to Chiswell's lead mines on New River, a tributary of the Kanawha, and from there to the confluence of the Kanawha with the Ohio. The treaty of Hard Labor did not satisfy the Virginia expansionists since it did not clear the region south and west of the Kanawha and north and east of the Tennessee of Cherokee title.
