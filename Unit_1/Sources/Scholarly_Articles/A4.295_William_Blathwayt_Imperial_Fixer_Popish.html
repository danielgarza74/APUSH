Stephen Saunders Webb

EXCERPT Original journal pages 3-4
JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/1920803
Selection Overview

Webb argues that William Blathwayt, who by 1687 held a cluster of posts including secretary at war, clerk of the Council, and auditor-general of the colonial revenues, is better seen as an integrated imperial "fixer" than as the holder of separate offices, a habit of study that obscures his bureaucratic skills. He notes that Blathwayt kept his hold on colonial affairs through the Revolution of 1688-89 and went on to twenty-seven more years of colonial dominance.

The diarist would not be surprised at Blathwayt's future service as secretary of state to William III or his decade of dominance at the Board of Trade. Translate William Blathwayt's responsibilities into contemporary terms. He was minister of defense; secretary to the cabinet; permanent undersecretary of state in the colonial office; member of Parliament on the government front bench; auditor of imperial accounts; and, subsequently, acting secretary of state for Europe and America and the leading member of the expert, standing commission for the export trade and commonwealth. Subtract the distortion of such a translation and still there remains an impression of Blathwayt's enormous responsibilities and power. This impression has been muted naturally by time and artificially by the scholarly habit of considering Blathwayt's roles individually. So, not any one office, but all, operating in an imperial context, best exhibit the bureaucratic Blathwayt. All his offices helped Blathwayt make colonial government paramilitary. His importance is bound up with these two points. One: colonial ad- ministration was not confined to the colonial office; rather, the plural re- sponsibilities of such men as Blathwayt linked plantation office, privy council, treasury, war office, admiralty, ordnance office, customs and trans- port commissioners into the hybrid today termed "colonial administra- tion." Two: the government of England's empire, even more than that of the mother country, was military, and the particular responsibility of the man who headed the war and plantation offices. The governor generals of the royal colonies were the agents, and ob- jects, of Blathwayt's fixing. Their relationship has been neglected, in part because the correspondence between the omnicompetent Mr. Blathwayt and the viceroys was for two and a quarter centuries in private hands, until it surfaced in Williamsburg, Virginia, after World War II. Neglect of both secretary and governors also is a reflection of the crippling pre- occupation of historians of the old empire with "mercantilism," a dated and narrow concept of commercial determinism, which has excluded from due consideration both the actual rulers of the empire and their ideas of government. Utilizing the Blathwayt collections both in London and Williamsburg, this essay reassesses the importance of "the Secretary of War and Plantations" for an emergent empire.' Nephew and protege of Thomas Povey, England's first colonial civil servant, Blathwayt, even in his initial posts, underwrote an imperial ca- reer.
