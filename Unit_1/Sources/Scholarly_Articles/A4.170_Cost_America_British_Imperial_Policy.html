MCCLELLAND Harvard University I The American Revolution-what Adam Smith preferred to speak of as "the late disturbances in the colonies"-has long remained a central puzzle of American historiography. In the maelstrom of events that led to Lexington and Concord the role of economic factors is still a subject of dispute. That Britain repeatedly sought to regulate production and trade within the colonies can easily be documented, as can the resentment that such regulations evoked throughout her American posses- sions. What remains obscure is the motiva- tion for colonial indignation. WVas British interference resented primarily because it jeopardized coloniial freedoms or because it jeopardized colonial pocketbooks? Essential to resolving such a dispute is some indication of the actual costs in- volved. Historians have repeatedly assailed the problem by listing all relevant imperial policies, noting whether the tendencies of each was to help or to hinder colonial development, and then-given that list of tendencies-reaching some vague conclu- sion on the net impact of all combined. Not surprisingly those conclusions have varied widely. Curtis Nettels, for example, asserts that imperial restraints were "re- strictive, injurious, negative"' while Oliver Dickerson claims that they were not.2 The solution to such a dispute is obvious enough. What must be assessed is not just the directional impact of each policy, but the dollars and cents involved. Only one aspect of British imperial policy has been subjected to cost-benefit analysis of this sort. In 1939-and more recently in 1965--attempts were made to measure the costs to the colonies of trade route distortions attributable to the Navi- gation Acts.3 Here, too, the dispute con- tinues unresolved. The estimates of Law- rence Harper imply that British imperial policy placed "a heavy burden upon the colonies"4 while those of Robert Thomas suggest the absence of any "significant hardship."5 This article has a twofold purpose. The first is to show that the conceptual frame- work of both Thomas and Harper is so defective that their final results are vir- tually meaningless. The second is to offer an alternative upper-bound measure of the cost to America of the British Acts of Trade. Before the cost of British domination can be appraised, the alternative to such domination must be clarified.
