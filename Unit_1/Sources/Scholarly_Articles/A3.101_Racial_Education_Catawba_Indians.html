James H. Merrell

EXCERPT Original journal pages 363-364
JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/2208567
Selection Overview

Merrell begins with two 1751–52 incidents, one in which the Catawbas drove off a Black trader and another in which they accepted a free Black man who interpreted for them, to argue that Catawba–Black relations cannot be explained by a simple, long-standing Catawba hostility toward Black people. He examines how Catawba attitudes toward Africans and African Americans took shape in the eighteenth-century Southeast.

The Racial Education of the Catawba Indians BY JAMES H. MERRELL IN JANUARY 175 2 THE COLONIAL TRADER MATTHEW TOOLE SENT AN urgent letter to James Glen, governor of the province of South Caro- lina. Writing from the Catawbas, a small "Nation" of Indians living in the Piedmont some 175 miles northwest of Charleston, Toole warned that his native hosts were unhappy about a "Negro Fellow" who had recently arrived there to trade. "The King and what Head Men there is in the Nation at Present is very angry that a Negro should trade here," Toole reported, "and has told him to be gone with his Goods . . .but he will not which gives them a great Deal of Uneasiness." Two months before he learned of this confrontation, Governor Glen was busy arranging for goods to be shipped to the Catawbas. Distribution of the merchandise among the two thousand or so inhab- itants of the Nation required the aid of someone who could speak the Indians' language. "[I]f no body comes that can interpret . . . ," Glen advised his agent in the area, call upon the "free Negro that lives among the Catawbas, and is received by them as a Catawba, that Speaks both their Language and English, very well. ...2 These two incidents preclude simple generalizations about the character of Catawba-black relations. While recent interest in the problem has increased scholarly awareness of the extent and the importance of these contacts (espe- cially in the Southeast), most students prefer anecdote to analysis or focus on white racial policies rather than on the Indians and blacks themselves. As a result, we still know little beyond the fact that some Indians were friendly toward blacks, others were hostile, and still others shifted from friendship to hostility over time. Beyond offering theories about innate antagonism, mutual attraction based on cultural affinities or a common plight, and the ability of whites to manipulate Indian-black relations, few have sought to account for these different patterns of behavior. Even fewer have entertained the possibility that Indians could be friendly to some blacks and hostile to others.' Explanation of the Catawbas' apparently contradictory actions in the winter of 1751-1752 and their avowed hatred of blacks in more recent times does not lie in vague theories about natural affinity or "hereditary antipathy." Nor can it be found by cataloging contacts or analyzing white attitudes and policies. In order to understand the origins and development of this relationship it is necessary to venture into the cultural frontier where Indian met African. The predictable paucity of evidence for contacts between two nonliterate peoples makes this journey perilous, and any conclusions must remain specu- lative. Nonetheless, extant sources do offer a glimpse of the Catawba-black frontier, permitting us to reconstruct the changing context of intercultural relations in the Carolina Piedmont.
