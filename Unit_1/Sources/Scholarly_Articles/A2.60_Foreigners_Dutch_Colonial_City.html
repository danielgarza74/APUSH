Selection Overview

Goodfriend argues that, although historians usually assume New Amsterdam's non-Dutch residents merged seamlessly into Dutch society, the city's English kept thinking of themselves, and were regarded by the Dutch, as members of the English nation. She traces how that cultural distance hardened once questions of identity became tied to political loyalty in the First Anglo-Dutch War.

Foreigners in a Dutch Colonial City Joyce D. Goodfriend, University of Denver, Colorado In his 1902 book New Amsterdam and its People, J. H. Innes devoted a chapter to the "English quarter" of the Dutch colonial city, painstak ingly assembling profiles of a series of residents with English roots.1 Innes's identification of the English as a distinct subset of the city's population has not been capitalized on by later generations of historians who, though taking New Amsterdam's heterogeneity as axiomatic, have not examined the intercultural dynamics of this fledgling society in a systematic way. Explicitly or implicitly, historians contend that the high proportion of non-Dutch in the city's population seamlessly merged into society and became culturally Dutch. This line of interpretation, though appealing, simplifies an intricate process of social and cultural negotia tion that was conducted on the local level, but unfolded in the shadow of circumstances in the seventeenth-centurv Atlantic world. In this article, I problematize the integration of European immi grants in New Amsterdam society by asking whether coexistence ineluc tably led to the absorption of foreigners into the Dutch population. I argue that participation in the city's economic and civic life was not enough to set non-Dutch Protestants on the path toward incorporation into the Dutch majority in the case of the city's English who, despite their decades-long associations with the Dutch citizenry, continued to consider themselves members of the English nation and to be regarded as such by the Dutch. Though lacking an independent ecclesiastical base that might have fostered a sense of separateness, New Amsterdam's English still maintained cultural distance from the Dutch, especially after questions of identity became enveloped in matters of political loy alty in the First Anglo-Dutch War. J. H. Innés, New Amsterdam and Its People (New York, 1902). 242 ■ NEW YORK HISTORY The first part of the article reviews the genesis of New Amsterdam's multiethnic society and elucidates the problems resulting from cultural differences. The next section documents the ways that authorities facili tated coexistence in the city and forms the prelude to a comparative analysis of the adaptive strategies of the German, French, and English immigrants to the city. The article concludes with an in-depth examina tion of the case of New Amsterdam's English against the backdrop of Anglo-Dutch relations in the mid-seventeenth century In 1661 Petrus Stuyvesant wrote that New Netherland was "only gradually and slowly peopled by the scrapings of all sorts of nationalities."2 A more celebratory tone infused the language of a Jesuit who visited New Amsterdam in 1646 and received a briefing from Stuyvesant's predecessor, Willem Kieft. "On the island of Manhate, and its environs, there may well be four or five hundred men of different sects and nations: the Director General told me that there were men of eighteen different languages."3 However one construed Manhattan's demography, there was no mistaking the high proportion of non Dutch in the city's population. From its earliest days, a diverse array of Europeans could be found in New Amsterdam along with substantial numbers of West Africans, most of them enslaved, as well as Indians who came to trade or on diplomatic errands.4 With a pressing need to recruit laborers for New Netherland and the reluctance of Netherlanders to transplant themselves overseas, the Dutch West India Company could not afford to exclude prospective immigrants on the basis of their national origins.
