Selection Overview

Thomas argues against the long-standing legend that George III’s attempt at tyranny caused the American Revolution. Examining the years 1763–1775, he concludes that the King did not decide and dictate policy but acted at his ministers’ request, often accepted their more moderate lines, and was caught up in the escalation of events, so that the evidence tends to exonerate him of real personal responsibility.

GEORGE III AND THE AMERICAN REVOLUTION P.D.G. THOMAS University College of Wales, Aberystwyth George III is probably best remembered as the King who lost Britain her American colonies. His personal reputation has especially suffered because of the legend that it was his attempt at tyranny that was the root cause of the American Revolution, a legend invented by contemporaries and for long endorsed by historians in both Britain and America. The Declaration of Independence was a piece of propaganda, a recital of the alleged political sins of George III in order to justify the renunciation of his sovereignty, ending with this verdict: A prince, whose character is thus marked by every act which may define a tyrant, is unfit to be the ruler of a free people. Thereafter George III was depicted in the United States as a ruler determined to crush the political aspirations of his colonial subjects, with the Declaration cited as evidence of this supposed historical fact. He had an equally poor press in Britain: in the long-fashionable Whig interpretation of history, where British history was seen as a triumphant march towards parliamentary democracy, George III was portrayed as the villain par excellence, a would-be autocrat seeking to turn back the constitutional clock. It is easy nowadays to forget this picture, as given in G.M. Trevelyan's classic History of England, a work still in print. The author describes 'the attempt of George III to recover the powers of the Crown'; and, when this was achieved, the result was the American Revolution. 'And so things drifted on to the catastrophe. On one side the unbending stubbornness of George III...'1 Recent British scholarship, particularly in the last decade or so, has transformed the whole picture of the King, above all in the masterly biography by John Brooke. George Ill's attitude to the colonies is there seen in the light of modern research and opinion, but put in rather general terms.2 The aim of this paper is to look at the years from the outbreak of peace in 1763 to the outbreak of war in 1775, to examine for this crucial period of the coming of the American Revolution the King's views on the American crisis during its various stages, and to ascertain as far as possible his actual influence on policy. There is no simple answer to these questions. The evidence is inadequate, and sometimes even misleading. A letter from the King to the Prime Minister, read by itself, might seem a policy directive; and then it turns out to be merely an answer expressing royal approval of a decision already made by the cabinet. His ministers kept him fully informed, both of news from America and of their own ideas and decisions. When the former Governor of Massachusetts, Thomas Hutchinson, had a long interview with George III on 1 July 1774 he was astonished at the King's detailed knowledge of America, and wrongly concluded that he was 'more his own Minister than is generally imagined'.5 George Ill's customary practice was in fact described to Hutchinson himself on 17 February 1775 by Lord Hillsborough, who had been the first Secretary of State for America, between 1768 and 1772.6 The K[ing] himself (he said to me) thinks as you do; but always will leave his own sentiments, and conform to his Ministers, though he will argue with them, and very sensibly; but if they adhere to their own opinion, he will say, 'Well. Do you choose it should be so? Then let it be'. And sometimes he has known him add, 'You must take the blame upon yourself. Nor did George III like to employ another method of royal influence, that directly on parliamentary voting. Many members of both the House of Lords and the House of Commons were willing to take their cue from the sovereign. At the height of the Stamp Act Crisis, in February 1766, when there was some doubt as to the King's views, one informed observer noted the consequent uncertainty: 'many who wish to know the King's secret wishes and act accordingly, are quite puzzled what to believe'.7 Yet it was with difficulty that even various ministers did sometimes persuade the King to influence parliamentary voting in favour of their policies. Until recently, historians have often approached the question of George Ill's political influence from the wrong angle, from the unfounded assumption that he was a man who usually had definite opinions on policies. This was not the case. George III certainly had the objective of restoring the Crown's power of appointing ministers. But, having chosen them, he let them govern. He often had no positive views on policy at all, and this was evidently so at first on the issue of the relationship of Britain with her American colonies. But that the King's views on America hardened with the passage of time, especially just before and during the War of Independence, has caused historians to read back into the earlier years of the colonial crisis a royal hard-line attitude that did not then exist.
