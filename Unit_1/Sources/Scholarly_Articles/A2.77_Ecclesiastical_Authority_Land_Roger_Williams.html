Selection Overview

James examines how churches in Rhode Island, whose petitioner John Clarke in 1662 promised a "livelie experiment" in soul liberty, held together without help from the state. He concludes that the founders' zeal for the one true church cooled, that theology gave way to heredity, taste, or social aspirations as grounds of affiliation, and that dissenting members could simply leave.

THE NEW ENGLAND QUARTERLY about the prospects for churches, where the brief record was by traditional standards, even less auspicious than in the political sphere. Only a few churches had formed; they were fragile, discordant, and embraced an appallingly small num- ber of souls. The experiment would be lively indeed. Perils lurked on every side. Religious schism had seemed threatening in the past; in a realm of soul liberty it could be unchecked. Cut adrift from the state, the church might well be unable to take religion to a whole community or even to preserve its own unity. Any individual could defy or ignore a church, whether on doctrine or a judgment on lapses from Christian conduct, without fear of punishment by the secular arm. No law would require everyone to attend public worship or provide religious education for the children. The conse- quences could be dangerous for civil society as well, because most Christians had for many years agreed that a common faith was necessary for the collective welfare and that the or- ganizations of church and state were inextricably tied. Both had to be coercive and monopolistic. In Christian tradition, both derived their authority and the essence of their laws from God. Church and state had proclaimed a need to work in part- nership. They had ordinarily used similar structures of or- ganization. James I summed up centuries of thought and ex- perience in the dictum, "No bishop, no king." Dissolve the partnership, destroy the likeness between the structures of church and state, and the effects might be catastrophic. Clarke's earnest petition dodged such difficult issues by fail- ing to define "true pietye." The term implied a single mode, though he proposed to allow diversity without limit. He en- dorsed neither a universal piety nor a piety exhibited by the elect and not counterfeited by others. For most people in the seventeenth century, however, piety still implied an embrace of the true ecclesiastical organization and submission to its authority as well as participation in its ceremonies.
