The Maryland Tradition Thomas W. Spalding, C.F.X. Less than a year after that strange new configuration across the Atlantic was welcomed into the community of nations by the Treaty of Paris of 1783, John Carroll composed his most ambitious literary piece: An Address to the Roman Catholics ofthe United States of America. It was a rebuttal of an apologia by a former Jesuit and cousin, Charles Henry Wharton, who had justified his conver sion to the Protestant Episcopal Church by an exposition of Catholic doctrines Carroll considered prejudicial to American Catholics. Even the hope of vindicat ing the faith, Carroll concluded, "should not have induced me to engage in con troversy, if I could fear that it would disturb the harmony now subsisting amongst all christians in this country, so blessed with civil and religious liberty; which if we have the wisdom and temper to preserve, America may come to exhibit a proof to the world, that general and equal toleration, by giving a free circulation to fair argument, is the most effectual method to bring all denominations of christians to an unity of faith."1 The United States of America was the product of a revolution that could have been duplicated in no other part of the world, a revolution that was itself the product of a colonial maturation unmatched in the story of imperialism and the generator of a political order truly unique for its age.2 Carroll was aware not only of the extraordinary character of that revolution but also of its profound effect upon religion. "[I]n these United States," he had instructed a member of the Roman curia two months after the Treaty of Paris, "our Religious system has undergone a revolution, if possible, more extraordinary, than our political one," which represented, according to Carroll, "a blessing and advantage, which [it] is our duty to preserve & improve."3 The product of the American Revolution with regard to religion that was truly 1. 52 U.S. Catholic Historian unique and radical was the neutral state that denied itself any control of religious belief or polity. The neutral state, however, was a concept as old as the plan of colonization of George Calvert, first Lord Baltimore, who in 1632 had envisioned Protestants and Catholics living in peaceful communion on the Potomac, free to believe and to live their respective theologies as they saw fit. John Carroll never doubted that freedom of conscience?embodied in the Maryland Act of Toleration as early as 1649-?and the concommitant separation of church and state it presup posed were the principles that served best his church in the new republic. The unwavering adherence of Maryland Catholics to these principles was reinforced by memories of the hardships and humiliations of the penal years, when, as Carroll put it, they had "smarted under the lash of an established church."4 More than that, Carroll was convinced that they were principles that offered the greatest hope of survival and progress to the universal church of the future. Throughout his life he played a missionary role, offering his "proof to the world" of their efficacy. To Leonardo Antonelli, cardinal prefect of the Congregation of the Propaganda Fide, he wrote in 1785, the year after he was named superior of the American mission: "Indeed, I truly believe that such solid foundations of Religion can be laid in these American States, that the most flourishing portion of the Church, with great comfort to the Holy See, may one day be found here."5 To Lord Robert Petre, a leading spokesman ofthe English laity, Carroll wrote soon after his elevation to the episcopacy in 1790: "The daily advantages arising to America from this policy [of equal rights to the professors of all religions] should be a lesson to Britain, which in other instances of law, government, trade &c furnishes so many useful instructions to us."6 To Archbishop John Thomas Troy of Dublin Carroll boasted in 1808, the year he was raised to archiepiscopal rank: "Our Executive never interferes in the discipline, doctrine, or regulations of any religious society."7 And in the last year of his life he advised his friend Charles Plowden, the English Jesuit, that "liberty of opinion" should be maintained on the question of a royal veto of episcopal appointments until the pope had spoken and added pointedly that "the principle of this Govt, not to meddle with the doctrine or discipline ofthe various religious denominations" relieved him ofthe burden of forming an opinion on such matters of church and state.8 Pride in his native land, its government, its laws, and an almost unqualified endorsement of its democratic and liberal principles Carroll brought to the Mary land tradition, of which he was the principal architect. So ardent was his patrio tism that there was, as one historian perceived it, "a remarkable disinclination" among non-Catholic Americans "to associate the flock of John Carroll with its doomed leaders across the sea."9 Liberty of opinion, or "a free circulation to fair argument," was also a legacy ofthe American Revolution that Carroll incorporated into the Maryland tradition.
