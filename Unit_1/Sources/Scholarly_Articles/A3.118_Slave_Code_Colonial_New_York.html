Selection Overview

Olson argues that New York’s slave code grew out of the rise and decline of slavery and, because New York held more slaves than any other northern colony, became harsher than those of New England, Pennsylvania, and New Jersey, equaling southern codes in severity after 1712, although enforcement was milder than the law allowed.

THE SLAVE CODE IN COLONIAL NEW YORK The body of laws governing the slaves in New York was the product of fluctuating development reflecting within it- self the rise and decline of the institution. During the early years after 1664 when slaves were comparatively few, their regulation rested primarily in the hands of their owners. From 1684 to 1702 a few restrictive laws were passed where- in slaves were legally classified with indentured servants. The enactment in 1702 of the "Act for Regulating Slaves" placed them in a legal category of their own, and marked the initial legislative effort to formulate a comprehensive code for their control. A vast expansion of it almost inevitably followed in 1712 as an aftermath of the delirium of fear en- gendered by the Negro insurrection of that year. The threat of a similar plot opened the way to the restatement and strengthening of the regulatory laws in 1730. The gen- eral principles of this code, reinforced from time to time by individual laws, remained the bulwark of slave control throughout the colonial period. The rising tide of anti-slavery sentiment in the post- Revolutionary period as expressed in the demand for liber- alization of existing slave laws, anid the remedy of slave abuses peculiar to the times, led to the first state-enacted code of 1788. Following the passage of the bill for gradual abolition, a decade later, the legal development of slave reg- ulation entered into its final phase with the passage of the general statutes of 1801 and 1817, which served chiefly to cushion the institution as it faded out of existence. Inasmuch as the slave population of New York was great- er than that of any other northern colony, its slave code was correspondingly harsher than those of the New England colonies, Pennsylvania, and New Jersey. The small scale of slaveholding tended to encourage a laxness of discipline on the part of masters which had to be overcome by rigid control. Moreover, the concentration in New York City of many slaves who were amenable to the influence of vicious forces served to tighten restrictions on slaves, particularly after the outbreak of 1712. However, the enforcement of the slave code was milder than its legal possibilities might lead one to imragine. The laws of New York accorded to the slave the dual status of property and personality. The formal recognition of the property element is to be found in the prelimi ary section of the 1702 law concerning petty thefts committed by slaves which read "Since slaves are the property of Christians, etc. "1 Of judicial pronouncements on the chattel aspect of slavery the most noteworthy was that of Justice Yates of the New York Supreme Court in the case of Conck- lin vs Havens, 1815. Yates held that slaves "are consid- ered, on questions in relation to the right of property in them, as goods and chattels, and consequently, such ques- tions must be decided by the same legal principles as are applicable to that sort of property."2 As a chattel it was illegal for a slave to make contracts to buy and sell of his own accord. He could be bought, sold, and taxed as property, and bequeathed and inherited by will. He could not testify against any freeman, white or black. His right to marry and to take property was not legalized until 1809. The attributes of personality inherent in the slave re- ceived legal sanction in various forms. One of the earliest of the slave statutes, that of 1706, encouraged the baptism of Negro, Indian and Mulatto slaves.
