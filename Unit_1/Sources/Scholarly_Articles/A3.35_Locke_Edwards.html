FROM LOCKE TO EDWARDS BY EDWARD H. DAVIDSON As early as 1669 Locke drafted "The Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina," a body of laws for the new colony.1 Throughout his life he collected and read widely in all sorts of books on westward explora- tion and settlement; and the Essay Concerning Human Understand- ing, both in its early, synoptic version 2 and in its final edition, shows that Locke may well have been the first philosopher, if not the first European citizen of international acclaim in the XVIIth century, whose thought had been deeply affected by the travel narratives, the legends of the Indian aborigines, and even the unblushing fabrications which came from America. The importance of this influence is not that the pages of the Essay are dotted with references to this reading, or that Locke came to have an enlarged scope for the geography of his mind wider than that of Bacon and Hobbes, both of whom were virtually untouched by the New World. The importance is, rather, that Locke's theory of the mind was undoubtedly the first which had a "modern" relevance to anthropology and myth. Locke saw the human mind as lodged within a vast, undefined amplitude of history-not the history of warring nations, of commercial loss and gain, or even of logic and principles, but the history of human consciousness itself. The human mind has come down an endless way from its primordial beginnings; it is not yet closed or finished; it may suffer from thinking of itself too grandly (and Locke sought to chart the limits and boundaries of the mind); yet it is all the while in some active participation with that realm of matter all around it. The New World opened for Locke the perspec- tives of the mind's history, even of its pre-history, because he saw in the superstitions and in the "antiquities" of America the first hints of the emergence of human consciousness and the first signs of what that consciousness would afterward become. Very early in his reading Locke recognized that the history of Europe from antiquity to the present could not be a measure by which to reckon the history of the New World. The first records of ancient times, whether stained with the blood of Old Testament venge- ance or marred by the havoc of tribal wars, were the narratives of men who had already acquired conscious thought. Locke was well aware that the bards from Homer to Vergil had long ceased to believe in the caprices of the gods whose lives they told; they knew that the gods were credited only by the ignorant and the foolish. By contrast, the folktales of Indians, the accounts of Aztecs and Mexicans, the historical narratives of the savages on the North Amer- ican mainland were so marked by childlike trust and vested with the colors of superstition that one might well consign them to that long- ago detritus of human thought which civilized man had abandoned about the time of the Stone Age. Locke perceived, however, that these legends and superstitions were not false; rather they were the first surviving evidence civilized man had so far found which clearly showed a way of thinking before Homer catalogued the gods and Joshua blew down the walls of Jericho with seven blasts from trumpets. Locke realized that the American Indians were not so much "sav- age" as they were human beings still living in the childhood of the human race: they were survivors, through an uninterrupted line of descent, of the psychic history of mankind before rational thought had taken place. The Peruvian, the Hottentot are "ourselves in our beginning." Civilized man is separated from the American aborigine not simply by two or five thousand years of differing climate and be- havior but by having somehow acquired what is unknown to primitive man, namely a critical, analytical intelligence. If it were interpreted aright, the evidence of the Americas might very well fill the gaps in the psychic history of civilized man who has for centuries been baffled by his seemingly unbridgeable remoteness from his ancient ancestors. The recently opened lands far to the west offered Locke the first evidence he or any man had so far found by which the prehistoric past and the outlines of man's thought might be drawn. His aim was, ac- cordingly, to answer on the plenitude of new evidence the important question: Why does the human mind conform to the shape of its geo- graphical location? Is the mind condemned to think only within its racial and natural boundaries, or is it free to move along the lines of its own special cognition? The explorations of the New World sug- gested that the Indians "contented themselves with the opinions, fashions, and things of their country as they found them, without looking any further." Locke drew a careful difference between "the Virginia king Apochancana" and an educated Englishman: "the dif- ference" was that the Indian's "exercise of his faculties was bounded within the ways, modes, and motions of his own country, and never directed to any other or further inquiries" whereas the Englishman was, even before birth, free of that naturalistic limitation.
