Law in the Colonial South By Warren M. Billings Who governed? And by what warrant did governors rule? The English who founded Jamestown in 1607, along with every other European who colonized the southern portions of North America, con fronted those fundamental questions whenever founder and settler alike wrestled with ways of devising "Just laws for the happy guiding and governing of the people there inhabiting."1 This quest for "Just laws" fits hand in glove with the creation of entire legal architectures that gave figure and adornment to the provincial societies that sprang up between the 1560s and the 1750s from the Chesapeake Bay to Florida and the Gulf Coast. Those designs, their differences from colony to colony, the inspirations that undergirded them, the way they worked? in short, the manner of their fashioning and by whom?were prominent features in the South's beginnings, but they are obscure subjects.2 What accounts for the obscurity? To explain it, some might point to the impositions of a documentary record thinned by losses from neglect and war and whose mastery demands adeptness with earlier forms of English, French, and Spanish; antique handwriting; and command of an arcane nomenclature of ancient legal vocabularies. There is no gainsaying that such evidentiary impedimenta pose formidable ob stacles, although they have never proved much of a deterrent to deter mined researchers. The existing historiography testifies as much. 604 THE JOURNAL OF SOUTHERN HISTORY Doing the history of law in the colonial South has always been a sometime enterprise marked by historians who dropped in, tarried for a time, and then went elsewhere. Gentlemen-scholars?antebellum dabblers who engaged in historical investigation as a sideline to their more remunerative callings as lawyers, physicians, or politicians? pioneered the field. Some of them championed preserving ancient records or founding historical societies and state libraries as havens of safekeeping for treasured colonial documents. All acted upon common assumptions that blended streaks of antiquarianism with dashes of Whiggishness and Anglocentrism. Insofar as they ascribed meaning or purpose to history, gentlemen-scholars regarded the colonial era as being mainly about the crossing of Old World civilizations to the South, and nowhere was that passage and its consequences more clearly evident than in the sprouting of European law across the region. Likewise, the gentlemen-scholars believed that contemplation of the South's legal past yielded utilitarian insights into the inexorable ad vance of human progress in their day and in the future. Shared postu lates did not translate into any disposition to examine that past in a concerted, systematic way, however. Instead, gentlemen-scholars op erated independently of one another, and the aggregate of their efforts was a hodgepodge of statutory editions, judicial biographies, and ex plorations of doctrine, jurisprudence, and the rise of courts.3 Descent into civil war in 1861 abruptly halted their dipping into legal history and visited colossal destruction on southern colonial ar chives. As southerners accommodated to a world without slavery, a new group of gentlemen-scholars dropped in. Its members rose to maturity during Reconstruction and were active intermittently between the 1870s and the 1930s. In outlook, they accepted the intellectual suppositions of their forebears but added novel twists of their own. Members of the postbellum generation took a more pronounced insti tutional approach that explicitly deemed legal things as an important facet of southern culture, one that defined and was defined by colonial societies.
