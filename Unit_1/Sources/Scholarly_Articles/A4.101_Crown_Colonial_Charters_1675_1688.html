Selection Overview

Haffenden argues that James II aimed to merge the remaining independent colonial governments into larger viceroyalties, but that lack of departmental coordination, delay, and shifting purposes meant the scheme was only partly realized.

THE GROWN AND THE COLONIAL CHARTERS 453 problems occasioned by a change of monarch should temporarily thrust aside the plans for the prosecution of the charters. But the effect of the former was unduly great because of a rebellion in the West of England led by the Duke of Monmouth and a rising in Scotland under Argyle. These two major disturbances, and the work of suppression that followed, hindered the initiation and then the development of legal proceedings against the outstanding colonial governments: Pennsylvania, Maryland, Rhode Island, Connecticut, the Jerseys, and the Carolinas. The first to be considered in the new reign were the colonies of Rhode Island and Connecticut. Edward Randolph had been ordered to prepare articles of indictment against both of them as early as May i685 in pursuance of the policy determined upon the previous fall? Before these articles were ready, however, the attention of the Lords was directed to the unsatisfactory manner in which the administration of Maryland was being conducted. Against Maryland, as against the New England govern- ments, prosecution had been under consideration in the weeks immedi- ately preceding the death of Charles IIV It is clear that by the summer the former threads of policy were being gathered together afresh. From Maryland news had arrived of the murder of Customs Collector Rousby and of the hindrance to which royal customs officials were still being subjected by Lord Baltimore's government. The letter from the comptroller of Maryland telling of this was read at a meeting of the Privy Council on July io, and such was the temper of the new king that initia- tion of legal process against the colony was at once ordered. As justifica- tion the Crown claimed that there had been "divers irregular proceedings . . . to the prejudice of his Majesty's Revenue . . . and obstruction of the Publique Justice in that Colony."4 Against Rhode Island and Connecticut, the following week, charges were leveled that the northerly colonies had made laws contrary to those of England and had imposed illegal fines on their inhabitants.
