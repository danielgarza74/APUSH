The Anglican Reaction to the Great Awakening
Gerald J. Goodwin
 
ARTICLE The Anglican Reaction to the Great Awakening

EXCERPT Original journal pages 343-344
JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/42973163
Selection Overview

Goodwin treats the Awakening of 1739 to 1745 as an interdenominational revival that affected all the colonial churches and gave colonists a common social experience. He argues that the Church of England stood as the one major church hostile to it, and that this opposition limited the revival's influence and left Anglicanism a minority church in America.

The evangelical Awakening1 re-invigorated the dissenting churches, stimulated personal piety, and kindled intense religious excitement among crowds of people in all the colonies. Revivalism, because it did not depend on creedal and liturgical forms was interdenominational and affected all the colonial churches.2 As the first popular mass movement in America, the Great Awakening gave the colonists a common social experience which formed a unifying cultural bond.8 The colonial Church of England distinguished itself by standing as the one major church hostile to the Great Awakening.4 Among Congregationalists and Presbyterians, the largest denominations swept up in the revivals, there arose critics and opposers. But the Church of England remained the most potent and unified anti-revivalist organization. Although dissenters could and did argue among them- selves over whether or not the excesses of revivalism cancelled its desirable spiritual benefits, churchmen were unable to participate in such a debate. 344 HISTORICAL MAGAZINE disorderliness, and irrationalism. Where pro-revivalists discovered revitalized piety, Anglicans reported only preposterous enthusiasm. Anglican opposition to the Great Awakening shaped both the course of the revivals and the development of the Church of England in America. Anglican ministers immunized their congregations against the evangelical virus, and thus limited the Awakening's influence. This antagonism encouraged colonials to identify nonconformity with evangelicalism, and Anglicanism with anti-evangelicalism. The colonial church spurned a vital and fruitful religious impetus and kept itself from sharing one of the vibrant ingredients of the emerg- ing American culture- evangelicalism. The importance of Anglican rejection of revivalism is obvious. But the causes of this response to the Awakening have not been properly analyzed. Several inadequate assumptions have been serv- ing, explicitly or implicitly, as explanations. Apparently some assume that revivalism and Anglicanism were completely antithetical and incompatible. But, as is well known, Methodist evangelicalism de- veloped within the Church of England and was inspired and led by Anglican clergymen.5 The most influential colonial revivalist was a Church of England minister, George Whitefield. Clearly, there was nothing inevitable about Anglican opposition to the Awakening. Others might suppose that the Calvinistic theological basis of the colonial revivals made impossible participation by "Arminian" Angli- cans. But the leadership of the Wesleys demonstrates that one could be both an ardent evangelical and an Arminian. Why were there not Calvinist and Arminian wings of the movement in America, as there were in England? It has been suggested that churchmen in the colonies simply followed the lead of the majority of their fellows in England who repudiated Methodism. One church historian explained that colonial ministers opposed Whitefield because they were "acting on instructions from home/'6 Actually, as we shall see, while they certainly were influenced by churchmen at home, colonial Anglicans came to their own conclusions about Whitefield and revivalism, conclusions which must have affected English attitudes.
