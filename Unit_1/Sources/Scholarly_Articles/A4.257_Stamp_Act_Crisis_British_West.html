Selection Overview

Spindel argues that the British West Indies deserve a place in the story of the pre-Revolutionary struggle: island colonists objected to the Stamp Act for the same reasons as mainland colonists but mostly expressed discontent cautiously, with St. Kitts and Antigua the exceptions. She concludes that the islands’ geography, slave majorities, and one-crop economies narrowed their room for resistance, and that the sugar islands must be treated as an integral part of the empire.

SPINDEL Marshall University, West Virginia Traditionally, colonial scholars have focussed their attention on the North American continent, where dramatic conflicts with Britain culminated in Revolution. No major article or book has yet dealt with British West Indians as active participants in the pre-Revolutionary struggle.1 This study attempts to correct that situation. Focussing on the Stamp Act crisis, it seeks to clarify the role of island colonists during the pre-war period and argues for a comprehensive appraisal of that role. A detailed picture of the Stamp Act crisis in the British West Indies can be created.2 Although the island colonies objected to the Stamp Act for the same reasons as their northern counterparts, with one striking exception they expressed their discontent cautiously. In Jamaica and Barbados, for example, the response was moderate and the law was enforced. At St. Kitts, however, and to a lesser extent at Antigua, precisely the opposite occurred. In short, the response of each colony was unique, determined by differences of history and environment.3 Yet there are several general observations that can be made. Far more evident than in North America is the interplay between ideology and self interest. Also clear is the relationship between resistance and pressure from the mainland colonies, and the internal conflict between the desire to resist and fear of the consequences. Perhaps most important is the influence of environment - climate, geography, and demography. Taken together, these factors shed light on an intriguing question : why were West Indian views on the Stamp Act, views remarkably like those of North Americans, not generally translated into hostile behaviour? The background for the Stamp Act crisis in the Caribbean had been established in part by a century of political development which largely paralleled that of the mainland provinces.
