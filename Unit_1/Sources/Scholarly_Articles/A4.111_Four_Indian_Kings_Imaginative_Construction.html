The "Four Indian Kings" and the Imaginative Construction of the First British Empire Eric Hinderaker MPIRE is a cultural artifact as well as a geopolitical entity; it belongs to a geography of the mind as well as a geography of power. To con- struct an empire makes imaginative demands, and pays imaginative dividends, for the nation that undertakes it. Beginning in the second half of the seventeenth century, England was engaged in just such an undertaking; after i688, constitutional, administrative, and financial revolutions pushed the process rapidly forward. New experiences, images, and symbols were required to define the new regime, which, in a manner typical of imperial powers, placed steadily increasing burdens on the backs of its subjects even as it enhanced their access to material and cultural goods. An empire beyond British shores quickly emerged as a central component of the nation's new cultural identity.1 In the context of this developing culture of empire, four Native Americans with ties to the Iroquois confederacy traveled as a kind of diplomatic entourage to London in 1710. They were neither the first Indians to visit the British Isles nor the last, but in cultural impact the episode was unique. In part this was a function of the social and political authority ascribed to the Indians by calling them "kings." 488 WILLIAM AND MARY QUARTERLY Indian kings" were the first who were thought to possess the authority of rulers- and whose purposes were clearly and explicitly diplomatic. The idea of Indian kings had radical implications for Britons, who had previously tended to regard Indians as natural men living in weak, stateless societies. This new impulse to project political sovereignty onto native leaders was entirely con- sistent, however, with the desire to extend English imperial authority in America: it posited the existence of native peoples who were capable of serv- ing as effective allies and agents of the crown in the empire-building process.2 Thus the notion of Indian kings had a concrete political logic, and the presentation of these emissaries as kings became essential to the official dimension of their trip. The visit acquired lasting significance, however, in a broader cultural realm, where the Indian kings found a place in the imaginative life of Great Britain. They embodied the expanding horizons of empire for a people who were just becoming aware of their nation's imperial possibilities. As symbols of the reach and scope of English power, the Indian kings were immediately memorialized in a great range of events and stories, texts and images, all of which preserved and extended popular associations connected with the visit. In these popular contexts, the figures of the kings were ambiguous, contra- dictory, and half realized; the new view of Indians as legitimate agents of state power jostled uncomfortably with the older idea that Indians were sim- ple and savage.3 Despite the inconsistencies in the various representations of the kings, the visit stimulated a new visual and verbal language of empire and thus helped to establish the images, conceptions, and ideals that would make imperialism a powerful component of British public life for more than two centuries to come. The visit grew from a notable military failure, the aborted Canada expedi- tion of 1709. In the summer of 1708, an opportunistic young New Yorker named Samuel Vetch presented a document entitled "Canada Surveyed" to the Board of Trade in London, arguing that England should undertake the "Glorious Enterprise" of conquering the French colony.
