Selection Overview

Grant argues that historians have wrongly said Pontiac's Rebellion frightened Britain into stationing ten thousand troops in America, and wrongly described the distribution of those troops as an expensive, considerate plan. He concludes that Britain was not as alarmed over the rebellion as has been suggested and that economy and home interests were the keynotes of the 1763 troop moves.

NOTES AND DOCUMENTS 75 Pontiac's Rebellion and the British Troop Moves of 1763 BY CHARLES S. GRANT Historians generally agree that a fundamental cause of the Amer- ican Revolution was the stationing of an army of ten thousand Brit- ish regulars in the colonies. Efforts by the British to obtain a colonial revenue were supported by the argument that colonists should help defray the expense of their own protection. Efforts to collect the bill led to the Stamp Act, Townshend Duties, and so on into Revolu- tion. Although the 1763 decision to station an army in America and the manner and purposes of troop disposition would seem important in any examination of Revolutionary background, the topic has actually received casual and inaccurate treatment. There are two basic errors in the present historical record on the stationing of British troops in America. The first may be called the "Pontiac's Rebellion error." Following in each other's footsteps, such authors as George L. Beer, John Miller, John Krout, Curtis Nettels, Samuel E. Morison and Henry S. Commager, Roy F. Nichols and Jeannette P. Nichols, and many others have indicated that Pontiac helped to frighten the British into their ten-thousand- troops-for-America decision. Actually, Pontiac's Rebellion had no perceptible influence on either the original decision or in the manner of distributing troops once they had arrived in America. These au- thors frequently cite Clarence W. Alvord's MZVississippi Valley in British Politics1 as the standard work, and here they are well ad- vised, for no other secondary account approaches Alvord in meticu- lous coverage of the 1763 troop decision. 76 THE MISSISSIPPI VALLEY HISTORICAL REVIEW The second error concerns the manner of distributing troops in America. There is a tendency on the part of historians to suggest a more expensive plan, a more considerate plan, than was actually effected or even seriously considered. Here, surprisingly, Alvord is the principal offender. He based his account of troop disposition and the significance thereof on what appeared to be a marvelously illu- minating document, Plan 2.59.3 However, that plan, far from being an important document authored and later executed by Jef- frey Amherst as commander in chief in America, was more likely the "doodling" of a clerk. The plan for distributing troops was actually far less expensive than indicated by the absurd 2.59, which drew Alvord and perhaps other historians down its false scent. These misconceptions, perhaps relatively insignificant by them- selves, tend to become significant in that they operate in the same direction. Each error has suggested a more solicitous attitude toward the colonies than was actually felt by the mother country. While the correction of these errors scarcely requires an overwhelming re- vision of war-guilt appraisals, it may suggest a revised view of the balance between British solicitude and British calculated self-in- terest. Generally speaking, the errors in misinterpreting the influence of Pontiac's Rebellion are of two magnitudes. Most authors make the error of first magnitude by concluding that the rebellion was an im- portant factor in causing the decision to station an army of British regulars on American frontiers. John C. Miller typifies this group: "Fears for the safety of the British colonies were magnified by Pon- tiac's Rebellion.
