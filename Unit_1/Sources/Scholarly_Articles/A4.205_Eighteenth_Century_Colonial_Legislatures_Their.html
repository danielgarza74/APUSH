Selection Overview

Olson questions the familiar way of measuring the "rise of the colonial assembly" by the points assemblies scored against governors and councils, a measure she says reflects reliance on governors' letters and assembly journals. She proposes asking instead what local needs constituents expected legislatures to meet, and how legislators and constituents interacted.

Eighteenth- Century Colonial Legislatures and Their Constituents Alison G. Olson It has long been the custom to speak of the "rise of the colonial assembly" in the first three-quarters of the eighteenth century. Over forty years ago, Charles McLean Andrews said of the period, "the most conspicuous feature-and one that was common to nearly all the colonies-was the rise of the colonial assembly with its growth to self-conscious activity and defacto independence of royal control." Some- what more recently, Bernard Bailyn has used the same term, "rise of the assemblies," in The Origins ofAmerican Politics, as has George Dargo, in Roots of the Republic. Jack P. Greene has stated that "the rise of the representative assemblies was perhaps the most significant political and constitutional development in the history of Brit- ain's overseas empire before the American Revolution."' But what do we mean by the term rise, and how do we measure it? One meaning of the word, certainly the most popular among historians recently, has been the as- semblies' wresting power from the colonial governors and councils; using this defini- tion, one measures the rise by ticking off points the assemblymen scored against the governors: for example, voting of governors' annual salaries by the assemblies; control of major provincial appointments, including those of agents to England; dis- qualification of placemen from the assemblies; and the setting of limits on the fees charged by officeholders. The historians' emphasis on that meaning is not surprising given their reliance on such sources as the governors' letters home, which emphasized assemblies' threats to imperial authority rather than their responses to local needs, and the assembly journals, which were meant to put on record the defense of the legislatures' institu- tional position vis-a-vis the governors. What local needs did constituents ex- pect legislatures to fulfill? How well did the assemblies fulfill them? How did the legislators view their relationship to their constituents, and how well did the constit- uents know what they were doing? Perhaps most important, how did the interac- tions of legislators and constituents, all these patterns of expectation and responsive- ness, change over the eighteenth century? Recent scholarship has addressed the functioning of particular colonial assem- blies in particular periods: studies have treated, for example, the committee system in South Carolina, membership in the North Carolina assembly, factional divisions in New York, petitioning in Virginia, the rise of "professional" leaders in the Mas- sachusetts assembly. Michael Zuckerman has made us question the relationship of the eighteenth-century Massachusetts legislature to local authorities; his work in- vites the general question whether assemblies built on the growing strength of local institutions or filled the vacuum created by their decline. Jon Kukla, Michael Kammen, and David Jordan have found that colonial assemblies responded efficiently to constituents' wishes during parts of the seventeenth century; perhaps the later responsiveness of assemblies to constituent pressures developed as part of a cyclical, rather than a linear, pattern. All the studies, including some fine ones by Greene's students, have tried to fit the assemblies more fully into the "political world of the . .. colonials."
