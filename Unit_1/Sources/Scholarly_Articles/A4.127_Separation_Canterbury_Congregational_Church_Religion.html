Selection Overview

Jeffries uses the church schism in Canterbury, Connecticut, to ask who was aroused by the Great Awakening and who left the established churches, setting aside the old claim that revivalism divided rich from poor. He concludes that the case confirms that religion was not a compartmentalized aspect of eighteenth-century New England life but was closely bound up in the web of society and government, while cautioning that one town may not stand for New England.

JEFFRIES THE Great Awakening and the consequent Separate move- ment have for some decades been topics of important historical inquiry. Not surprisingly, the research has bred changing and sometimes conflicting interpretations as well expanded knowledge about mid-eighteenth-century revival- ism and separatism. Central to much of the investigation and historiographical debate alike have been the questions of wh was stirred by the revival and of who was sufficiently aroused to leave the established churches. These are vital questions. Because learning "who" is an essential step towards learnin more about "what" and "why," identification of and informa- tion about the people involved are crucial to understanding the Great Awakening and the Separate movement. The issue of economic class has focused much of the discus- sion about those caught up in revivalism and separatism. Closer investigation was needed to identify more precisely the men and women involved in the religious enthusiasm and con- troversies. Recent studies have in general confirmed the view that the revivals and church schisms cut through class lines.4 Even more fruitfully, they have uncovered lines of inquiry beyond simple religious or economic considerations. Without denying the importance of religious conviction and zeal in the Awakening and its repercussions, these studies place the religious phe- nomena in significant and complex sociopolitical contexts. It seems plausible, for example, that the religious revivals and controversies may indeed have in some ways, though not in strict class terms, involved "an estrangement between the lower and upper orders of society."5 There is evidence that the Sepa- rates tended to attract younger people, especially younger sons, and "those at the bottom of the social ladder-bondservants, Indians, Negroes, women, and adolescents."6 In Norton, Mas- sachusetts, males who joined the church during the Awaken- ing were "significantly younger, less established, and less suc- cessful in the world" than those who had joined prior to 1740; they seem to have been "temporarily frustrated" young men with little land and small influence in town affairs.7 Other studies suggest that the Awakening had relatively greater effect on males, particularly younger ones,8 that family upbringing ' See, e.g., Richard L.
