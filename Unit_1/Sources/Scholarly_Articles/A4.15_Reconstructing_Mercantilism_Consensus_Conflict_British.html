Selection Overview

Text of this page (original journal page 257)

M ERCANTILISM ranks among the most controversial terms ever utilized by economic and political historians. Such was not always the case. Until the mid-twentieth century, relative unanimity prevailed among most scholars regarding the basic tenets of mercantilist ideology. Today, however, there is very little agreement on any precise definition for the word mercantilism, and an aura of ambiguity now surrounds it. Some have suggested that we drop the term altogether; others recommend that we define it to include only a loose and vaguely connected assortment of disparate ideas. Challenging the historiographical clouding of mercantilism requires a thorough reconstruction of the term’s fundamental precepts. It requires a new interpretation acknowledging intellectual diversity among mercantilist thinkers while simultaneously identifying a coherent and meaningful set of economic principles among them. Mercantilism was not a static theory but rather a highly dynamic analytic lens permitting a striking multiplicity of economic visions. Diversity, however, arose out of consensus: out of the common supposition that a favorable balance of trade and the resulting influx of money into a country—the balance-of-trade doctrine and specie objective—were the principal means to power and plenty. Most mercantilists did not adhere to Midas’s folly—the belief that money is the only form of wealth—but they undoubtedly assigned a particular importance to money. Money, in the mercantilist view, was a commodity distinct from any other, deserving particular attention from the regulatory state and justifying, in turn, a continual emphasis on the balance of trade that allowed a state and its people to accumulate specie. The balance-of-trade doctrine and specie objective were so widely presumed, and so deeply entrenched, that it would be anachronistic to label mercantilism a conscious theory. Mercantilism, rather, was a lens through which early modern thinkers analyzed economic conditions, grounded upon a presumably self-evident maxim about money. Mercantilism crumbled after the mid-eighteenth century, but not because the desired ends of political economists changed. Power and plenty were still the underlying goals. Mercantilism crumbled because the means to those ends encountered critical opposition. With the emerging opinion that money was neutral—that the quantity of money, in other words, bore no relation to power and plenty—faith in the particularity of money nec- essarily shattered and, with it, the balance-of-trade doctrine and mercan- tilism itself. Free trade replaced mercantilism as the predominant political economy in Britain in the nineteenth century, and free trade’s protectionist opposition subsequently abandoned most references to money. The balance-of-trade doctrine and specie objective comprised the mercantilist consensus. Consensus, however, must never obscure diversity, for the two worked in tandem. The mercantilist quest for a general trade surplus demanded immense creativity, provoking fervent debate over the most effective means to ensure a positive trade balance, resulting in a mul- tiplicity of competing economic solutions. The obfuscation of key differences among mercantilist thinkers was a cardinal sin of economic historians from the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. In spotlighting, almost exclusively, points of con- sensus, they unjustly caricatured mercantilism, treating early modern thinkers as essentially unanimous on major points of economic policy. It was not that earlier historians failed to recognize occasional disagree- ments; they simply believed the differences to be relatively insignificant. Mercantilism, they argued, was static and uniform, and they happily con- trasted it to the allegedly more sophisticated laissez-faire system.
