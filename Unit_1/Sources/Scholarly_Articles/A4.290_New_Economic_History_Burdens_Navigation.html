Selection Overview

Walton responds to Peter McClelland's attack on the estimates of Lawrence Harper and Robert Thomas, and defends the value of the quantitative "new economic history" for this debate. He concludes that the new methods have not altered Harper's general conclusion that it was not exploitation but friction from continued changes in regulation, 1763-75, that urged revolt, and that their main contribution has been to support that position with evidence.

The distinct impression made at the session was that the new economic history has made little, if any, contribution to the analysis of the economic effects of British imperial policy. It seems doubtful that McClelland's measure of the costs of British regulation will be widely accepted amongst interested scholars. He touches on only one aspect-albeit the most important one-of the Acts of Trade, namely, the forced indirect routeing of commodities through England because of British mercantilist restrictions. His upper-bound measure of these burdens is un- doubtedly too large, however, and he reaches no new conclusions regarding the association between British imperial policy and the American Revolution. On the other hand, Thomas's study explicitly covers a wide range of issues and explicitly attempts to measure both the major burdens and the benefits imposed on the colonists by British rule. As will be shown, the main deficiency of Thomas's study is his technique of measuring the burdens from the indirect routeing of goods.2 It should be emphasized that Harper, Thomas, and McClelland agree that the indirect routeing of certain commodities3 was the major economic burden of British imperial policy, and we shall focus attention on this main issue. Other costs (and benefits), such as duties (and bounties) on various colonial goods, are explicitly considered and measured by Harper and Thomas, but by comparison are found to be of secondary importance. The major source of disagreement and most critical aspect of the controversy is the technique of measuring the loss of colonial income due to indirect routeing. It is not a question of disagreement on empirical matters. Thomas specifically criticizes Harper's technique of measure- ment,4 and, as stated above, McClelland finds the conceptual framework of both their studies greatly in error. The major purpose of this article, therefore, is to clarify the differences in their techniques of measurement, disclose any errors or biases in them, and compare their results. Section ii illustrates Thomas's technique of measurement and points out cer- tain errors in his approach. McClelland's criticism of Thomas's approach fails to reveal the full extent of his errors or the likely biases resulting from them.5 Special attention is given to Thomas's study because it is the most comprehensive and because the net effect of his errors is to support his claim that his estimates are upper-bound (too large).
