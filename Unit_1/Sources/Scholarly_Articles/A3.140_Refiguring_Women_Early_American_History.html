Refiguring Women in Early American History Terri L. Snyder W HAT does it mean to refigure women in early America? In part, it refers to a need to reconsider women as subjects in early American studies, in light of advances in theories of gender and studies of sexualities, the intertwining of cultural and political history, and the evolution of Atlantic and continental frameworks. A range of possibili- ties for this kind of refiguring was apparent at the 2011 William and Mary Quarterly and USC-Huntington Early Modern Studies Institute Workshop, “Women in Early America.” The workshop series is designed to explore emerging trends in early American history, and for some the focus on women’s history was hardly, well, new. Papers reframed women as agents in imperial expan- sion and Native claims to territory; refocused them as actors in and beyond marriages and households; reexamined them as figures of print and on the auction block; and revisited the relationship between female subjectivities and the public sphere. These fresh interpretations revealed an emphasis on women’s positions in spatial and collective relations and questioned the normative categories that we use in studying women (and men). The con- tributions testified to the ways in which a refiguring of women as subjects, while important in and of itself, also advances our understanding of early America. The workshop call for papers paraphrased Mary Ritter Beard, who both collaborated with her husband, Charles, and published works on women’s history in her own right. Beginning in the 1930s, Beard argued for the inclusion of, as she phrased it, “woman as force” in history. What she meant by that phrase was something that we take as a commonplace today, that the “personalities, interests, ideals, and activities of women must receive an attention commensurate with their energy in history” because “women have been a force in making all the history that has been made.” 2 Beard also argued that “the narrative of history must be reopened, must be widened to take in the whole course of civilisation as well as [those subjects that she felt historians already addressed—]war, politics, gossip and economics.” 3 In other words, Beard not only envisioned a history of women but also advocated integrating women into the narrative of history itself and, as a consequence, expanding the scope of historiography. During the last thirty years, historians of early American women have answered Beard’s call in many ways.
