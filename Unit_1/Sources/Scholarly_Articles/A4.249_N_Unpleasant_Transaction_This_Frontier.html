Selection Overview

Sleeper-Smith argues that kinship networks gave Native and mixed-ancestry women in the western Great Lakes fur trade real authority under French rule, and that British and then American efforts to regulate the trade and acquire land eroded it.

Relatedness was a primary determinant of power, and nationality re- mained immaterial in this face-to-face world. Many mixed-ancestry de- scendants were prominent cultural brokers who spoke on behalf of native communities and helped negotiate successive relationships with France, England, and the United States. In public ceremonies associated with alliance making they served as translators and often spoke on behalf of their villages.1 This article focuses on three native women who held positions of authority and autonomy because of their access to furs through indige- nous kin networks and to trade goods through their fur-trader husbands. Their names appeared in written records, when Britain and, subse- quently, the United States sought to regulate the trade by licensing trad- ers.
