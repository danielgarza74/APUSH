Selection Overview

Stark and Finke use the Carnegie Institution's colonial church data and Weis's lists of colonial clergy to build a statistical portrait of American religion at the start of the Revolution. They challenge the nostalgic view that the nation began in piety and later lapsed, and they treat denominations as competing in a relatively unregulated religious market.

Sociological Analysis 1988, 49, 1:39-51 American Religion in 1776: A Statistical Portrait Rodney Stark University of Washington Roger Finke Loyola University of Chicago While historians of American religion are aware that the original colonies did not exude universal piety, they have provided few statistics on colonial religion. Without these statistics it is difficult to describe colonial religion with precision, much less attempt to describe or explain changes since colonial times. In the following paper we provide a relatively detailed statistical portrait of American religion at the beginning of the Revolu- tion. To construct data on colonial religion we draw from two essential sources. The first is the Carnegie Institution's project on colonial churches, as conducted by M. W Jerne- gan, and the second is Weis 's series of publications on colonial clergy. Not only do these data provide a more accurate account of religion by colony and denomination, they also serve as a benchmark for religious growth. Finally, we offer an initial look at the shifting fortunes of American denominations, 1776-1850. Nostalgia is the enemy of history. No educated person any longer believes the ancients were correct about a fall from a Golden Age. Yet, we frequently accept inaccurate tales about more recent "good old days"-tales which corrupt our understanding of the past and mislead us about the present. Perhaps we are more susceptible to nostalgic error about religion than on any other subject. It seems an article of faith that this nation began in puritanism and piety, but has since lapsed into worldliness, secularity, and indifference. Of course, the Pilgrim story makes this image all the more compelling. Elsewhere we have challenged this view by showing how high and stable are 20th century patterns of American religious belief and participation (Stark and Glock, 1968; Stark and Bainbridge, 1985). Here our corrective efforts focus on a reappraisal of the past-on recon- structing and assessing the religious situation in America at the beginning of the Revolution. Historians of American religion are well aware that the original colonies did not exude universal piety. Indeed, there is consensus that in colonial America no more than ten to twenty percent of the population actually belonged to a church congregation (Sweet, 1947; Ahlstrom, 1974; Hudson, 1981). Being sociologists, we are trained to fear statistics not based on actual counts, and these are not. Furthermore, we would like to be able to describe the denominational make-up of colonial America with some precision. Such statistics are vital benchmarks for our current efforts to construct a quantitative portrait of the churching of America-the process by which the nation was transformed from one in which most people were unchurched to one in which about two-thirds belong. As we examine this process, we are especially interested in seeing which denominations gained and which lost out. THE CARNEGIE DATA Recently we discovered the means to construct a quite reliable and detailed statistical portrait of religion in America for 1776. There are two essential sources. The first is the Atlas of the Historical Geography of the United States, by Charles 0. Paullin, edited by John K. Wright, and published jointly by the Carnegie Institution of Washington and the American Geographical Society of New York in 1932.2. The second is a series of publications on colonial clergy and congregations compiled by Frederick L'ewis Weis, published by The Society of the Descendants of The Colonial Clergy (Weis, 1936, 1938, 1950, 1955).3 The Atlas was one of the truly great team projects ever conducted by American historians. In 1903 the first outline of the project was proposed by J. F. Jameson, then professor of American history at the University of Chicago. Several years later Jameson became director of the Department of Historical Research at the Carnegie Institution and serious work began. Those helping fashion this great mapping of American history make up a Who's Who of early 20th century scholars. For example, Frederick J. Turner played a leading role until his death, while the mapping of American Indian materials was supervised by A. L. Kroeber. While some of the maps in the Atlas are unremarkable, having been based on well-known data, many others were based on extensive primary research (the project lasted nearly 30 years). One of the most amazing of these original research projects involved an effort to identify and locate every church congregation in America for the period January 1, 1775 to July 4, 1776! This project was directed by M. W. Jernegan, a University of Chicago historian who specialized in the colonial period (cf. Jernegan, 1931). How good are these data? They were produced by what appears to have been an exhaus- tive search of old documents, diaries, newspapers, and accounts to note every mention of a church and then to search out its location. The procedures, described at some length by Jernegan, strike us as wholly practical, requiring simply the application of substantial re- sources-and these seem to have been plentiful at the Carnegie Institution during this time. Indeed, after John C. Merriam became president of the Institution in 1921 the scholars seem to have been in complete control. 1.
