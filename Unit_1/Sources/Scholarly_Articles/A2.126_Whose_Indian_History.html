Selection Overview

Richter argues that scholarship on the Indian peoples of early America may be running out of fuel despite the promise of Fenton, Jennings and Crosby, and he concludes that the lessons of Indian history must be connected to the experiences of other early Americans in an inclusive narrative of the North American past. (historiography)

Whose Indian History? Daniel K. Richter S CHOLARSHIP on the Indian peoples of early America may be running out of fuel. Perhaps that assertion seems strange in the context of this semicentennial symposium. The Institute, after all, published in the mid-i 950 s William Fenton's call for a marriage of history and anthropology in a new "ethnohistory" of native-European relations. Two decades later it issued Francis Jennings's paradigm-shattering The Invasion of America and Alfred Crosby's introduction to the scholarship showing that many millions of native North Americans died from Euro- pean viral infections during the colonial period.' Such works promised to revolutionize early American historiography: comfortable assumptions about the peaceful transit of Western Civilization to the Howling Wilder- ness could, it seemed, no longer be supported in light of Jennings's iconoclasm and an appreciation of the demographic carnage that followed I492. 380 WILLIAM AND MARY QUARTERLY scholars who hoped (in our more hubristic moments) to heed Fenton's call and build what Jennings labeled a new "history of how Euramericans and Amerindians shared in the creation of the society that became the United States of America."2 As the eighties became the nineties and our articles turned into books, the Institute continued to play an important role. Under its imprint, three major monographs appeared that sought to refocus early American history through close examination of Indian ex- periences; several others issued from scholars with close ties to the Institute and its sponsoring agencies.3 By such measures, then, the field has never been healthier, and the Institute may justly take pride in its contributions. I fear, however, that the spate of publications at the turn of the decade heralds, not an exciting period of new departures, but the end of the line. The warning signs are many. Although the subfield some call ethnohistory and others the New Indian History continues to flourish on its own terms, laments proliferate about its scant impact on larger areas of scholarship, on high school and college textbooks, and on the popular mind. Reginald Hors- man's early i 980s observation that "native American history has never quite thrown off the parochial air that dominated it when it was merely a subfield of an equally parochial frontier history" still seems to hold.4 Apparently, the perspectives on native peoples and their relations with European colonizers developed since the I 970S belong only to a tiny sect within the already small scholarly priesthood of early Americanists.
