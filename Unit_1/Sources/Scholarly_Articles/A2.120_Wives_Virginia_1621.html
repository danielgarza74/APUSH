Selection Overview

Ransome uses newly found Ferrar Papers to identify the fifty-seven young women sent to Virginia in 1621 as wives for the settlers, arguing that they were daughters of artisans and gentry with social respectability and domestic skills, not abandoned waifs like the vagrant children shipped in the same years. He suggests that the early Virginia migrants looked more like the mixed multitude who peopled New England than is usually thought.

Wives for Virginia, i62 I David R. Ransome T t HE fifty-seven young women who went out to Virginia in i 62 I to become wives for the settlers are no longer age-less and nameless. We now know that among the passengers on the Marmaduke, Warwick, and Tiger were the teenager Jane Dier, the 25-year-old widow Marie Daucks, and the 28-year-old spinster Allice Goughe. Nor had these fifty-seven women much in common with those abandoned waifs, the vagrant children who were being shipped in those same years from London to Virginia.' The women were the daughters of artisans and gentry. Indeed, Lucy Remnant and Margaret Bourdman were the nieces of knights. Anne Rickard (or Richards) was not the only one to come recommended by responsible citizens, nor was Elizabeth Markham alone in being brought to the Virginia Company by her parents. Documents have recently come to light that reveal such facts. They also testify to the women's social respectability and domestic skills and incidentally suggest where they lived just before they left England. Collectively, such records make possible a more precise assessment than hitherto of early emigrants to Virginia.2 David R. 4 WILLIAM AND MARY QUARTERLY The documents also elucidate the efforts made by the Virginia Com- pany to provide "younge, handsome, and honestly educated Maides" for the unhappy settlers.3 Warrants and receipted bills reveal the cost of clothing, equipping, and sending the women to Virginia; two further documents name the forty-four men who financed the venture. Moreover, the company's efforts, as revealed in the new documents, help to destroy the justice of any contrast formerly made between the provident Puritan management of early New England and the rather less foresighted adventurers of the Virginia colony.4 After i 6i9 the company's leaders systematically endeavored to make conditions in Virginia more pleasing to the colonists and more profitable to the investors. In its first years the colony was run on military lines.5 Those who went over were almost all young men, and they were subject to ordinances modeled on the martial laws imposed on English troops in the Low Countries.6 Not surprisingly, the early colonists resented such a harsh regime, seeing themselves not merely as a garrison but as the first generation peopling a settlement, men who expected the comforts of family life and the right to bequeath their holdings to their heirs. Under Sir Thomas Smith the company made an effort to remedy the lack of women, but the attempt appears to have been neither well planned nor generally welcomed.
