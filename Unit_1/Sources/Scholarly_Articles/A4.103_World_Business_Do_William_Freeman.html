Selection Overview

Hancock argues that explanations focused on the mother country, such as the Commercial, Financial, and Glorious Revolutions, are only partial, and uses the letterbook of the London merchant William Freeman to show another side. He concludes that the first British empire was not governed by a unified set of ideas, policies, or administrators but was a structure created by decentralized opportunism within a framework set by the Navigation Acts.

"A World of Business to Do": William Freeman and the Foundations of England's Commercial Empire, i645-1707 David Hancock T THE empire that England built between i65i and I775 was a kind of empire, significantly larger than the conglomerate that came to be known as Great Britain. It was global, combining terri- tory as far east as India and as far west as America. At the same time, it was commercial, regulated from i65I onward through a series of mercan- tile laws, known as the "Navigation Acts," that sought to reassert control over trade with English America after the chaos of the Civil War allowed the colonists to establish commercial relations with the Dutch and the French. Historians of Great Britain and her empire have proffered various explanations for the buildup of the empire and the expansion of overseas rule. In the second half of the seventeenth century, England experienced a Commercial, a Financial and a Glorious Revolution that contributed in very different ways to the construction of the empire. Chief among their concerns are the roles played by agency and mutuality on the one hand and credit and trust on the other hand. Many of these approaches have been styled "Atlantic," although some embrace India, which even loosely speaking is not in the Atlantic, and others offer little else than relabeled traditional imperial narrative.
