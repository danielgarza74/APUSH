Selection Overview

Waldstreicher argues that Staughton Lynd was right that the most problematic aspect of Charles Beard's work on the Constitution was its failure to see the importance of slavery, a critique largely ignored by later historians. He returns to Beard's own reading of Madison to show that Madison understood slavery's political weight and handled it with careful indirection (historiography).

CE NT E N N I AL S Y M P O S I UM ON C H AR L E S B E A R D’ S E C O N O M I C IN T ER PR ETA T IO N The Beardian Legacy, the Madisonian Moment, and the Politics of Slavery DA VI D WAL DS TR EIC HER Fifty years ago Staughton Lynd observed that the most problematic aspect of Charles Beard’s work on the Constitution was not his focus on economic fac- tors but his inability to see the importance of slavery (1967, 3–21, 135–52). Historians who rightly took issue with Beard’s and the Progressive historians’ lack of interest in ideas have largely ignored this critique, despite the appear- ance of works on slavery and politics in the founding that take as many if not more cues from Bernard Bailyn than from Charles Beard. If one goes back to Beard, one ﬁnds that he did not, in fact, base his entire interpretation on who owned what kind of bonds. When responding to critics in the introduction to the 1935 edition, he avers that he had “read voluminous writings by the Fathers, and I was struck by the emphasis which so many of them placed upon economic interests as forces in politics and in the formula- tion of laws and constitutions.” Thus, Beard called James Madison “a profound student of politics” because The Federalist presented “a masterly statement of the theory of economic determinism in politics”; as a result, he claimed that “the inquiry which follows is based on the political science of James Madi- son.” Beard also notes Madison’s “appeal to the slave-holders’ instinctive fear of a servile revolt” and Madison’s distinctive way of dealing with the matter: “naturally, it is Madison whose interest catches this point and drives it home, by appearing to discount it” (Beard 1935, vii, 14–15, 16, 174). Madison provides a useful vantage point for reassessing Beard in light of the usually segregated politics of ideas, of economic interests, and of slavery. Beard appreciated Madison’s creativity and subtlety. He would not have been surprised by the Madisonian revival in recent scholarship. Careful critics have found a variety of liberal and conservative virtues (and sins) in Madison. Per- haps for this reason, commentators have barely addressed the toughest ques- tion with respect to Madison’s politics and constitutionalism: the place of slav- ery in it. It will not do to say that the issue of slavery in the republic, and in constitutional design, did not occur to Madison or that the problem of slavery was visited on him by politics or that it was a side or derivative issue. And it will not do to blame Madison for the limits of compromise any more than it will do to praise him for the higher virtues of compromise. Yet there are clues that suggest he was quite aware of, as well as accepting of, the tragic limits of his politics and preferred us to understand, and thus continue to think about rather than paper over or re-ratify, the decisions the framers made. Four facts are essential for understanding Madison, slavery, and the Con- stitution, and all four suggest the necessity of considering slavery as a matter of thought as well as interests and compromises. The ﬁrst is that there was no point in his public life when he did not believe that slavery was wrong. The second is that there was no point in his public life when he did not ﬁnd slavery to be an important factor in Virginia and national politics and an embarrass- ment on the international stage—not to be discussed publicly except when necessary. The third is what followed, both naturally and because of his po- litical sensibility: slavery had been and would be factored into statecraft in some fashion. His job, indeed, as a theoretically antislavery Virginian espe- cially, was to ﬁnesse it. The fourth is that he gave us the three-ﬁfths clause in 1783, defended and praised it in 1787 and 1788, and recommended it again to Virginia in 1829. For Madison, slavery was politics and rhetoric as well as an economic interest. In 1776 Madison wrote to his printer friend William Bradford that Lord Dunmore, the royal governor of Virginia, was enticing slaves to help to put down the rebellion. Slavery, in other words, was already at issue at the be- ginning of the revolutionary war in terms of economic interests and military strategy but also in the battle for hearts and minds—including those of the slaves themselves: Madison urged Bradford not to report Dunmore’s actions in his newspaper. During the war, he supported the notion of blacks serving as soldiers on the American side—in Virginia, not just in the north.
