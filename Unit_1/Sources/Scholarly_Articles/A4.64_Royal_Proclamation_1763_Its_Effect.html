Selection Overview

Del Papa argues that the Proclamation of 1763 was meant to end illegal settler encroachment that angered Native peoples, and that it blocked organized settlement west of the Appalachians by Virginia land companies such as the Loyal Land Company until the Indian boundary line of 1768 opened the way.

The Royal Proclamation of 1763 new boundary line, located further westward than the Proclamation Line of 1763 and superseding it, opened up for settlement large areas of the formerly proscribed trans-Appalachian region.3 Most historians have accepted the interpretation of the Proclamation of 1763 as a temporary measure aimed at prohibiting settlement west of the Appalachian Mountains from the date of its publication until the establish- ment of the Indian Boundary Line in 1768. A number of historians have also agreed with the interpretation that, even as a temporary device, the proclamation was a dismal failure in preventing individual, unorganized settlement of the trans-Appalachian region by frontier farmers.4 What his- torians have not agreed upon, indeed, what they have not even taken into consideration, is the question whether or not the proclamation was a success or a failure in preventing collective, organized settlement of the area west of the Appalachians, settlement undertaken by colonial land companies. Yet an examination of the activities of three Virginia land companies, the Ohio, Loyal, and Mississippi land companies, reveals that during the entire period the proclamation was in force, none of these enterprises was successful in carrying out collective, organized settlement in the trans-Appalachian region. The most important and influential of the three companies was the Ohio Company, formed in 1747 by Thomas Lee for the purpose of trade and land speculation.
