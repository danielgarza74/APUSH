Selection Overview

Merritt argues that Indians in Pennsylvania who accepted Moravian Christianity created a distinctive spiritual life in which new practices overlapped with old ones such as dreaming and the power of blood, and that Christianity offered them a way to make sense of life in the white world.

Merritt IN December 1747, Joshua, a Mahican Christian at GnadenhUtten, Pennsylvania, told a Moravian missionary there that he had seen "many Indians in a dream, who asked him to say something to them about his God."' They came from Meniolagomekah, a village eight miles away. When Joshua told them about "the Lamb who was hung on the Cross for their sins" and about Christ's sacrifice of blood, one of their leaders urged his followers to pay close attention and remember Joshua's words.2 Several weeks after Joshua related his dream, the missionary Christian Heinrich Rauch noted significantly in his journal that a Delaware arrived from Meniolagomekah, asking to hear about the Christian Savior.3 That a dream vision foretold a meeting between Christian Mahicans from GnadenhUtten and non-Christian Delawares from Meniolagomekah is perhaps less remarkable than it seems. For several decades, the village had been home to an extended network of New Jersey Delawares who had kin- ship ties to natives along the Susquehanna River at Shamokin, Nescopeck, Waphallopen, and the Wyoming Valley. The captain of Meniolagomekah, twenty-seven-year-old Memenowal, began to visit occa- sionally, curious about Moravian religious practices. Certainly, by the time Joshua dreamed of their meeting, the Delawares were well aware of the local Christian Mahicans, who, in turn, were most likely familiar with their Delaware neighbors. Still, dreams were powerful signs, noted and interpreted by Indians and missionaries alike and by Christians and non-Christians, and dreams were particularly important in connecting backcountry communities. The meaning of dreams, however, and the influence they had on Native American society and religion in the eighteenth century did not always promote the peace and cooperation suggested by Joshua's vision. Other, more troubling images also informed Indian spiritual life. In February 1749, more than a year after Joshua's dream, a strange Indian came by way of Meniolagomekah to Gnadenhiitten and described, as he probably had to the Delaware village, a recent dream that had come to a Nanticoke along the Susquehanna River. This Nanticoke had become ill the summer before and disappeared. When found two days later "in the bush," he said he had been to heaven "and had been with God and had seen him and spoke with him." God revealed to the Nanticoke that "he had made brown and white people. To the Indians, he gave the Sacrifice, which they were to offer to him if they had not acted properly. T white people he had given the Bible." However, both Indian and white people had strayed from their proper ways, and God "was not at peace with either." Further, God told the Nanticoke that Indians and whites should not "go the same way," especially since "the white people were very cunning and had a large mouth. If Indians associated with them, the white people would devour all of them." To show his disapproval and anger, God had caused a recent spate of epidemics and famine among the Indians of the region.5 Rather than driving a wedge between Pennsylvania Indians and whites, as the Nanticoke dreamer perhaps intended, this revelation, and many like it, sparked serious debates in Indian communities about native spirituality and the place of Christianity in their cultures. Some historians have sug- gested that Indians turned to Christianity as a desperate reaction to a world in crisis.
