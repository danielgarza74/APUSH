Selection Overview

Thornton argues that the Maryland charter, the model for the other proprietary colonies, drew on the English palatinate of Durham, a link historians have neglected, and that the later development of the proprietary provinces was fundamentally shaped by this original reliance. He adds that it was only in the eighteenth century that this natural diversity was challenged on both sides of the Atlantic, a challenge that produced revolution.

The Palatinate of Durham and the Maryland Charter by TIM THORNTON* The relationship between the North America and England in the early seventeenth century continues to evoke vigorous debate. Understandably: it holds many of the keys to the history of both Britain and the USA and it is a vital case study in the general question of the rela- tionship between core and periphery, between imperial metropolis and dominions. Most recently, exponents of American exceptionalism have come under heavy fire from two contrasting historiographical tendencies. The first emphasises that American society was no radical or lasting departure from contemporary English society;1 the second returns to themes of colonial history and throws new light on the place of the colonies in an extended English politics.2 Stimulated by these develop- ments, this article revisits a tie to the old country which has often been seen as mere antiquarianism and consequently irrelevant. Even those his- torians who have placed more emphasis on the continuing connections between the British Isles and the American colonies, and the creative power of that connection, have tended to overlook one of the most impor- tant of those connections for colonial American politics, its contribution to the shaping of the proprietary charter. Given that the Maryland Charter provided the model for all the other proprietary colonies of early English America, it is remarkable how little discussion has been devoted to one of the founding principles of the char- ter, its links to the English palatinate of Durham; it is the intention of this paper to rectify this omission.3 A major reason for the lack of interest in *Tim Thornton, MA, DPhil (Oxon.) This reconsideration is vital for an understanding of the early American experience. In the latter stages of the nineteenth century, historians of early America had no doubt about the nature of the Durham clause of the Maryland Charter.4 They saw it as feudal and believed that it was con- ceived in the context of a feudal reaction by autocratic monarchs who were doing their best to counter the growing confidence of the English House of Commons. Newton Mereness argued that the charter was "brought into existence in an atmosphere of absolutism".5 M.P. Andrews thought that the charter was obtained at a time when "political parties were being formed-one composed of the upholders of the prerogatives of Tudor rule; the other a 'popular' group that, at the risk not only of livelihood but of life itself, was to assert the doctrine that sovereignty had its true origin in the people".
