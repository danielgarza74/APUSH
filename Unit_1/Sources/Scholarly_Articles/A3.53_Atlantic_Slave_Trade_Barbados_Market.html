Selection Overview

Galenson uses Royal African Company auction records from Barbados to estimate annual slave prices by age and sex. He argues that rising slave prices in the West Indies raised the share of children in the transatlantic trade, a link between American slave markets and the demographic history of both Africa and America.

THE JOURNAL OF ECONOMIC HISTORY VOLUME XLII SEPTEMBER 1982 NUMBER 3 The Atlantic Slave Trade and the Barbados Market, 1673-1 723 DAVID W. GALENSON Evidence drawn from records of auctions held by the Royal African Company in Barbados between 1673 and 1723 is used to obtain annual estimates of slave prices by demographic category. These price series are then used to investigate the implications of an economic analysis of the demographic composition of the slave trade. The results provide quantitative support for the prediction that rising slave prices in the West Indies caused an increase in the share of children among the population of slaves in the transatlantic trade. This economic effect may have been a significant link between American slave markets and the demographic history of black populations in both Africa and America. IN 1640, thirteen years after its initial settlement by British colonists, Barbados was the home of a community of planters who produced a variety of crops, including tobacco, cotton, ginger, and indigo, with a labor force composed predominantly of white indentured servants. By 1660, the island's economy had been transformed, as planters had switched to a virtual monoculture in sugar, and had converted their labor force to one made up overwhelmingly of African slaves.' 492 Galenson rising demand for labor created by the island's sugar revolution had already produced a black majority in the island's population by 1660, and the growth of the colony's slave population pushed the share of blacks still higher in the decades that followed, to more than three-fifths of total population in 1670, and over three-quarters by 1700.2 By 1680, the sugar planters of Barbados were the wealthiest group of men in British America. Their shipments made up more than half of total annual British sugar imports, and were worth more than the total value of exports to England from all the American mainland colonies. The wealth of the Barbados planters was based on plantations not yet rivalled in numbers and scale elsewhere in the British colonies, as fully 175 planters owned 60 or more slaves.3 But while sugar and slaves were two salient characteristics of Barbados in the late seventeenth century, death was a third. The island's extremely high mortality rates quickly led planters to the realization that they would not raise their holdings of slaves through natural increase of the population, and convinced them of the need to rely on the transatlantic slave trade to prevent their labor force from dwindling. The result was a massive continuing importation of African slaves. Recent estimates indicate that net decennial migration to Barbados during the five decades between 1670 and 1720 ranged from amounts equivalent to 40 to 70 percent of the colony's population at the start of the respective decades, and that blacks accounted for between 70 and 90 percent of net decennial migration to the island in this period.4 The Royal African Company was formed in 1672 as the successor to the defunct Royal Adventurers into Africa, which had survived for barely ten years. The new company was granted a monopoly of the slave trade to the colonies of English America by Charles II.
