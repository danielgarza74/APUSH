Selection Overview

Calloway argues that Britain’s neglect of its Indian allies in the 1783 Peace of Paris embittered them and shaped British-Indian relations for the next thirty years. Each side distrusted the other, yet both stayed in the alliance out of shared fear of American expansion, and Calloway concludes that British aid only delayed American dominion over Native lands.

Suspicion and Self-Interest: The British Indian Alliance and the Peace of Paris By Colin G. Calloway* In 1783 news reached North America that provisional terms of peace had been agreed upon in Paris. Confirmed by treaty in September, the agreement recognized the independence of Britain's thirteen former colonies and proclaimed the sovereignty of the United States north to the Great Lakes, south to the Floridas, and west to the Mississippi. The peace settlement made no mention of the Indian peoples who inhabited this territory, even though many of the tribes had participated in the Revolutionary War as allies of the British. In dealing with one another, European states did not acknowledge the sovereignty of Indian tribes. European powers regarded North America as a vacant land which could be claimed by right of discovery or by right of settlement, and which could be conquered and transferred by one European nation to another. Treaties with Indian tribes did not alter these claims or rights. The United States expected and claimed the interior of North America on the basis of charter rights and as successors to the rights of the British crown. Nevertheless, the Peace of Paris threw relations between Britain and her Indian allies into a new phase. Each party now had to contend with the United States and, after 1783, British Indian relations existed against the backdrop of a delicate international situation. Indian tribes who had become accustomed to dealing with King George and his representatives found that, in many cases, their territory had been transferred to the United States and that the Americans claimed their lands by right of conquest. This article seeks neither to retell the story of Indian involvement in the American Revolution nor to review the relations of Britain, the United States, and the Indian tribes in the post-Revolutionary decades. Rather, it shows how British neglect of her allies at the Peace of Paris embittered the Indians and affected the pattern of British-Indian relations for the next thirty years. The Peace of Paris caused Indians to view their British allies with a suspicion born of harsh experience, but it also increased the threat to Indian lands and cultures which had driven them into the arms of the British in the first place. In many ways, the British and Indians had been natural allies ever since the outbreak of war in 1775, if not before. American colonists made the first overtures for Indian help, but the British were more successful and employed Indian auxiliaries on a far greater scale.1 The British commonly believed that Indians did not know the meaning of neutrality and that failure to enlist them as allies was tantamount to guaranteeing their eventual hostility.2 For their part the majority of Indian tribes identified American land hunger as their greatest threat and believed that redcoats and Indians faced a common enemy. British agents assured the Indians that the Americans were intent upon their destruction, and American frontiersmen demonstrated the validity of these warnings.
