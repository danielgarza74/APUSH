Selection Overview

Washburn argues that much of Sarah Drummond’s 1677 petition to the Lords of Trade, which historians have accepted almost as readily as the English government did, was false or questionable. He adds that it helped destroy Governor Berkeley’s reputation as a man and a public servant, and that the allegations of cruelty after Bacon’s Rebellion are of minor importance among the charges against Berkeley.

The Humble Petition of Sarah Drummond Wilcomb E. Washburn* OA N the twentieth day of January, i677, William Drummond was hanged by the neck until he was dead. The story of his capture and of his acid interview by Governor Berkeley is a well-known episode of Bacon's Rebellion. Sir William Berkeley is supposed to have greeted Drummond, one of Bacon's chief lieutenants, with an exaggerated bow. "Mr. Drumond! You are very welcome, I am more Glad to See you, than any man in Virginea, Mr. Drumond you shall be hang'd in half an hour... ." 1 The deed was not done so quickly, however, or so simply, as these words would suggest. There were certain details to be taken care of, such as a court-martial: nevertheless the deed was done in less than a week, and an important rebel began his ascent from infamy to martyrdom. Several months after the execution, Sarah Drummond, relict of the hanged rebel, boarded ship for England to seek redress from His Majesty's government. Sarah, like her husband, had been an active supporter of the rebellion. "The child that is unborn," she is reported to have said, "shall have cause to rejoice for the good that will come by the rising of the country." THE HUMBLE PETITION OF SARAH DRUMMOND 355 It was she, it is said, who called James City, seat of royal authority in Virginia, a "little sodom."8 In England, Mrs. Drummond presented a petition to the Lords Com- mittee for Trade and Plantations, and through them, to the King whose power she so despised. Neither the Lords nor the King seriously ques- tioned its truth at the time, and the King's subsequent order to the Vir- ginia government justified Sarah's claims. Historians of Bacon's Rebellion have accepted the truth of her petition almost as readily as did the govern- ment of England, and they have used the incident to confirm their belief in the alleged "cruelty" and "terror" exercised by Berkeley in putting down the rebellion. John Daly Burk asserted that her case, "for injustice, has been seldom exceeded."4 Sir William Berkeley could not defend himself. He had died on July 9, three months before the case was heard. Sarah Drummond's allegations are really of minor importance in the total list of chicaneries charged to the aged and loyal governor. Yet the story of her petition is of interest because it illustrates the difficulties of administering a distant dependency in days when decisions were made on the basis of scanty, and frequently untruthful, information.
