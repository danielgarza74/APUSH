Selection Overview

Westerkamp surveys earlier readings of Anne Hutchinson (as victim, threat, neurotic, heretic, protofeminist) and argues that her mysticism challenged Winthrop's sense of order, and that her femaleness challenged his relationship with God. She concludes that by claiming the right to teach and a direct relation to the Spirit she offered women a chance to recover the self, which undermined Winthrop and his clergy.

Anne Hutchinson, Sectarian Mysticism, and the Puritan Order MARILYN J. WESTERKAMP Anne Hutchinson has been one of the few women to attain canonical status in the history of colonial New England. Her marvelous intellectual abilities (so unusual in a seventeenth-century woman), her popularity among Boston men as well as women, and the powerful political and theological implica- tions of her challenge render Hutchinson a force that must be explored if colonial Massachusetts is to be understood. Not only are historians fascinated by this extraordinary woman herself, they are intrigued by the colony's response to her; for in that very response the founders may have revealed their essential character. So a few books and many articles have analyzed and reanalyzed Hutchinson as victim of Puritan injustice, as threat to the Puritan experiment, as menopausal neurotic, as antinomian heretic, as rebel (occasion- ally a protofeminist one). Three biographies published in 1930 all portray Hutchinson as a pioneer for civil and religious liberty, crusading against the strictures of Puritan society.' As a historical corrective, Edmund Morgan pointed out that while the modern scholar might sympathize with Hutchinson's plight, especially the arbitrary nature of her trial, the Puritan colony was fairly new and Hutchinson represented a real threat to its political stability.2 More recently, Emery Battis claimed to reexamine the facts surrounding Hutchinson and the society she challenged. Since Massachusetts was a biblical commonwealth, heresy posed a serious threat to social unity and political stability.4 A final group comprises those interpretations that examine Hutchinson as rebel. Legal scholars analyze the dynamics of Hutchinson's trial as the state trial of a disruptive dissenter.5 One social historian considers Hutchinson a latent feminist leading other women in protest against the rigid male hierarchy, while a sociologist emphasizes the state's response to her challenge as the means by which Winthrop solidified his power.6 These essays appear to offer different interpretations, yet beneath the surface lies a basic similarity revealed by the title of an essay collection on Hutchinson: "Troubler of the Puritan Zion."7 Anne Hutchinson was a deviant, a disturber of an otherwise peaceful, stable Puritan society which, of course, identified itself with the promised land. The identification of an individual as deviant involves two separate factors. First, a deviant must have something to deviate from, a stable society that establishes the conventions and characteristics of a status quo. That is, the very recognition and definition of deviance assumes the existence of certain value structures that make up a "collective conscience."8 Some ideological hegemony, however basic, must be operating. In counterpoint to this main- stream a deviant exhibits certain behaviors, supported by a specific belief system, that place him or her outside, perhaps in opposition to, that main- stream.
