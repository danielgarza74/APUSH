EVAN HAEFELI Anti-Catholicism in Colonial Virginia olonial Virginia does not figure much in accounts of early American anti-Catholicism. The evidence just cannot compare to what is available in the northern colonies, especially New England. Early American anti-Catholicism in Virginia is not ignored in surveys, but it plays a marginal role. Within the historiography of the Old Dominion, anti- Catholicism is even less evident. A recent study of early Virginians’ ideology, or how they “understood and experienced their world,” says nothing about it. Even the Jesuit priest and notable historian of early Virginia religious his- tory Thomas Buckley does not think anti-Catholicism matters much. Virginians “paid scant attention” to the few Catholics living in the state “until the constitutional convention of 1901.” Things had apparently been a bit different in the first half of the twentieth century. Back in 1962, Bruce Steiner had felt it necessary to challenge the “black” picture earlier historians had painted of the condition of Roman Catholics in colonial Virginia. That conclusion had been “based principally upon an examination of the anti- Catholic laws.” His study of the Catholic Brent family showed how laxly those laws could be enforced. To his mind, colonial Virginia exercised “a practical toleration despite anti-Catholic laws.” Since then, historians have emphasized Virginians’ capacity for tolerance over their prejudices. In this, they are reverting to the way nineteenth-century Virginians liked to see themselves: “‘the Mother of States and Statesmen’ on the subject of civil and religious freedom.” Consequently, we now have an increasing number of accounts of Virginia’s contribution to religious freedom, but still no proper account of anti-Catholicism’s place in its early history.1 Admittedly, historians have long acknowledged the existence of anti- Catholicism. In the 1980s, Rhys Isaac underlined how fundamental anti-Catholicism was to eighteenth-century Virginia. Officers of government, including judges, had to take an oath of allegiance and the Test Act, “an explicit expression of detestation for the papist doctrine of transubstantiation, and so frequently served to reaffirm the official identity of white Virginians as Protestant Englishmen.” The passage of the 1774 Quebec Act tolerating Roman Catholicism in Canada provoked widespread opposition, including a “swinging ballad” in The Virginia Gazette that “categorized the king’s advisers as ‘Papist Knaves’ and asked defiantly whether ‘free born men’ would be ‘rul’d by Popish law, Because they freedom claim.’” Popery was identified with “oppression” in colonial Virginia, and as such had long been “a part of Anglo-American folk culture.” More recently, Scott Rohrer noted the “intense anti-Catholicism” of Virginia’s parish-based religious life. However, neither he nor Isaac elab- orated on their insights.2 Part of the problem is a matter of perspective. If one thinks of anti- Catholicism primarily as a sort of ethno-religious prejudice that can be measured by how people treat actual Roman Catholics or their religion, one will not find much in Virginia. Until the late twentieth century, the number of Roman Catholics living in Virginia was tiny and unobtrusive. However, if one recognizes anti-Catholicism as an ideology, a well-developed way of thinking about the world that guided action on a variety of issues, only some of which directly involved actual (as opposed to imagined) Roman Catholics or their actual (as opposed to imagined) religion, then anti-Catholicism turns out to be a fundamental aspect of early Virginia society. It justified the foundation of the colony and sustained its allegiance to the mother country. In addition to defining official religious practice, it set limits on social inclusion and political participation. It influenced attitudes toward foreign countries and their subjects. In these cases, colonial Virginia’s anti- Catholicism functioned much as it did within England. Indeed, Virginian anti-Catholicism was largely an overseas extension of English anti-popery.
