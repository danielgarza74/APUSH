Selection Overview

Tiedemann uses Queens County, Long Island, as a test case of Britain's effort to win civilian support in areas its army occupied. He concludes that British misrule, shaped by avarice, ambition, and prejudice, left most residents, especially those who had been neutral before the war, patriots less by choice or conviction than by British default.

Given the significance of the political dimension of the conflict and Britain's inept handling of it, the topic merits further examination. Queens County, Long Island, New York, provides an appropriate locale. As one of the first areas recaptured by the British, it was a logical place to initiate the work of reconstruction and signal the government's good intentions for the future.3 Revolutionary Queens County constitut- ed what is today the Borough of Queens in New York City and the adjacent County of Nassau. It was bounded on the north by the Long Island Sound, on the south by the Atlantic Ocean, on the east by Suffolk County, and on the west by Brooklyn and the East River. In 1776 it consisted of five towns-Newtown, Flushing, Jamaica, Hempstead, and Oyster Bay-and had a population of approximately ii,ooo, most of whom were farmers.4 If the people of Queens County had had their way, there would not have been a Revolution in I776. A decisive majority of the adult male residents, 6o percent, were neutral in the period immediately before the war.
