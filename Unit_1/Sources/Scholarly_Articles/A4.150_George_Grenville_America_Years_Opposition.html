Selection Overview

Lawson argues that the usual portrait of George Grenville as a man obsessed with the Stamp Act after 1765 needs basic qualification. He shows that Grenville’s hard line on Parliament’s right to tax was not representative of his approach to other colonial questions, and that he grasped the issues the colonists raised and sympathized with some of their claims.

This summary of George Grenville's conduct in opposition has found few detractors since it was written some twenty-five years ago.' It is a fair portrait of a man obsessed with an ill-fated policy, trying desperately to justify his actions for posterity. Yet it requires basic qualification and correction. Grenville's hard line on taxation was not rep- resentative of his approach to other aspects of British colonial policy. He had a firm grasp of the issues raised by colonial protestors in this period and not only criticized government policy but also sympathized with cer- tain claims made by the Americans. Too often these facts have been ignored or neglected. Grenville's reputation has suffered from the legacy of the Stamp Act, which has clouded judgment of his role as an opposition mem- ber of Parliament. The evidence to condemn Grenville as the scourge of the American spokesmen for colonial rights appears overwhelming. Each debate on America from I765 to I770 bears witness to his contempt for the claim that Parliament's legislative power was limited. On three issues in dispute, Grenville brooked no compromise. The first, Parliament's right to levy taxes in America, was nearest his heart, and he addressed it with a passion that derived from his responsibility for the Stamp Act of 1765. In his opinion, this legislation provided an essential basis for ordering Anglo- American relations. It set British and colonial taxpayers on a similar foot- ing and established the principle that America should contribute directly to the cost of its own defense. He underlined in vivid terms the cost to English taxpayers and the dangerous precedent of yielding to colonial threats and violence. He dismissed political and economic arguments for repeal as a smokescreen for lack of resolve on the part of the government. "America would not have been in this condition," he told the Commons on February 7, I766, "if they had believed that we would enforce the law, "3 and he ridiculed the Declaratory Act that accompanied the repeal for embodying the notion that Parliament had the right to tax but should refrain from exercising it. This combination, he wrote in March I766, added up to "a shameful and pernicious measure which is intended to give up now and forever the fundamental rights of the kingdom and legislature of Great Britain."4 Grenville maintained this stance in the protracted debate over the Townshend duties of I 767. Again it was the government's lack of determi- nation to enforce parliamentary legislation that aroused his anger. He had no objection to Townshend's desire for revenue from trade but assailed his distinction between internal and external taxation. William Pitt had first raised this idea in January I766, and Grenville had attacked it then, and throughout the repeal debates, as nonsense.5 Yet, much to Grenville's consternation, Townshend told the House on February i8, I767, that even though the distinction was not "founded on reason," it was "proper to be adopted in policy."6 Grenville tried throughout the spring to per- suade the House of Commons not to endorse any measure that admitted a limit to Parliament's powers. Townshend's concession, he said on May I 5, would lead to others until Parliament retained no authority at all. Few in the Commons shared these fears.
