Selection Overview

Jennings argues that the Iroquois "empire" is an exaggeration, fostered by Iroquois diplomacy and by colonial governments that wanted the Iroquois to look larger than life, and that Covenant Chain records instead show continual Indian efforts to accommodate and cooperate with Europeans. He contends that what defeated the Indians was European refusal to treat cooperation as more than an expedient.

THE CONSTITUTIONAL EVOLUTION OF THE COVENANT CHAIN FRANCIS JENNINGS Chairman, Department of History, Cedar Crest College "THE COVENANT CHAIN" is a term frequently encountered in the records of Indian Affairs of New York and Pennsylvania. It was a favorite of the Iroquois chiefs, much usel by them in treaty negotiations, and consequently it came to be associated with the Indian confederacy which has often been described erroneously though none- theless dramatically as the Iroquois "empire." The Iroquois themselves were not shy about pub- licity, and they made no discernible effort to de- flate their oversized reputation. Indeed the his- torical statements they frequently recited to English officials show a remarkable awareness of the diplomatic uses of history, not to speak of a truly civilized talent for giving it a self-serving twist every now and then. Because the governments of New York, and then of Pennsylvania, had their own reasons for wanting the Iroquois to look larger than life, their record keepers were willing to accept and even to further increase the pretensions of the Iroquois. It is difficult, therefore, to get a reasonable under- standing of the extent of Iroquois ascendancy over other Indians. The source documents must be put to severe tests of verification. Abstractly, the Chain was a unique institution created by contract for eliminating vio- lence and reducing conflict between Indians and Englishmen within specified and bounded terri- tories. It was a means for adjusting boundary changes. It was also an agency for facilitating English expansion against the French and their allies, and for overcoming physical obstacles: Covenant Chain Indians pioneered, secured, and financed the Englishman's way to the trans-Ap- palachian west. The Chain had a historical existence with a be- ginning and an end. Its earliest antecedents can be traced to the alliances between the Indians and Dutch of New Netherlands. I have been careful here not to say "the Iroquois" and the Dutch because of an exception that must be made for the non-Iroquois tribes living along the Hud- son River. It is significant that these so-called River Indians placed the beginning of the Cove- nant Chain at a much earlier date than the one given by the Iroquois. In 1703, the Schaghti- cokes of the upper Hudson stated that their an- cestors' contact with the "first Christian" had occurred eighty-five years earlier, thus dating it at 1618. "Then we tied them with a Roap," said the Schaghticokes, "but now they are fastened with an Iron Chain to ye tree of welfair so that wee hitherto have stood firm to ye Covenant Chain with our father." 4 This tradition apparently became confused dur- ing the prolonged disappearance of the Living- ston Indian Records in which it is preserved, so that the 1618 treaty was ascribed to the Mohawks instead of the River Indians. Hunt and Trelease have pointed out the error.5 The Mohawks them- selves claimed 1643 as their date of first alliance with the Dutch." The records consistently show the River Indians in di- rect treaty negotiations with New York's officials and without either intervention or participation of Iroquois chiefs.7 There are precise dates to illuminate the treaty relationships. In 1672 the Mohawks complained at Albany of being harassed by the Mahicans of the Hudson Valley, and the English commander at Albany promised to force the Mahicans to be at peace.8 In 1675 the English still addressed the Mahicans and other River Indians as "breth- ren." 9 In 1676 New York's Governor Andros incited the Mohawks to attack the River Indians who were mobilizing to raid New England during King Philip's War,10 and in 1677 the River In- dians acknowledged that Andros "is become our father." 11 The change from brethren to children marked the moment of their subjection. But it was Andros rather than the Mohawks who had become father. Since the River Indians thereafter frequently mentioned their linkage to the Cove- nant Chain, it is clear that the central link in that Chain-at least, during its early days-was New York.
