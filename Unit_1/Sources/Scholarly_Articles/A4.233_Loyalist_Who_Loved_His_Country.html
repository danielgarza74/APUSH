EXCERPT Original journal pages 344-345

Selection Overview

Riordan argues that the loyalism of Thomas Hutchinson, the last civilian governor of colonial Massachusetts, grew out of a strong colonial and provincial commitment and was forged in the face of conflict, and that his history of the colony shows him to have been more moderate and more devoted to his country than his rebel opponents allowed. He presents Hutchinson as one case in a broader project on the diversity of loyalism.

THOMAS HUTCHINSON, HISTORIAN 345 What follows examines the loyalism of Thomas Hutchin- son (1711-1780), the last civilian colonial governor of Mas- sachusetts, who Bernard Bailyn’s magisterial biography dubbed “the most important loyalist of all.”2 Hutchinson merits such status for several reasons—his major role in most of the famous events leading to the Revolution in Boston, his demonization in Massachusetts (and beyond), and by the simple fact that his vast surviving written material tempts one to think that we re- ally can plumb the depths of this key actor. That roughly half of his papers are in the state archives of Massachusetts, having been seized by rebel authorities in spring 1775, and that the other half are in the British Library, the result of his forced exile for the final six years of his life, testifies to how fully the Revolution sundered his world. Hutchinson also provides a valuable point of entry because he stood at the vanguard of loyalism, and his words, actions, and experiences as early as the mid-1760s anticipated many critical loyalist themes and experi- ences that would take clearer shape on a larger scale a decade or two later. Just as it is now commonplace to accept that there were many different reasons to be a patriot, so too, many different impulses led to loyalism. Even for so seemingly doctrinaire a standard-bearer as Thomas Hutchinson, being a loyalist was a dynamic status, and one he did not fully accept for himself until well after his remove to England.
