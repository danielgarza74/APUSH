Selection Overview

Osterud argues that historians of rural capitalism, whether of the "market" or the "social" school, share unexamined assumptions about gender, including male household heads' right to represent women and children in the market. She holds that gender relations are central to the history of capitalism in the countryside.

The "social" historians' perspective, which is most often based on some version of Marxist economic theory, calls the mar? ket orientation of farmers into question but does not question these as- sumptions about gender.2 Participants on both sides of the debate tend to ignore the common, gender-based ground beneath their supposedly opposing views. A few historians, such as Toby Ditz, Allan Kulikoff, and Stephanie McCurry, have noted the contradictions implicit in "yeoman" ideology-that men's supposed independence was based on their actual authority over and dependence on the labor of women and children in their households ?but scholars have seldom scrutinized the material roots of these formulations.3 A feminist approach points out t lematic character of fundamental social relations and raises new ques? tions about how male dominance became incorporated into capitalist society. It will not do, however, to conceptualize the gender system as a sep? arate dimension of social relations that interacts with capitalism.
