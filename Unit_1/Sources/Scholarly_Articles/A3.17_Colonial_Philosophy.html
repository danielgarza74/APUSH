Selection Overview

Buranelli argues that American colonial philosophy has been neglected, beginning with Tocqueville's judgment that Americans had no philosophical school of their own, and that its leading figures deserve study alongside colonial literature and science. He concludes that Edwards, Johnson, Colden, and Witherspoon lead back to Plato, Locke, Augustine, Berkeley, and Newton, and that the field brings intellectual rigor to American studies.

Colonial Philosophy Vincent Buranelli* W X T HEN Alexis de Tocqueville published the finest of all com- mentaries on American civilization, he noted that abstract ideas survived with difficulty in the intellectual atmosphere of the United States. "I think that in no country in the civilized world is less attention paid to philosophy . . . ," he wrote. "The Americans have no philosophical school of their own, and they care but little for all the schools into which Europe is divided, the very names of which are scarcely known to them."' De Tocqueville was in America for less than a year in i83I-32; his judgment on philosophy appeared with the publication of the second vol- ume of D'mocratie en Amerique in i840; all of which might prompt the obvious rejoinder that between those two dates the judgment ceased to be true, since the Transcendental Club had begun to meet in Boston in i836, and since Emerson had published Nature in that year and The American Scholar in i837. Whatever one may think of transcendentalism as philos- ophy, here surely there was no indifference either to European schools of thought in general or to an American school in particular. Again, it appears arguable that if De Tocqueville had been gifted with his wonted prescience he might have foreseen the coming of pragmatism. Diverted by the spectacle of democratic nation-building in the wilderness, did he not overlook the seeds of theory that would one day sprout into a system? Did he visit this country a little too soon? Such criticism is hypothetical. When writing his masterpiece, De Toc- queville had lost touch with American thought of the moment, and he could not reasonably have been expected to perceive everything germinat- ing a decade before. The really puzzling thing is his ignorance of the past. Even today the phrase "colonial philosophy" falls on the ear with a peculiar ring. We rarely hear it, even though colonial history is an old friend, even though we feel quite comfortable with colonial literature and architecture and journalism and are beginning to get used to colonial science. Since we are fully aware of the subjects on the periphery of philos- ophy-colonial religion and political theory-it is illogical to neglect the central discipline itself. Colonial metaphysics and its ramifications into epistemology, psychology, and ethics ought to claim its share of scholarly research; the leading colonial philosophers ought to stand beside the writers, artists, scientists, and political theorists of the period. Yet it is not so. General histories of colonial philosophy are so rare as to be curiosities. It is still possible to write a survey of colonial culture that allots chapters to everything from sectarianism and scholarship to art and music-to everything, that is, except philosophy.8 There are celebrated working models for this approach. Vernon L. Parrington remains silent about most of the colonial philosophers.' Merle Curti, in a work profess- edly on intellectual history, isolates major strands of colonial thought for explicit treatment: literature and science are there, but not philosophy.5 References are numerous enough to prove that philosophy as such was a living reality in the colonies and to puzzle us that it has so infrequently been given its due. Religious notions shade easily into metaphysics, po- litical theory links up with psychology and ethics, while education neces- sarily touches philosophy at every point.
