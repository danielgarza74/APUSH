Selection Overview

Parker takes up a nineteenth-century minister's claim that Edwards's melancholy shaped his theology, and places Edwards's emphasis on experimental religion within Puritan fears and debates about melancholy. She concludes that his conception of experimental religion nonetheless represented a profound assault on the authors of New England orthodoxy.

JONATHAN EDWARDS AND MELANCHOLY GAIL THAIN PARKER IN 1890o, the Reverend Joseph Crocker, a Congregational minister full of confidence in his own enlightenment, un- dertook a psychological study of Jonathan Edwards. He por- trayed Edwards as a melancholy youth, fatally lacking in ani- mal spirits, who "was turned to studying his own heart in the light of a cruel theology, when he ought to have been engaged in healthy sports or mental pursuits that would have given a different direction to his thoughts." What the young Edwards took for sins were the phantoms of his diseased imagination. His ecstasies and trances, his awful visions of the depravity of man, seemed to Crocker "incipient mental disorder." "Poign- ant gloom and distress over purely fictional sins is the evidence of delusional monomania. A boy to-day with such symptoms would be put under the care of a skillful neurologist."' It is easy to see the ludicrous aspects of Crocker's analysis and to overlook his provocative suggestion that Edwards' melancholy might effectively have prevented him from putting his conceptions to the test of experience.2 Throughout his life Edwards was acutely aware of the danger that the insights that came to him in his closet were mere delusions. Moreover, his emphasis on the need for experimental religion was clearly informed by his familiarity with the disease of melancholy in himself and others. Although the endemic melancholy of Puritan life has been exaggerated, it cannot be dismissed as an illusion of modern criticism.
