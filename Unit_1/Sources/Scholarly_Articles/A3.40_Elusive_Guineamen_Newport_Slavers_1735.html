Selection Overview

Deutsch begins with Newport merchant Godfrey Malbone, reputed the colony's leading Guinea trader despite his failed early voyages, and observes that legend places the slave trade at the center of Newport's commerce. She goes on to examine Newport slavers, finding that merchants tried other routes at least as often as the famous triangle, spread their risks, and diversified.

The Elusive Guineamen: Newport Slavers, 1735-1774 SARAH DEUTSCH By contemporary estimate, Godfrey Malbone knew more about the "Guinea" trade than anyone in Newport, Rhode Island. In 1740 the local merchants thought him "the most considerable Trader of any here to the Coast of Guinea."'1 His early life shows little enough cause for this dubious tribute. A native of Princess Ann County, Virginia, Malbone traveled to Newport as a young man and apprenticed himself to a ship's captain. By 1731 he had married, been elected a vestryman at the Anglican Trinity Church, and become a captain in his own right. The records do not reveal whether, in these early years, Malbone himself ever went to Africa. Then, at some point in the next five years, Malbone came into possession of a con- siderable estate in Virginia, including some fifty slaves. The value of this estate changed Malbone from captain and em- ployee to merchant and adventurer. It gave him the means to chart his own course.2 Between 1736 and 1740, Malbone sent three small slaving vessels to Africa under three different captains: one Aldridge, Caleb Godfrey, and Pollipus Hammond. Fate did not smile on these ventures. The first vessel, a schooner carrying eight slaves and some gold dust, was "over-set and intirely lost"; the master and crew alone survived. In early 1740, the last of the three was over and the fifty-five slaves on board drowned; the captain and eight men spent six days, three without food or drink, in the ship's boat. By April of the same year, Malbone had gained his reputation as a Guinea trader.3 The competition cannot have been keen. BY the mid-eighteenth century, Newport, Rhode Island, had become a major North American port, rivaling Bos- ton in its mercantile activity. Ezra Stiles counted sixty shops on the town's main street in 1i761.4 One out of every three buildings was a warehouse or commercial establishment. New- port had in common with other major ports of the Northeast distilleries, frequent rum surpluses, and slaving merchants. Historical legend, built around reputations like Godfrey Malbone's, has placed the colonial slave trade at the center of Newport's commerce, and for Newport, unlike other ports, the legend refuses to die. Even those historians who question the trade's importance to New England maintain its impor- tance to Newport.5 Newport, then, is an appropriate place to begin examining the slave trade's role in New England.
