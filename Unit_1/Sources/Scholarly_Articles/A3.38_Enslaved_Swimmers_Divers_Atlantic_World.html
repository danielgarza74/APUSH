Selection Overview

Dawson argues that many West Africans were skilled swimmers and underwater divers long before enslavement and that they carried these skills to the Americas, where they shaped the work and leisure of the enslaved and often surpassed those of Europeans.

Enslaved Swimmers and Divers in the Atlantic World Kevin Dawson Long before a single coastal or interior West African was enslaved and cargoed off to t the length of his days under the skies of the New World, many had become adept swi mers and underwater divers. West Africans often grew up along riverbanks, near lake close to the ocean. In those waterways, many became proficient swimmers, incorporati this skill into their work and recreation. When carried to the Americas, slaves bro this ability with them, where it helped shape generations of bondpeople's occupat and leisure activities. From the age of discovery up through the nineteenth century, the swimming and un water diving abilities of people of African descent often surpassed those of Europeans their descendants. Indeed, most whites, including sailors, probably could not swim reduce drowning deaths, some philanthropists advocated that sailors and others learn t swim. In 1838 the Sailor's Magazine, a New York City missionary magazine, publi the inscription on a city placard titled "Swimming." It read: "For want of knowled this noble art thousands are annually sacrificed, and every fresh victim calls more str upon the best feelings of those who have the power to draw the attention of such per as may be likely to require this art, to the simple fact, that there is no difficulty in flo or swimming." Similarly, Theodorus Bailey Myers Mason's 1879 pamphlet, The Preserva tion ofLife at Sea, claimed, "The great majority of people cannot swim, and strange as may seem to you, there are many who follow the sea as a profession who cannot swim stroke."
