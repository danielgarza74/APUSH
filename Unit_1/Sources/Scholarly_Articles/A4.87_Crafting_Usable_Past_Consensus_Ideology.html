Selection Overview

Gordon (historiography) argues that historians of America tend to be intellectual nationalists, and that no other epoch has suffered such relentless conservative and consensus interpretation as the Revolution, packaged into a neat and attractive story. He contends that even the debate over ideology explores only the boundaries of the consensus, leaving its validity unquestioned.

Crafting a Usable Past: Consensus, Ideology, and Historians of the American Revolution Colin Gordon H TISTORIANS of America tend to be intellectual nationalists. They are, after all, both students and citizens of the society in which they live. They may feel a filial obligation to that society, a desire to celebrate its traditions and to affirm the relevance of themse and their work. "[T]he sense we make of the history of our national origins," noted Bernard Bailyn, "helps to define for us, as it has for generations before us, the values, purposes, and acceptable characteristics of our political institutions and cultural life." In scholarship, these predilections tempt historians with the notion of a usable past-with the possibility of finding historical relevance and happiness. Historical mem- ory is easily clouded by optimism, apologia, or sentimentality, and the past is too often recalled for present purposes. Celebrations of Jeffersonian or Jacksonian democracy, New Freedom or New Deal, Gilded Age or Great Society, however, all pale beside the historiography of the nation's primal moment. 672 WILLIAM AND MARY QUARTERLY attributed to a brief intrusion of celebratory conservatism in the i950s. This "new conservatism" apparently disappeared with the neo-whig re- vival of the next decade. Bailyn's The Ideological Origins of the American Revolution, published in i967, became the new paradigm for Revolution- ary studies and the catalyst for a "republican synthesis" of the early national period. Recent commentators have credited Bailyn with invigo- rating (or even transcending) the old behaviorist-idealist debate; they have also hailed neo-whig interpretation as a departure that owes little to its conservative progenitors.2 A reconsideration of Bailyn's role in the development of Revolutionary historiography is germane to a fuller understanding of neo-whig interpretation, to the current debate over republicanism, and to the struggle, in recent years, to transcend not only the conclusions but the very terms of the Bailyn paradigm. The neo-whig relationship to what may be called the "conservative- consensus" school is one of continuity rather than conflict. "Since roughly the end of World War II," acknowledged one neo-whig historian, "we have witnessed a resumed and increasingly heightened insistence on the primary significance of conscious beliefs."3 Fascination with the role of ideas or beliefs characterizes both conservative and neo-whig history, and illuminates their shared tradition. Emphasis on the causal force of ideology assumes that historical experience is rational and conscious-that consid- erable thought lies behind every act. Emphasis on consensus assumes that historical experience may be understood in terms of resolution rather than conflict-that behind rhetorical wrangling or distracting divisions, com- mon purpose was the leitmotif of early American society. Of course, ideology itself need not be "rational" (witness Bailyn's paradigm of paranoia), and in this sense the neo-whigs are concerned less with forethought than with mere articulation. But the cohesive and causal force of ideas is a staple of conservative and neo-whig analysis alike. Bailyn and his followers (such as Gordon S.
