Selection Overview

Burke argues that events at the frontier village of Schenectady show that Leisler’s Rebellion had wide and lasting local effects, and that Anglo-Dutch ethnic hostility alone does not explain the divisions it produced.

Thomas Burke, former research coordinator for the Colonial Albany Social History Project, is the 1988 recipient of the Hendricks Manuscript Award. In the spring of 1689, with Britain in constitutional turmoil following the flight to France of James Π, and William and Mary not yet enthroned, disaffected subjects in New York and New England seized control from royal appointees. Massachusetts of ficials placed Governor Edmund Andros under arrest at Boston, while in New York, after some hesitation, Jacob Leisler, a captain of militia, commandeered that colony's government. Historians of early New York have long presumed that this uprising, "Leisler's Rebellion," was an event that focused most significantly on New York City and adjacent areas of Long Island and Westchester Coun ty. One historian has even written that, "there is little evidence that New York communities were ever paralyzed by dissension, except in New York City and King's County during Leisler's Rebellion." More recently, the rebellion has been portrayed as the result of Anglo-Dutch ethnic hostility and economic competition which developed during the period prior to 1689. In addition, the revolt is recognized as having initiated a period of extended pro vincial political factionalism, although most analysts conclude that provincial dissension dissipated shortly after the arrival of Gover nor Robert Hunter in 1710.1 1. New York History An examination of events at the frontier, farming community of Schenectady compels a reassessment of these conclusions. Ther was in fact a sharp division within the community betwee Leislerians and anti-Leislerians. The career of the most prom nent Leislerian, Reyer Schermerhorn, and the actions of the ne work of individuals who supported or opposed him in 1690 and after, reveals the serious and extended impact of Leisler's Rebellion on this remote Mohawk Valley village. The events of 1689-90 crystallized dissension within the com munity and established a long-lasting pattern for dispute. In tota the evidence suggests that Leisler's Rebellion was not solely a Ne York City-based phenomena, and that, at least for the frontier com munity at Schenectady, the impact of the events of these years was divisive and enduring. The issue of land ownership and acquisi tion was fundamental. However, the conflict was played out, no between a constellation of English and Dutch antagonists, but rather between groups of "ins" and "outs"—first settlers and later arrivals —seeking access to the one resource that mattered—land. Land ownership and disputations thereof—essential factors i the division within Schenectady in 1690 and after—were a centr theme in the community's history in the years immediate preceding Leisler's Rebellion. Schenectady was founded in 1661 by fourteen proprietors and their families, led by Arent van Curler These individuals removed from established communities at Bever wyck (Albany) and Rensselaerwyck and received properties situated along the south bank of the Mohawk River, with the farm lands located on low-lying flats to the west of the village itself.
