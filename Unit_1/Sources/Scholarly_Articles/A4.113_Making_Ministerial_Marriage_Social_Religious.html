Selection Overview

Hochstetler argues that the move in late-seventeenth-century Massachusetts from civil to minister-officiated weddings, which resembled Anglican practice, did not abandon Puritan values and gave colonists a connection to the old ways during the political upheaval of the Dominion of New England.

The wedding - private, devoid of religious symbols and objects, and of- ficiated by a civil authority - exemplified the Puritan, as opposed to the Anglican, ideal of marriage and family. In the colony's first five decades, all Massachusetts men and women entered into marriage through similarly secular ceremonies. By the start of the new cen- tury, however, Bay colonists' weddings looked remarkably different from those of their grandparents. Couples recited their vows be- fore a minister and surrounded by friends and family, and festive celebrations of food and music or visits to neighbors to receive con- gratulations followed. These public, religious affairs bore a strong resemblance to the traditional Anglican Church nuptials that Puri- tans renounced when migrating to Massachusetts. Yet, in embracing minister-officiated church weddings, colonists were not abandoning their values. In the charged political atmosphere of late seventeenth- century Massachusetts, the innovation of ministerial marriage iron- ically provided a connection to the old ways. Life in New England, and in the Bay Colony in particular, re- volved around the concept of the covenant - a promise between par- ties to fulfill an obligation to one another. Covenants formed the basis for Massachusetts Puritans' most important relationships, in- cluding that between the individual and God, between God and the gathered church, and between God and society.
