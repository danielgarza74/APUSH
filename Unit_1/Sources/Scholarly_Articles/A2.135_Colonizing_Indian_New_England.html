Neal Salisbury The Colonizing of Indian New England FOR MOST OF US who still think about history at all, "colonial New England" means "Puritan New England." The region's history, we were taught, begins with the struggles of the Pilgrims in the winter of 1620-21, and acquires true momentum with the "Great Migration" of the 1630s. Whether we admire the settlers for their dedication to both principle and practicality, or are repelled by their seemingly neurotic intolerance and cruelty, we tend to equate New England's beginnings with the arrival of pious, self sufficient families who made the region itself pious and self sufficient. To those unfamiliar with New England, Indians rarely enter this picture at all. (Squanto, legendary "friend of the Pilgrims" whose name was once a household word, is no longer a fixture in American history textbooks.) Those who have spent time in the region recognize many of its place names as "Indian" (the lan guage is actually Algonquian), and some know a story or two, as often as not apocryphal, behind them. To the extent that they conceptualize the history of Indian-English relations at all, it can usually be summed up in the rhyme which one Yankee historian recalls from his childhood: the settlers "fell first on their knees and then on the aborigines."l Again, regardless of our particular feel ings toward either Indians or Puritans, we tend to assume that relations between the two peoples were characterized by an unre mitting hostility caused by an unbridgeable social and cultural gap. Though not without factual foundation, this view relies on mental structures which the settlers themselves employed for understanding their experiences. Though we do not share their ideological orientation, we continue to see the history they wrote about from a perspective defined by that orientation. And we tend to think of outsiders?Dutch, French, non-Puritan English, and, most of all, Indians?as figures who existed to heighten the drama of that history at certain points. Finally, we tend to think of New England as somehow constituting an excep tion from the historical processes which accompanied Europe's overseas expansion elsewhere. This essay suggests a different perspective on the early history of colonial New England, one that is bicultural rather than monocul tural. In place of dramatic narrative, it emphasizes contexts, both temporal (the backgrounds of natives and settlers) and spatial (developments outside the region), as well as processes (economic, demographic, cross-cultural). By tracing the interplay of these factors, we can see that for most of the seventeenth century? between the great episodes of conflict and struggle?southern New England was a frontier society in which two very different social worlds co-existed and interacted in ways that decisively shaped the ultimate direction of the region's development. By 1600, the southern, agricultural portion of New England was inhabited by 125,000 or more Algonquian-speaking natives resid ing in village-bands of anywhere from two hundred to two thou sand each. While most of their contacts and exchanges were with one another, the natives of the region also sent agricultural sur pluses, local craft products, and the highly prized wampum shell beads of Long Island Sound to non-farming bands in northern New England and the Maritimes, and received, among other items, copper that originated in the Great Lakes and European glass beads, iron tools, and copper pots?products of the French fur trade which had become regularized in eastern Canada during the preceding generation. Despite the presence of such goods, and even an occasional direct contact in the form of an exploring voyage or fishing vessel, Europe as yet had no discernible effect on life in southern New England.2 Over the next century all that would change; yet while following a variety of strategies in confronting colonization, most Indian groups sought above all to maintain a system of social relations in which autonomous bands provided for their own subsistence through a plurality of food sources, while sealing ties with other communities?be they Indian or English? through reciprocal exchanges and alliances.
