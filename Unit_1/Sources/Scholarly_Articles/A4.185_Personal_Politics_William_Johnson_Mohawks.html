Personal Politics: William Johnson and the Mohawks
Michael J. Mullin
 
ARTICLE Personal Politics: William Johnson and the Mohawks
JOURNAL American Indian Quarterly 17, no. 3 (1993)
EXCERPT Original journal pages 350-351
JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/1184878
Selection Overview

Mullin examines how Anglo-Indian agent William Johnson integrated himself into Six Nations politics through gifts, the sponsorship of the Mohawk leader Hendrick, and marriage to Molly Brant. He rejects the assumption that the Six Nations had no choice but to follow Johnson, arguing that they had other options and followed him because he fit their own policy objectives, while Johnson in turn played on colonial misunderstandings of Iroquois political structure.

PERSONAL POLITICS observers, and later anthropologists and historians, were unable to distinguish between the Iroquois League and the Iroquois Confederacy.10 When Johnson moved the focus of British Indian policy westward, he maintained the Six Nations pre-eminent position in British Indian policy by playing on colonial misconcep- tions about the Iroquois political structure.11 This invention allowed him to maintain that the Iroquois controlled the Ohio Country.12 He could also continue giving the Mohawks a pre-eminent place in Anglo-Indian relations. Throughout his career as Indian superintendent, Johnson's success depended on the relationships he maintained with Indian leaders. During the 1740s and early 1750s, Johnson drew on the support of the Mohawk leader Hendrick. Johnson's political success was the result of an informal arrangement with the Mohawks. They supported Johnson at Confederacy Council meetings while he maintained their political prestige among colonial politicians.i3 Johnson continued to support this arrangement even after he knew the Mohawks claim of primacy among the Six Nations was false. He allowed the Mohawks to maintain this fiction in formal meetings with colonial politicians.14 Without Mohawk support, his influence within Iroquoia diminished.15 One of the reasons why the Mohawks were willing to work with Johnson lies in how he operated. He conducted both business and politics in a manner the Mohawks understood: He cultivated personal alliances. These individual relationships were based upon reciprocal gift-giving, and in this way he established a network of personal alliances. These relationships were first cultivated among the Mohawks, and then later among all of the Six Nations. He built personal connections on a "leader/follower" basis.16 Later, Johnson extended these connections into the Ohio Country. He built these alliances through his gift- giving practices. As Wilbur R.Jacobs observed, the "Indian custom of giving and receiving presents proved to be a decisive factor in the story of Indian diplomacy along the Ohio and Northwest frontiers" during the eighteenth century.17 The evidence suggests thatJohnson used gifts as part ofhis normal business operations before he became officially involved in Indian politics.18 Johnson's leader/follower network probably paralleled Hendrick's own personal network initially. Over time, Johnson's network came to dwarf that of the Mohawk leaders. One reason for the extension ofJohnson's personal network was that he had resources few, if any, Iroquois could match. This was particularly true after 1746, when Johnson officially became involved in Indian affairs.19 In his official capacities Johnson presented the Six Nations gifts on behalf of New York Colony. Because of the New York Assembly's parsimonious attitude,Johnson often provided the Indians presents from his own storehouse.20 Johnson did so because he knew Iroquois alliances depended on presents;21 Johnson had to hope the New York Assembly would reimburse him later. By using his own goods, Johnson could present the gifts in his own name. This practice allowed Johnson to extend his own personal network beyond the Mohawks.22 As the 1750s and 1760s progressed Johnson set himself up as the penultimate gift-giver.
