Blacks as American Loyalists: The Slaves’ War for Independence
James W. St. G. Walker
 
ARTICLE Blacks as American Loyalists: The Slaves’ War for Independence
JOURNAL Historical Reflections / Réflexions Historiques 2, no. 1 (1975)
EXCERPT Original journal pages 51-52
JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/41298659
Selection Overview

Walker argues that the history of black Americans in the Revolution has emphasized Patriot figures such as Crispus Attucks and has lost sight of the many black Loyalists whose interests were closely linked to the British cause. He concludes that, through their actions rather than written ideology, black Loyalists struggled and sacrificed for the full freedom of their people.

Blacks as American Loyalists: The Slaves' War for Independence James W. St. G. Walker University of Waterloo In August 1775 a free black named Jerry, labelled a Tory, was hanged and burned by a Charleston mob on the grounds that he was inciting slaves to rebel against their republican masters. Black Christians in Savannah were whipped and imprisoned when their teaching and preaching activities seemed to encourage the slaves to align them- selves with the British against the interests of the colonial revolution, while a similar belief inspired the "anti-loyalist" George Galphin of South Carolina, owner of the Silver Bluff plantation on which a black Baptist church had recently been formed, to deny free black preachers access to his estate in 1775. 1 In the two hundred years since these incidents took place, the fact that the interests of the blacks and the cause of the Loyalists were often intimately linked has been lost to American history. Instead Crispus Attucks, the first black casualty of the revolutionary conflict at Boston in 1770, and such Patriot soldiers as Peter Salem and Salem Poor, have been regarded as typical of the blacks' response to the division between Britain and her colonies. The martyr, Jerry, who gave his life for black freedom, has been replaced in the lexicon of heroes by other black men who fought for the freedom of white America. Most of the written history of the subject would lead one to believe that the blacks overwhelmingly supported the Patriot side. W. B. Hartgrove, for instance, wrote in a 1916 article: "Feeling the same patriotism which the white man experienced, the Negro bared his breast to the bullet and gave his life as a sacrifice for the liberty of his country." Even Benjamin Quarles, in his scholarly study of The Negro in the American Revolution , pays far more attention to Patrio- tic blacks than to black Loyalists. Quarles' frontispiece, showing a black soldier spearing a British major, reflects the tone that dominates most writing on the subject. For Franklin, the Patriot blacks were fighting to "win the freedom of their country as well as their own," while those who joined the Loyalists were selfishly engaged only in seeking "their own freedom." Though Lerone Bennett, Jr., points out ironi- cally that "black men toiled and fought so that white men could be free," his account of "The Negro in the American Revolution" supports the others in the impression it gives of a war between Britain and America in which the slaves adopted the Patriotic side.2 Historical writing almost always serves or is made to serve some social or political purpose. Harriet Beecher Stowe's introduction to the 1855 edition of William C. Nell' s Colored Patriots of the American Revolution, argued that knowledge of the black man's valour and self-sacrifice in the cause of Revolutionary America "will redeem the character of the race," while Wendell Phillips wrote quite bluntly in the 1852 edition of the same work that "The following pages are an effort to stem the tide of prejudice against the colored race."3 Nell's book was undisguisedly an abolitionist tract; more recent scholarship has had an equally significant purpose. It has apparently been deemed important by liberal American scholars to emphasize the black man's role in the winning of independence for America, since that role can serve as a qualification for full American citizenship and justification for black inclusion in the mainstream of American history. However valid that might be, it is surely unnecessary, and it is even dangerous in its implications; for it suggests that blacks - simply as Americans - do not qualify as heirs to the revolutionary promise, and it seems to assume that only those who fought for American freedom deserve a share in it.
