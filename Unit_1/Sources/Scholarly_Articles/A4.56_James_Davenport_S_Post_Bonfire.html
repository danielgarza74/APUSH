Selection Overview

Cray argues that after the 1743 New London bonfire and his 1744 public apology, the revivalist James Davenport became a calmer minister who has been neglected by historians drawn to his sensational career. He finds that Davenport struggled to reposition himself within the clerical mainstream in the subdued religious climate of the late 1740s and 1750s, gaining acceptance among Presbyterian colleagues but not among many parishioners.

James Davenport's Post-Bonfire Ministry, 1743-1757 Robert Ε. Cray, Jr. Few events excited more commotion in New England during the eighteenth-cen tury religious revivals known as the Great Awakening than the New London bonfire of 6 March 1743 engineered by the radical New Light minister, James Davenport. An impassioned crowd of laypersons, attempting to rid themselves of "their idolatrous love of worldly thing[s]," gathered at Davenport's command a number of personal possessions, along with tomes authored by Puritan divines he considered unsound. The books they carried to the town wharf and cast into a bon fire. Shouts of "Hallelujah" and "Praise to God" filled the air, and the smoke from the books prompted some persons to proclaim that the deceased authors now roasted in hell for their errors.1 People leaving a nearby meetinghouse looked on in horror at the scene. The next day Davenport urged his followers to burn their fine clothes, wigs, rings, and necklaces—anything that they had turned into an "idol." He even contributed his own plush breeches to the pile, according to one account. Although additional books went into the flames that day and the pile of personal possessions awaited the torch, persons in the crowd suddenly called a halt to the proceedings, and shame and embarrassment quickly subdued the assemblage. News of the affair caused Old and New Light clerics to forget their differences and roundly condemn Davenport. The Long Island minister, who had fostered divisions as well as revivals through his long sermons and attacks on rival ministers, had finally overreached himself with his pyrotechnic activism. In 1744 a contrite Davenport asked forgiveness in a public apology.2 The retraction signaled a dramatic change in the once fiery minister. His new ministry exhibited a much calmer demeanor. Laboring primarily in New Jersey, the post-bonfire Davenport has been neglected by historians, who have usually focused on the sensational aspects of his revivalist career. Yet Davenport did not really disappear from view; rather, he strug gled to reposition himself within the clerical mainstream, constrained, as were even leading evangelicals, by the more subdued religious climate of the late 1740s and 1750s. Even the "Grand Itinerant," revivalist George Whitefield, was unable to repli cate the fervor of the Awakening in the northeast, despite the large crowds he attracted. Davenport faced an even greater obstacle: he had to convince fellow min isters and laypersons that the zealous enthusiast of book-burning fame, who had been judged insane by two tribunals in Connecticut and Massachusetts in 1742, merited a pulpit. Inevitably, Davenport's past career hampered his efforts at employment, leaving him with limited and uncertain prospects.3 The later career of James Davenport illuminates the dynamics of ministerial rep utation after the waning of the Great Awakening in the northern colonies. Neither the way in which one-time radicals fared in its wake nor the subsequent relation ship of once active revivalists to their colleagues and the laity has been fully explored. The Awakening empowered congregations to think for themselves and act independently of ecclesiastical authority, sometimes with dire consequences for ministers. Leading figures such as Jonathan Edwards and Gilbert Tennent reassessed their fervent embrace of revivalism, conscious of the schismatic divisions it spawned. But less information exists about the second-tier revivalists such as Davenport, Andrew Croswell, Timothy Allen, and Jonathan Barber, who, like their better-known counterparts, helped reshape the ecclesiastical landscape.
