The New York Slave Conspiracy of 1741 as Black Protest
Thomas J. Davis
 
ARTICLE The New York Slave Conspiracy of 1741 as Black Protest
JOURNAL The Journal of Negro History 56, no. 1 (1971)
EXCERPT Original journal pages 17-18
JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/2716023
Selection Overview

Davis argues that the events of New York's 1741 conspiracy are best understood as black protest against enslavement, a protest that continued and grew confrontational because the grievance could not be remedied. He cautions that Daniel Horsmanden, who compiled the trial record, was prejudiced, and concludes that some blacks reached a point of confrontation in 1741 but that it is doubtful any collective culmination was reached or even planned.

THE NEW YORK SLAVE CONSPIRACY OF 1741 AS BLACK PROTEST by Thomas J. Davis Thomas J. Davis is Director of Afro-American Studies at Manhattanville College, Purchase, New York. Protest comes in various sizes and shapes characterized only by the nature of their contents and the results they produce. Often its focus is narrow, reflected in a demand to stop a particular abuse or malpractice such as an excessive or unjust tax, corruption, misrepresentation, lack of service, misuse of funds or authority, crime, poverty - any circumstance or condition, either real or imagined, that is considered unjust and ground for complaint or resentment. More than complaint or resentment, though, protest usually suggests action not only to express grievance, but also to get redress.1 By intending to redress a grievance, protest usually suggests reform and ends usually when that is accomplished. There are situations, however, where redress of a particular grievance does not or cannot bring a remedy for complaint or resentment; hence, protest continues, often growing progressively so that it becomes confrontation, intentionally or not. An example of this is provided by a series of events associated with the New York Conspiracy of 1741.2 As the new year of 1741 turned on the old calendar, rumor in New York had it that many of the more than 2,000 slaves, and some other persons, had conspired to burn down the town and to murder most of its approximately 9,000 white inhabitants.3 The possibility of such a plot gave New Yorkers pause 1 Several of the studies in Joseph R. Proof of conspiracy often makes for controversy because evidence of only a few concrete facts is required for a conviction. As the word "conspiracy" suggests, those charged with it stand accused of planning in secret, and often the details of those plans do not come fully to public knowledge. Thus, most of the weight of accusation hangs on indirect evidence and implication - guilt by association. Frequently such proceedings leave more than a few reasonable doubts.5 Many persons in 1741 had theirs. To them and to many persons since then, such a scheme as the alleged conspiracy seemed horribly silly. It was considered absurd that slaves, even with some help, could have executed successfully such a plot. But it was established to the satisfaction of a Court that a plot had existed, and so, thirteen blacks were burned at the stake, sixteen were hanged along with four whites, and seventy-one were deported from New York.6 Even so, the nature of the evidence - as reflected in an indictment for conspiracy - allowed conclusions contrary to the findings of the Court easy to make. Consequently, many persons have cast doubts so as to make almost everything connected with the conception of conspiracy ridiculous. In fact, many persons have written off the episode as mass hysteria.7 Admittedly many of the whites in the city at the time acted with emotional excitement and, perhaps, with excessive anxiety. Also, the legal proceedings may seem somewhat paranoiac.
