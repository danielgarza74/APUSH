Selection Overview

Norton uses 145 seventeenth-century Maryland defamation suits to study the lives and values of the ordinary, largely illiterate colonists, and contrasts patterns of sexual slander there with Essex County, Massachusetts, where the sex ratio was more balanced.

Gender and Defamation in Seventeenth-Century Maryland Mary Beth Norton O N February 26, i637/8, James Cloughton, a sailor, told the Maryland provincial court that although he was "alwaies of good fame and honest repute hitherto," a planter named Anthony Cotton had slandered him by declaring that "if he [Cotton] had pleased h might have had him [Cloughton] whippt at virginea; and that if he [Cloughton] had had his deserts he had beene hanged in new England." More than four years later, John and Restituta Hollis came to the same court to complain that Thomas Boys had defamed Restituta by saying "that he would prove her ... a whore, in Court"; they demanded "satisfaction for her fame." These first two defamation suits filed in Maryland had inconclusive results: Cloughton eventually withdrew his complaint, and there is no record of a judgment in the Hollises' action against Boys.1 But Restituta Hollis and James Cloughton were only the first of a large number of seventeenth-century Marylanders who, after being insulted, sought to restore their good names through court action. One hundred forty-five defamation suits filed and pursued in the colony during the century provide historians with an extraordinary opportunity to study many details of the lives and values of the ordinary, illiterate folk who composed the bulk of the population of early Maryland.2 Ms. Norton is a member of the Department of History, Cornell University. 4 WILLIAM AND MARY QUARTERLY Defamation cases in other locales in the early modern world have begun to attract the attention of historians, some of whom have studied defamation as one of a number of community interactions while others have concentrated on speech and language or on the development of Anglo-American laws and legal practices regarding slander. Most have recognized that there are consistent patterns of gender differences in defamatory statements, but none have made those differences the central focus of their work.3 And yet, in Maryland at least, sex differences make defamation cases stand out dramatically from other types of civil litigation. Analysis of a sample of approximately 2,000 civil cases heard in the Maryland courts during the seventeenth century shows that women appeared in only i9 period before i670. Because defamed persons' access to the courts was sharply limited after i67I (see discussion below), I have not consulted the later county records that exist only in manuscript. Five of the cases discussed here, all of them post-i668, were technically brought as criminal prosecutions, but for easier analysis they are handled in this article as though they were civil suits brought by the defamed person.
