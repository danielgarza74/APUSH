Selection Overview

Tolles argues that Quaker contacts with American Indians over three centuries have been, on the whole, nonviolent and motivated by friendship, sympathy, understanding, and respect, from Josiah Coale in 1658 to the 1869–1879 "Peace Policy." He notes that as far as the Friends could control the contacts they were constructive mutual interactions, and observes that they were far from the only contacts, many of which were bloody.

NONVIOLENT CONTACT: THE QUAKERS AND THE INDIANS FREDERICK B. TOLLES Howard M. Jenkins Professor of Quaker History and Research; Director, Friends Historical Library, Swarthmore College (Read November 9, 1962, in the Symposium on Our Contacts with American Indians, Polynesians, and Africans) CONTACTS between peoples of different tra- ditions and cultures are often violent. The vio- lence may be physical or psychological; it may take the form of bloodshed and murder or it may result in subtler forms of destruction-emotional disturbance and cultural collapse. Whatever its outcome, the violence is often, one might say, is usually present, and the consequences are dis- astrous to both sides. For three centuries, all across the North Ameri- can continent, the Religious Society of Friends has been making contacts with the American Indians. But the Quaker contacts have been of a special kind: they have been, on the whole, non- violent contacts. To put it more positively, they have been motivated and animated by friendship, sympathy, understanding, respect, love. So far as the Friends could control the contacts, they have been, not damaging collisions, but construc- tive mutual interactions. Three hundred years ago, Josiah Coale, an English Quaker, after being beaten and im- prisoned by the New England Puritans, found a quiet refuge among the Algonquin Indians. "The Quakers are honest men and do no harm," said the Indian chief, "and this is no Englishman's sea or land, and Quakers shall come here and welcome." Coale was deeply moved by the friend- liness of the Indians. "I do confess this to be the Lord's hand of love towards me," he wrote; "through the goodness of the Lord we found these Indians more sober and Christian-like to- wards us than the Christians so called." 1 That was in 1658. A hundred years later, John Woolman, who had "for many years felt love in [his] heart towards the natives of this land," undertook a dangerous journey in war- time to the Indians of Wyalusing on the upper Susquehanna. While he was there, preaching and praying, he heard one of the Indians saying 1 Bowden, James, The history of the Society of Friends in America 1: 125, London, 1850. in his own tongue to the interpreter: "I love to feel where words come from." 2 Another hundred years and Friends in large numbers were in the West, manning the Indian agencies among the Comanches, the Cheyennes, the Pawnees, the Kickapoos, and the other na- tions of Kansas, Nebraska, and the Indian Terri- tory. For ten years, from 1869 to 1879, Friends administered the Northern and Central Super- intendencies for the federal government under President Grant's "Peace Policy." Evidence could easily be cited to show that the men and women who left their homes in the East to be- come Superintendents, Agents, and schoolteachers did so because of a genuine religious concern and that they evoked in their charges a warm and friendly response.3 Even now the concern is still active. The American Friends Service Committee is at work among Indians in Arizona, California, Colorado, North Dakota, Oregon, and Washington. And the Indian Committee of Philadelphia Yearly Meeting, which has been interested in the Seneca Indians of Pennsylvania and New York for more than a century and a half, has been trying vainly to protect their reservation near Kinzua from the federal government, the same govern- ment that gave them their lands in 1794 and promised by treaty "never" to reclaim the lands "nor disturb the Seneca nation." That struggle being lost, the Friends are now doing what they can to help the Senecas through the difficult period of relocation and adjustment to new sur- roundings. 94 FREDERICK B. TOLLES [PROC. AMER. PHIL. SOC. "Tradition of conscience." Is that it? Is that why Quakers have so consistently befriended the Indians throughout their history? Conscience, one might point out, is no monopoly of Friends, and conscience in its basic sense is too much an individual faculty to be transmitted over the years by tradition. One can refer to the Quaker ''peace testimony," the religious pacifism of Friends, as the explanation. But this too, though in some sense passed on by tradition, is at bot- tom an individual conviction, not uniformly shared by all Friends. The basic reason, I believe, is theological. It springs from the central Quaker belief in the Light Within, the measure of God's spirit which is in every man. Most modern Friends would leave the matter there, content that they had found the reason for Quaker humaneness and humanitarianism. If all men are children of God, if all are equally illuminated, actually or potentially, by the Divine Spirit, one should have respect for all men, regardless of the color of their skins or the color of their ideologies. A thoroughly logical argument. Yet actually, as Henry J. Cadbury has made clear, it is not so simple as that. The early Friends, at any rate, "were moved by an inner impulse, to satisfy which they avoided bearing arms, taking oaths, holding slaves," and, I may add, maltreating the Indians. "The effect of their action on others they could hardly ignore, but their own 'clearness,' to use a well-known Quaker term, was their impelling ambition." Their first impulsion was purely inward, subjective, uni- lateral, if you like-a search for peace of mind. The actions so undertaken had valuable social consequences, to be sure, but it was the motive, not any advance calculation of those consequences, that set the action on foot. John Woolman put it this way: Traveling up and down of late, I have had renewed evidences that to be faithful to the Lord, and content with his will concerning me, is a most necessary and useful lesson for me to be learning; looking less at the effects of my labor than at the pure motion and reality of the concern, as it arises from heavenly love.5 When the early Friends reconmmended social ac- tion, Henry Cadbury concludes: "they were not thinking of the Light within others, within the object or recipient of their enlightened behaviour, 5Journal, 69-70. but within themselves." And he adds: "I know of no mention of the Light Within others as a motive for our own action." 6 Nevertheless, there was one way in which ref- erence to external factors, to other human beings, figured in the impulse to Quaker social activity. It is Henry Cadbury again who has called this to our attention. He quotes George Fox, the founder of Quakerism: "Be patterns, be ex- amples . . . that your carriage and life may preach among all sorts of people. . . . Then you will come to walk cheerfully over the world, answering that of God in every one." 7 Again and again Fox uses that phrase, "answering that of God in everyone." In the context of the passage I just quoted it is clear what Fox meant: let your lives be exemplary, let them preach; if you do that, your conduct will "answer, that is, correspond to and appeal to an inner witness in other persons." This witness provides "not so much a motive or a sanction . . . as a corrobora- tion." 8 Often Fox uses this phrase in speaking of the heathen, though in so doing he was at odds with contemporary theology. One recalls that he wrote religious epistles to the Cham of Tartary, to the Emperor of China, to the Great Turk, and to Prester John, the mythical ruler of the East (though the latter's address was so uncertain, and had been so uncertain for almost five cen- turies, that it was rather difficult to deliver the letter to him). One also recalls that other Quakers made mighty efforts to convince the Pope at Rome and the Sultan near Adrianople by appearing in their presence and preaching the gospel of the Light Within. It is not recorded that any of those men became Quakers, but it was not for want of honest and vigorous efforts. The Indians and perhaps the Negro slaves in the American colonies were the principal groups of non-Christians with whom the Friends came into contact.
