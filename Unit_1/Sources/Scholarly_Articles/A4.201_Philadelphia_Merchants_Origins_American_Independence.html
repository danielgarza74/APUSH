Selection Overview

Oaks describes Philadelphia's merchants as the most important occupational group in the struggle against British policies in the 1760s and early 1770s. He notes their complaints about the Revenue and Currency Acts of 1764 and the constitutional objections some of them raised, while showing that these measures at first did not outweigh the bitter internal Pennsylvania struggle between the Proprietary and Assembly factions.

PHILADELPHIA MERCHANTS AND THE ORIGINS OF AMERICAN INDEPENDENCE ROBERT F. OAKS* Assistant Professor of History, University of Texas, Arlington EARLY IN 1764, Joshua Fisher, a wealthy Quaker merchant in Philadelphia, received a let- ter from his son Thomas in London. The young man told his father about the ministry's financial problems. Most Englishmen agreed, the young Philadelphian reported, "that North America should be brought in for Snacks." The govern- ment, in the wake of Pontiac's Rebellion, proposed to send fourteen regiments to America to be paid and maintained by the colonists themselves. "It is certainly an Innovation on our Charter of Privi- ledges," wrote Thomas, since the proposed man- ner of raising the money was a stamp duty.' Young Fisher's warning of the impending Stamp Act and his fears for the rights and lib- erties of Americans were among the first of many similar objections which would be raised to British policy over the next decade. Throughout the struggle which ultimately led to independence, merchants in America's largest city played a central role in bringing Pennsylvania to Revolu- tion. Though as a group they increasingly dis- agreed among themselves, especially after 1770, merchants remained the most important occupa- tional group in the struggle against British policies throughout the decade. Though the Stamp Act was by far the most volatile, it was not the first objectionable act passed since the end of the French and Indian War. Privately, some merchants complained about the new Revenue Act's duties on previously free items and the attempt to favor West Indian sugar interests at the expense of North America. Postwar business conditions were bad enough already without addi- tional restrictions. They complained even more vigorously about the Currency Act's outlawing of paper money. Since Pennsylvania's paper currency had been stable for years, Philadel- phians wondered why their colony should be penalized for the poor fiscal policies of others. The act, some merchants predicted, would hurt economies on both sides of the Atlantic. Some Americans also expressed constitutional objec- tions. One Philadelphian of English birth was so upset by the new restrictions, which deprived him "of the general Rights of his Birth," that he re- solved to wear "Russet-grey" rather than "send home one Farthing to purchase the Commodities of England." There were even questions at this early date about the propriety of Parliament's passing laws for America, "so that near Two Mil- lions of People are subj ect to home Decisions without a single yea or nay." 2 Neither of these previous acts, however, pro- duced any organized protest. The outgoing Penn- sylvania Assembly, in the fall of 1764, requested their agent Richard Jackson to seek repeal or amendment of the Revenue Act and to work against passage of the proposed Stamp Act. Proprietary sup- porters tried to use the issue of British taxation to their advantage, placing Benjamin Franklin, then busily circulating petitions to enhance his campaign for royal government, in an awkward position. Franklin, as leader of the Assembly party, needed the support of the British govern- ment to accomplish his goals and did not want to jeopardize that support by backing a strong pro- test against the Stamp Act. His opponents, on the other hand, aided by John Dickinson, wrote pamphlets and counterpetitions pointing out that the Assembly party was trying to surrender the province into the hands of the very people who had recently begun enforcing the Navigation Acts, outlawed paper money, closed western lands to settlement, passed the Revenue Act, and now threatened the Stamp Act. The success of their strategy is difficult to determine, but, though the Assembly party retained its majority, the two major leaders, Franklin and Joseph Galloway, lost their seats in the 1764 election.4 But aside from this partisan use of the Stamp Act for local political purposes and mild protests such as Thomas Fisher's letter to his father, initial reaction in Philadelphia was generally one of indifference. By early May, 1765, every issue of Philadelphia newspapers carried news of the act. When Pro- prietary leaders learned that John Hughes, a wealthy ex-baker and political associate of Frank- lin and Galloway, had been named stamp distribu- tor for Pennsylvania, they accused the Assembly party leaders of participating in a plot to go far beyond their original objective of making Penn- sylvania a royal colony. Franklin's opponents charged him with using the act to extend his per- sonal control over the province and of supporting the act's passage in the first place with this ob- jective in mind.6 The early opposition remained primarily politi- cal, rather than philosophical or constitutional, but as opposition in other colonies such as Massa- chusetts and Virginia became more pronounced, Philadelphians too began to see larger implica- tions. Yet the generally conservative tone of the Quaker city, coupled with the Assembly party's desire to hold down visible dissent, dampened opposition. The Assembly waited until the last minute to appoint a committee to represent the colony in the Stamp Act Congress, held in New York to present a united colonial opposition.
