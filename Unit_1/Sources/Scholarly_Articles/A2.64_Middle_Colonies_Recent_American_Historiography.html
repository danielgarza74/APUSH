Selection Overview

Greenberg (historiography) argues that the Middle Colonies have drawn less scholarly attention because their ethnic, religious, economic, and political diversity gives them no single pattern, and he concludes that recent scholarship shows a region marked by diversity, conflict, and instability. He notes that two books argue for calm and harmony in Pennsylvania but says their evidence is not entirely appropriate to the conclusions.

Notes and Documents The Middle Colonies in Recent American Historiography Douglas Greenberg IT has become something of a cliche among early American historians that a preponderant share of scholarship has been devoted to New England and the South at the expense of the Middle Colonies. Indeed, this relative lack of interest in the Middle Colonies has been a curious phenomenon. Certainly, these settlements were not devoid of personalities. William Penn, Benjamin Franklin, Lewis Morris, and William Livingston, to name but four, possessed qualities that have attracted the attention of historians. Nor were the Middle Colonies without their episodes of high drama. Leisler's Rebellion, the New York and NewJersey agrarian distur- bances, and the march of the Paxton Boys, to say nothing of the crucial role of the Middle Colonies in the War for Independence, rival anything to be found in the history of provinces to the north or south. Yet New York, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania even now do not loom as large in the literature as do their neighbors, nor, it may be surmised, do they occupy a central place in courses on colonial history. In part, of course, this is because men from New England and Virginia acquired greater fame for their roles in the Revolution while contemporaries regarded the Middle Colonies as loyalist strongholds- and the biases of the generation of Adams and Jefferson continue to affect our understanding of the pre-Revolutionary era. In addition, the longer histories of the Southern and New England colonies, reaching almost to the beginning of the seventeenth century, have been at least partially responsible for their dominance in our bibliographies and syllabi. But most significant, the Middle Colonies have drawn-less notice because they were-and are-so much more difficult to grapple with than their neighbors. Among the southern settlements, the issues of slavery and aristocratic control inform much of what historians have written. These two themes run clearly through the literature and give it coherence. Moreover, since issues of class and race have been so central throughout American history, scholars have been naturally attracted to the southern colonies where, it is assumed, the colonial roots of these national problems may be found. Students of New England and the South thus benefit from having an established set of significant problems on which their work can center. For the Middle Colonies it is more difficult to isolate unifying themes or identify seminal works of scholarship. Not that race, class, or religion had no impact upon the history of these provinces; in fact, these factors were more divisive than in New England or the South precisely because none of them dominated or controlled the flow of provincial life. The point to be recog- nized at the outset, therefore, is that the Middle Colonies did not exhibit any single historical pattern or group of patterns upon which a ready inter- pretation may be based. The complexity can be bewildering. Populated by an extraordinarily large number of ethnic and religious groups, economically diversified, politically fragmented, and socially heterogeneous, New York, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania have lacked their Perry Millers and Wesley Frank Cravens. Yet it is this very diversity that has enlivened recent studies; for fashions do change, and today we are more likely to find our colleagues echoing Milton M. Klein's trend-setting suggestion of several years ago that the experience of New York and, by implication, the other mid-Atlantic colonies more completely reflected the varied character of colonial life and more fully prefigured the nineteenth-century nation than the experience of either New England or the South.' Some may question Klein's argument, but there can be no denying that the Middle Colonies have become a focus of intense interest in the past decade.
