Selection Overview

Wilson uses the American Revolution as a case study in how historians interpret religion, noting that successive generations of scholars have found it variously in patriot preachers, frustrated Episcopal loyalists, and marginalized radicals (historiography). He proposes a "kaleidoscope" approach to religion and revolution as reciprocal exchange within a common culture, and finds religion more consequential over the longer term than in the immediate struggle.

Wilson Religion and Revolution in American History Depending upon how defined and opened, "religion and revolu- tion in American history" serves as a case study illuminating the general topic: how shall we interpret religion as a historial subject? For religion is a specialized aspect of culture which purports to offer nonfalsifiable meaning about all that occurs (history) not to speak of all that is (nature). The American Revolution is partic- ularly useful as a historical location for such an inquiry because successive generations of scholars have explored how religion was implicated in the struggle for independence. Schweitzer's discov- ery, in the course of his "Quest of the Historical Jesus," that interpreters have, like proverbial gazers into wells, chiefly found their own images reflected in the watery depths applies here as well. And these images have varied from patriot preachers to frustrated episcopal loyalists to marginalized radicals.' Our inquiry begins with some elementary observations. "Re- ligion" typically designates those aspects of a culture that set terms of reference for collective (as well as individual) human life. Whether studied as beliefs or rituals, or as institutions or mean- ings, such terms of reference set a "horizon" of consciousness. They are cultural givens and their religious quality is taken for granted, putting them virtually beyond examination. When pu- tatively religious actions (such as rituals) or beliefs (such as doc- trines) become self-conscious, in the extreme when taken as sub- jects of inquiry, they lose that status and become matters of aesthetic taste or intellectual satisfaction. 598 | JOHN F. WILSON qualities take flight as they are objectified in consciousness. To identify a phenomenon as "religious" refers to the relationships in which it is embedded not to a quality that inheres without respect to setting. Religion presents itself so to speak, as beyond review, with decisive powers attributed to it. But analytically considered, its religious quality arises from the relationships and is not independent of them. Thus religion cannot be viewed as a bounded and differentiated cultural reality-to be isolated and examined without regard to the relationships that confer its reli- gious quality. Revolution is a cultural construct in marked tension with religion. One makes references to more fundamental values or truths-even when the present seems unendurable and religion's only payoff consolation-whereas "revolution' conceptualizes the sharpest kind of political break in human community. It denom- inates the breakdown and displacement (indeed replacement) of authority; it signifies purging a social order, self-consciously sub- stituting new patterns that are untainted by association with old regimes. We perceive revolutions as taking place in stages or developing over time. But the defining qualities represent a virtual antithesis to religion. In modern Western societies, "revolution" is primarily lo- cated in discourse about politics as distinct from religion, making evident the cultural distance between the terms. The conventional separation of religion and politics, which on the whole has served the modern West well, should not obscure the tension between these concepts. If revolution means the overthrow of a regime by any means (that is, old rules no longer apply), such a notion throws into high relief the typical interdependence of regime and religion. Insofar as revolutionary movements seek to mobilize religious motivations and ideals, they confirm the reciprocal, if tension-filled, relationship between religion and revolution. To fruitfully explore the topic, I distinguish several different frames of time in which religion, in its various aspects, interacts with the dissolving legitimacy of the British regime in the North American colonies. Religion has a greater potential influence in proportion to its distance from the central political events of revolution. This follows from the defining characteristic of reli- gion: its role in codifying cultural horizons.
