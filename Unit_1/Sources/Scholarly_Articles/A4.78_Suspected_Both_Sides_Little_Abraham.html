‘Suspected on Both Sides’: Little Abraham, Iroquois Neutrality, and the American Revolution
Caitlin A. Fitz
 
ARTICLE ‘Suspected on Both Sides’: Little Abraham, Iroquois Neutrality, and the American Revolution
JOURNAL Journal of the Early Republic 28, no. 3 (2008)
EXCERPT Original journal pages 299-300
JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/40208153
Selection Overview

Fitz takes up the Mohawk chief Little Abraham, perhaps the most prominent Iroquois neutral, whom she finds as understudied and misunderstood as the neutrality he advocated. She notes that historians have stressed the Iroquois shift from neutrality to warfare, with the Oneidas and Tuscaroras siding with the Americans and the other nations mostly with the British, and she turns attention to the neutral position that Little Abraham and others sustained.

"Suspected on Both Sides" Little Abraham, Iroquois Neutrality, and the American Revolution CAITLIN A. FITZ "This, then, is the determination of the Six Nations" the Mohawk chief Little Abraham told an assembly of New York Whigs in 1775. "Not to take any part, but, as it is a family affair, to sit still and see you fight it out ... for we bear as much affection for the King of England's subjects, upon the other side of the water, as we do for you, born upon this island."1 Such was the position that Little Abraham espoused at the beginning of the Revolution, and such was the position that he and many others would sustain throughout the conflict. But despite having been perhaps the most prominent Iroquois neutral of the Revolution, Little Abraham has been as understudied and as misunderstood as the neutrality that he advocated. Historians have long noted that most Iroquois sought neu- trality early in the war, but that they eventually took up arms as the conflict burned its way through New York. Most Oneidas and Tuscaroras sided with the Americans, and most Mohawks, Onondagas, Cayugas, and Senecas sided with the British. Indeed, Little Abraham rarely gets more than a brief reference before the spotlight shifts to mili- tary partisans like the Mohawk warrior Joseph Brant. Iroquois neutrality appears as an ephemeral stage in the Revolution, doomed to failure and thus leftrelatively unexamined.2 But Abraham's experiences merit another look, and forseveral rea- sons. First, they illuminate a critical era of eighteenth-century diplomacy, suggesting that support forneutrality was stronger than most scholars have thought. Driven by kinship ties, political considerations, and mili- tary expediency, I will argue, Abraham gradually and grudgingly decided to perform diplomatic work forthe Americans in order to remain mili- tarily neutral. But instead of rendering him innocuous, Abraham's pur- suit of neutrality through a limited American alliance ultimately lefthim vulnerable to the wrath of Whigs and Loyalists alike. Indeed, driven by the exigencies and horrors of war, most people in Abraham's community of Tiononderoga eventually fled to British strongholds like Niagara and Montreal. But Abraham stayed, determined to protect his own lands and those of his community. In the process, he retained a great deal of sup- port from the people who remained at Tiononderoga, from other Iro- quois, and even from many of those who had left.Far from an anomaly, Abraham's message of neutrality resonated among many Iroquois and deserves more attention. 2. The best account of the Iroquois during the Revolution remains Barbara Graymont's The Iroquois in the American Revolution (Syracuse, NY, 1972). For native people's wartime experiences throughout North America, see Colin G. Calloway's The American Revolution in Indian Country: Crisis and Diversity in Native American Communities (New York, 1995). Both authors mention Abra- ham. Stone, Life ofJoseph Brant- Thayendanegea, Including the Border Wars of the American Revolution (2 vols., New York, 1838), 1: 147-48. Rethinking Iroquois Participation in the American Revolution," Explorations in Early American Culture 4 (2000), 148-65.
