GOALS AND FUNCTIONS OF PURITAN MISSIONS TO THE INDIANS* by Francis Jennings Cedar Crest College ABSTRACT Some modem historians have accepted uncritically the Puritans' own evaluation of their function as agents of altruism with respect to American Indians. A more careful examination of the data, especially those concerning John Eliot, John Winthrop and Edward Winslow, suggest that political and economic motives, as well as religious aims, underlay Puritan missionary activities. Resultant disaffection with the Puritans on the part of many New England Indians helped delay English expansion in some areas for more than a century. The political functions of Jesuit missions to the Indians have been described frequently and are accepted by historians without demur. Different functions have been attributed to the Puritan missions of seventeenth century New England (see Mather 1694: 134-147). Reviving the traditional rationale of the Puritans themselves, recent historians of their missions present them without differentiation or substantial qualification as agencies of altruism (0. Winslow 1968: 96; Vaughan 1965: vii-viii). I have given special attention to Alden T. Vaughan's New England Frontier, attending on the one hand to its internal logic, and on the other to the degree of correlation existing between what the book said was in source documents and what I found in those documents. Vaughan's study seems defective to me both in its inherent credibility of argument and in its reportage of the sources, and his conclusions seem rather to be refuted than supported by his evidence. *Paper read at the 17th Annual Meeting of the American Society for Ethnohistory, Cornell University, October, 1969. 198 FRANCIS JENNINGS Vaughan states in his preface that Puritan documents can be accepted as truthful because, as he puts it, the Puritans "had no reason to conceal their attitudes or actions toward the Indians." My own finding has been that the Puritans had many reasons for concealment of motives and behavior, and that their documents normally exhibit misleading half-truths as well as more exceptional, but exceptionally important, falsifications. Vaughan states also that he had "concentrated on the acts and attitudes of the Puritans toward the Indians," and had not "for the most part, attempted to account for the actions and reactions of the natives." He took this approach, he says, "by necessity, as well as by inclination." I have neither accepted the necessity nor shared the inclination. When Indians are regarded as rational human beings rather than as mythical creatures, their actions and reactions do not seem so difficult to infer from both circumstances and the available evidence. The same statement, it may be added, holds true for the Puritans. However, this paper is not intended as a polemic. The more urgent task, to which it is addressed, is to present an alternative reading of the sources. According to the royal patent of Massachusetts, "the principall Ende of this Plantacion" was to "wynn and incite the Natives of [the] Country, to the Knowledg and Obedience of the onlie true God and Savior of Mankinde, and the Christian Fayth" (Massachusetts [Colony] 1853-54: I: 17). However, after arrival in New England, the Puritans busied themselves with other matters. From the record of their activity, it would be easy to conclude that "the principal Ende" was not an end at all. Practically speaking, there was no Puritan missionary work at all until 1643 even though Boston had been founded in 1630. The changes occurring after 1643 made possible a massive literature in which the missions have been portrayed as one of the noblest works of man in any era. This literature endowed Massachusetts' chief missionary with the epithet "the Apostle to the Indians" by which he is mentioned to the present day. Rhetoric considerably outran performance. A hard-nosed, avowedly racist, nineteenth century historian confessed that "little, if any, credit" was due to the orthodox Puritans "for labor spent or for success attained in that work" (G. E. Ellis 1885: 257-258). The "Apostle" himself reported toward the end of his life that his results had been disappointingly meagre. Responding to questions from England, he evaded the question of how many Indians had been admitted to full communion, and protested as "too strickt" another question about how many converts were being "daily added" (Eliot 1809: 126). His associate Daniel Gookin remarked that most of the "praying Indians" had "not yet come so far as to be able or willing to profess their faith in Christ and yield obedience and subjection to him in his church" (Gookin 1792: 182). From Eliot's account elsewhere, the number of Indian communicants in 1671 can be calculated at certainly less than one hundred, and probably much lower (Eliot 1671: 6). In the great crisis of King Philip's War, many of the praying Indians deserted their superficially adopted faith to join battle against Christian Englishmen (Leach 1958: 73-84).
