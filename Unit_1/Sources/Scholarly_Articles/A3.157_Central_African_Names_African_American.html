Central African Names and African-American Naming Patterns John Thornton OR many years, historians have tried to understand how elements of African culture survived among African Americans. One line of investigation has focused on naming patterns, since such patterns can reveal the extent to which concepts of family, lineage, and kinship were retained beyond the Atlantic crossing. Recent research challenges the old idea that masters assigned names to slaves or that slaves imitated masters' systems of naming. If African-American naming patterns are not simply derivative of the slave experience or imitative of Euro-American systems, then the possibility of African contribution and nomenclatural persistence becomes much stronger.' One of the first African naming patterns to be recognized was the Akan custom of giving "day names."2 This pattern can be documented in America. A day name was given to a baby according to the day of the week on which it was born. The discovery of day naming was relatively easy, because of the obvi- ously non-English form of the names, which on investigation turned out to be Akan, and because modern Akan speakers continue to name children in the same way. Thus anthropologists could collaborate with historians to explain the custom of giving day names, at least for Akan-speaking African Americans. But it is not always so easy to recognize African names or naming patterns, since not all Africans followed the Akan pattern and not all African names are readily recognizable as foreign, as we shall see.4 This problem of recognition and identification is compounded by the fact that few students have explored African naming patterns historically. Historians of Africa have not concerned themselves with this line of research, and historians of the Americas have not found it easy to consult the original sources. If the pattern of giving names has changed in African societies since the seventeenth century, a modern African or an anthro- pologist with experience among modern Africans might not recognize the older names or pattern as African. We know that naming patterns in other parts of the world have changed over time, and there is no necessary reason to assume that Africa was any different. This article examines naming patterns for the Angolan region-that is, west-central Africa, including populations in modern Angola, Zaire, Congo-Brazzaville, and Gabon, conventionally called the Angola Coast in the era of the slave trade-using historical documentation. The investiga- tion focuses on Angolan names and naming patterns in order to provide Americanists with basic information about this important group of people. The article also explores an American case study to show how understand- ing the African system may reveal its continued functioning in America.
