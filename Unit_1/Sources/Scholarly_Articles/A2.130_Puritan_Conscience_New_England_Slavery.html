Selection Overview

Rosenthal argues that while agitation for abolition in Puritan New England was minimal, the theological problem slavery posed for the clergy led orthodox Puritans, including Cotton Mather (who nonetheless owned slaves), toward radical positions on race and, eventually, abolition. He concludes that intellectual opposition to slavery came from orthodox Puritans and that arguments, not only economics, helped topple it.

PURITAN CONSCIENCE AND NEW ENGLAND SLAVERY BERNARD ROSENTHAL AS has often and accurately been pointed out, agitation in puritan New England for the abolition of slavery was minimal. A murkier area of understanding, however, is to be found in the theological problem that slavery presented to the New England clergy. While the issues did not generate the passion and divisiveness as those emanating from New En- gland's central theological question of the Lord's Supper, the matter raised religious questions that led the most orthodox puritans toward radical positions in regard to matters of race and, ultimately, abolition. As David S. Lovejoy has shown in an important essay on slavery and Puritanism, the revolution- ary abolitionist fervor of Samuel Hopkins was learned at the feet of Jonathan Edwards.' This is not to say that Edwards espoused abolitionism. The point is, rather, that the theology of Jonathan Edwards contained inherent assumptions about God and man that, taken to their logical conclusion, would lead to the position Hopkins took in opposing slavery and the powerful slavery interests in Rhode Island. In doing so, how- ever, Hopkins established no clerical precedent. The fact is that in puritan New England when the cause of the Negro was espoused, it was usually from the ranks of the clergy that the issues were pressed. In short, the main efforts on behalf of slaves were likely to come from orthodox Puritans. Counted among these was none other than the archetype of puritan orthodoxy, Cotton Mather. Let it be understood at once that Cotton Mather is not to be presented as an abolitionist. He owned his own slaves and seemed to show no signs of remorse. The visible saints who came to New England in 1630 on the Arbella had a reasonably com- fortable belief that they were the elect of God. They were, however, the first and last puritan generation that could make such an assumption. Were their offspring also saints? And who among the new immigrants to God's special province could claim such a distinction? The difficulties inherent in these questions, the compromise of the half-way covenant, and th democratization of the Lord's Supper, led by Solomon Stod dard, have been well documented. But what of the blacks? Could they be a covenanted people? Surely they existed in New England and were therefore in some way linked to the federal covenant, but what of the covenant of grace? Were they to be included? Could they be of the elect? Implicit in these uncertainties was something as fundamental as the ques- tion of whether blacks had souls, for if they did, it would be difficult to deny them the possibility of conversion. Theologi- cally, the safest course was not to assume that they were outside the covenant of grace. After all, it was puritan dogma that no- body could say with absolute certainty that one had received salvation, as Ann Hutchinson discovered in claiming direct communication with the Holy Ghost. The easiest course, then, would appear to be to allow blacks the opportunity for salva- tion. The problem also had its secular side, which in the short run at least was to be triumphant. The conventional wisdom of the day assumed that one could not keep Christians as slaves. Thus to convert the slaves was to free the slaves, or so it was feared by many, and the issue was joined on that point. In facing these problems, Cotton Mather argued from the most orthodox of positions. Of course there might be some elected ones among the Negroes! What other conclusion could an orthodox Puritan reach?
