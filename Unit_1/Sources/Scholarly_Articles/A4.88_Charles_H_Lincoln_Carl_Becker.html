98 WILLIAM AND MARY QUARTERLY As the first book-length statement of the Progressive interpretation of the Revolution, Lincoln's The Revolutionary Movement in Pennsylvania, 1760-1776, deserves examination. The present essay offers reason to sur- mise that Lincoln's and Becker's books had common origins; suggests why Becker's work became famous and Lincoln's languished until recently in relative obscurity; and shows that Lincoln's monograph has had some his- toriographic influence, though it has never received widespread atten- tion.3 In Lincoln's view, "no people in all America were more democratic than the dissatisfied communities in the Quaker colony, and it was because the provincial government would not grant them equal rights or equal oppor- tunities that these dissatisfied people welcomed a national movement un- der cover of which they might revolutionize their own colonial conditions." Two groups spearheaded this movement: the "masses" of "lower" and "middle" class Philadelphians who were exploited by the "rul- ing aristocracy" of that city, and the German and Scotch-Irish settlers in the western part of the province who had grievances against the Quaker mercantile "oligarchy." These aristocrats and oligarchs faced "an almost solidly democratic sentiment throughout the west, and an equally strong sentiment among the masses in the city of Philadelphia." They maintained their power by disfranchising all but a handful of Philadelphians and by gerrymandering assembly representation against the west. For the op- pressed groups in the city and the west, therefore, "force was the only alternative and the troubles with England gave an opportunity for its ef- fective exercise." Following this logic, Lincoln devoted six chapters to de- scribing social and economic conditions in Pennsylvania, and then turned in his seventh chapter to the "Introduction of International Questions." ciety Quarterly, LIII [i969], I27).
