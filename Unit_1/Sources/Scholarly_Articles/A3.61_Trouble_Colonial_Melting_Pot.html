Trouble in the Colonial Melting Pot PHILIP GLEASON WE ALL KNOW the Founding Fathers have, of late, fallen upon hard times. Even so, it comes as a shock that Benjamin Franklin, long held up as an model of enlightened tolerance, should now be branded a racist. Historians of immigration are of course aware that, as Carl Wittke observed some sixty years ago, Franklin was "not always laudatory" in what he said about the Germans of Pennsylvania.1 However, the empha sis on his "broad racism" is more recent and is, at least in part, a function of the sharp distinction drawn since the 1970s between race and ethnicity.2 Ronald Takaki is, in my opinion, the key person in this downward revision of Franklin's reputation. In his Iron Cages (1979); in an essay reprinted in his widely-used anthology, From Different Shores (1987, 1994); and in his multicultural history, A Different Mirror (1993), Takaki features Franklin's racism quite prominently but pays almost no attention to his derogatory comments about the Germans.3 The crucial evidence on both counts comes from Franklin's "Obser vations Concerning the Increase of Mankind, Peopling of Countries, &c" (1751), the main body of which comprises a pioneering exploration of the principles of demographic growth and their implications for Brit ish imperial policy.4 After having shown that population increases or decreases according to the structure of economic opportunity, and that the English alone could fill up the colonies, Franklin complained about German immigration, using very pejorative language. "Why," he asked, should the Palatine Boors be suffered to swarm into our Settlements, and by herding together establish their language and manners to the Exclusion of ours? Why should Pennsylvania, founded by the English, become a Colony of Aliens, who will shortly be so numerous as to Germanize us instead of our Anglifying them, and will never adopt our Language or Customs, any more than they can acquire our Complexion.5 Perhaps Franklin's somewhat puzzling reference to the "complexion" of the Germans led him to further meditations on race and color, for in This essay is the presidential address delivered at the annual meeting of the Immi gration and Ethnic History Society, held in St. Louis, April 1, 2000. Even the Europeans?including all Germans except the Saxons?were "generally of what we call a swarthy Complexion." That left the English and Saxons as "the principal Body of White People on the Face of the Earth," and Franklin wished for their increase. He then spun out an elaborate conceit which contains his most compromis ing racial language: And while we are, as I may call it, Scouring our Planet, by clearing America of Woods, and so making this Side of our Globe reflect a brighter Light to the Eyes of the Inhabitants of Mars or Venus, why should we in the Sight of Superior Beings, darken its People? why increase the Sons of Africa, by Planting them in America, where we have so fair an Opportu nity, by excluding all Blacks and Tawneys, of increasing the lovely White and Red? But perhaps I am partial to the Complexion of my Country, for such Kind of Partiality is natural to Mankind.6 These are surely strange and shocking addenda to a sober analysis of demographic principles. Even if Franklin intended them ironically (as a recent scholar argues), he soon reconsidered, excising both paragraphs from all but the earliest published versions of the paper.7 That did him no good with the Pennsylvania Germans?or, as we have seen, with recent historians. The former turned his reference to "Palatine Boors" against him with devastating results in the Pennsylvania election of 1764. This was the first time unfavorable attention was drawn to Franklin's ethnocentrism/racism, and it is this episode I want to look into here. What makes it professionally relevant for us is the fact that it took place as part of a major crisis in colonial Pennsylvania?one in which racial, religious, and ethnic issues were centrally involved. It is a complicated story, and I will begin with a thumbnail sketch of its devel opment before looking more closely at its racial, religious, and ethnic aspects. Recognizing that any starting point cuts into the seamless robe of history, our story begins in December 1763 with the so-called "Paxton Boys" massacre of some twenty Conestoga Indians, the last remnant of a group which had for years lived peacefully among white settlers near Lancaster, Pennsylvania. John Penn, the recently-arrived proprietary gov ernor, immediately ordered that the perpetrators of this atrocity be brought to justice, but no one was even arrested, much less convicted and pun ished.
