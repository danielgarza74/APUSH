Selection Overview

Schmotter argues that historians of the New England Awakening, by turning to lay conversions, have missed that for most Congregational ministers the revival was above all a professional crisis about the purposes and responsibilities of their calling. He concludes that the clergy's attempt to meet the crisis through clerical discipline instead destroyed the consensus of ministerial interests needed to enforce it.

SCHMOTTER Cornell University IN RECENT YEARS HISTORIANS OF THE GREAT AWAKENING IN NEW England have turned away from the ministry to concentrate on lay par- ticipation in the revivals. Through quantitative methodology they have sought to determine who was converted in the 1740s and have hypothesized the reasons for these conversions.1 This approach has pro- vided illuminating insights, but its success should not suggest that further study of the ministry cannot expand our understanding of the Awakening. Questions about the Congregational clergy's role in the revivals of the 1740s remain unanswered, largely because scholarship has neglected the issues that these ministers themselves deemed most important. What historians have missed is the fact that for most New England ministers the Great Awakening represented above all a professional crisis. The most pressing questions for Congregational pastors of the 1740s con- cerned the purposes and responsibilities of their calling. Ministers had to decide how to deal with popular preachers like George Whitefield and James Davenport, men who openly scorned the ministry's qualifications and standards. Clerical Professionalism and the Great Awakening 149 embrace the "New Light" without sacrificing ministerial authority. And others less fortunate had to choose between their conception of pastoral duties and the affection of their parishioners. Viewed in this light, the events of the Great Awakening suggest that an underlying cause for many problems faced by ministers in the 1740s was a heightened sense of clerical self-interest best termed professionalism. Ironically it was a concern for professional stability that produced condi- tions that led to the very instability most pastors sought to avoid. To understand how this happened requires an examination of the develop- ment of the new clerical professionalism of the early years of the eighteenth century. Congregational ministers had discussed the problems and policy of their calling since the founding of New England, but the 1690s saw a new intensity of concern for these matters. Increasingly ministers saw their status, prestige, and pastoral effectiveness threatened. Many feared they had lost their parishioner's hearts and minds, for the rancor caused by parish debates over the Half-Way Covenant two decades before still re- mained. Although seldom overtly anticlerical, New Englanders no longer hesitated to challenge their pastor's spiritual and ecclesiastical authority. In the Connecticut Valley, for example, congregations repeatedly voiced their displeasure with ministerial candidates from Harvard College whom they suspected of Arminian leanings.2 Further, political developments on both sides of the Atlantic also heightened Congregational ministers' anxi- ety. Few Boston ministers could forget how Sir Edmund Andros had commandeered the Second Church for Anglican services during his rule as governor of the Dominion of New England. Even though Andros' gov- ernment had been deposed in 1689 the threat of interference from London remained. The new royal charter of 1691 that established religious toler- ance in Massachusetts only underscored the uncertainty with which ministers faced the decade of the 1690s. While most New England pastors understood the realities of imperial politics and were prepared to accept the new edict of tolerance, they soon found themselves facing critics whose lack of caution and courtesy was unprecedented. In 1695 a Salem merchant named Thomas Maule circu- lated a pamphlet entitled Truth Held Forth and Maintained which called Congregational ministers "Persecuting Priests," and attacked them for suppressing Quakers in the 1630s and for their conduct in the recent witchcraft fiasco in Essex County.
