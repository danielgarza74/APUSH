Spain and the Founding of Jamestown
William S. Goldman

Selection Overview

Goldman asks why Spain, which had acted swiftly and violently against rivals at Fort Caroline and Araya, never attacked Jamestown. He argues that the new doctrine of reason of state led Spanish officials to weigh fear of war with England, lack of money, and a low estimate of the danger, so that Spain's decision to let Jamestown survive helped shape a new Atlantic world.

Spain and the Founding of Jamestown William S. Goldman I N the summer of 1611, a motley crew of Spaniards under the com- mand of a soldier of fortune named Diego de Molina hove into view at Point Comfort, the English lookout post at the mouth of the James River, some miles downstream from the new English settlement at Jamestown. Molina landed with two members of his crew and, when questioned by the English, explained that he was in the vicinity search- ing for a sunken Spanish ship in an attempt to recover its cannon. As Molina and his crewmen apprised the English of this unlikely story, they were captured by an armed party of fifty men. At the same time, English pilot John Clark boarded the Spanish vessel to help guide it through the shoals to a safe anchorage. After an overnight standoff, the Spanish ship sailed away, taking Clark with it and deserting its own captain and crewmen on land as English captives. Molina languished in captivity for seven years before regaining his freedom, and his two fellow crew members died in captivity, one within sight of land on his voyage back to Spain. In previous decades Spain had shown itself eager to swiftly and violently counter any threat to its sov- ereignty or empire, most clearly in the 1565 attack on French Huguenots at Fort Caroline, Florida, and in the 1606 expulsion of the Dutch from the salt-rich peninsula of Araya in modern-day Venezuela. The English, too, had received their fair share of punishment at places such as San Juan de Ulúa in 1568. 2 Yet throughout the turbulent early years of the English colony, no Spanish attack materialized and no galleons filled with veteran tercios appeared on the Chesapeake Bay. Possibly the most surprising aspect of the English foray into the New World was the lack of any Spanish response. Faced with a clear invasion of territory it had claimed since the fifteenth century, Spain did not act to safeguard its domain, instead contenting itself with diplomatic complaints and impotent threats. And in not acting, the Spanish government under King Philip III knowingly abandoned its claim of imperial domination of the Indies, a contention on which Spain had built much of its wealth and power during the preceding decades. The founding of Jamestown was a fundamental turning point in the history of the Atlantic world, and it was the Spanish decision to allow the colony to survive that began the delineation of linguistic, cultural, and political boundaries of that developing world: roughly, a Spanish-speaking South America and an English-speaking North America. After more than a century of Spanish domination, the New World was now home to rival European empires, and the map of the Atlantic world began to take shape. Despite the myriad volumes on the topic of Jamestown and early English expansion in North America, little has been written about the Spanish involvement—or lack thereof—in the colony’s early years.
