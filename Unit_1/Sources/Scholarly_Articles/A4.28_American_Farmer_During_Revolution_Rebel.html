Selection Overview

Brown asks what share of the overwhelmingly rural colonial population was Whig, Tory, or neutral, and shows that definitions are slippery because many colonists changed sides or hid their opinions. He concludes that far more farmers were Whig or neutral than loyal, and that farming itself did not determine allegiance except by breeding indifference; military, native-place, and personal considerations mattered more.

WALLACE BROWN THE AMERICAN FARMER DURING THE REVOLUTION: REBEL OR LOYALIST? One of the more interesting questions about the American Revolution is what proportions of the population were Whig, Tory or neutral.l The classic answer, from John Adams, that like Gaul the people were divided into thirds is not entirely satisfactory,2 partly because the problem of defi- nition is insuperable. Many colonists changed sides, sometimes more than once, often according to the changing fortunes of war. Many more, possibly a majority, tried to stay neutral. Thus Samuel Miles, a Connecticut farmer, admitted that during the Revolution he "kept as much out of the way as possible." 3 Such sentiments were common. Further, secret opinions compli- cate matters. Mathew Lymburner, a farmer from Penobscot, Massachusetts, said he "affected to be neutral in the political disputes . . . but in private was a Loyalist." 4 And what is one to make of Francis Pemart, a Peekskill farmer, who claimed that he served as a rebel forage master but with "an irltention of Serving the Loyalists"! 328 AGRICULTURAL HISTORY farmers' stand in the Revolution certainly looms large, but not as large as numbers might suggest, because the dynamism of opposition to the British was mainly located in urban areas. Here were found the politicians, legis- lative assemblies, debates, newspapers, wealth, the economic and cultural maturity, and necessary sophistication. Here were the concentrations of the population needed to raise militant audiences or rebellious mobs. Here the apparatus of imperial control the royal administration, the army and the navwas most keenly felt. Here too the unpopular British policies and legislation of the 1760's and the 1770's bore most heavily. Rural areas were comparatively little affected. David Ramsay, the contemporary Whig his- torian, commented that farmers in South Carolina could not be rallied over "a trifling tax on tea," a beverage they were unable to drink anyway. He also noted the farmers' general remoteness from "distant evils" in Massa- chusetts and the British Parliament.6 Even the Proclamation of 1763 barring western settlement was of more concern to eastern speculators (some like Franklin were Whig, others like Galloway were Tory) than the mass of frontier pioneers who simply ignored it.7 Ramsay also stressed the traditional inland dislike of dominant Charles- ton and the seaboard, an observation which could be applied to most other colonies.8 Many westerners saw the seaboard, the source of most of their troubles unfair taxation, lack of representation and protection, etc. as a more obvious enemy than Whitehall. Sllch quarrels did not necessarily make Loyalists of farmers but they did increase apathy and indifference. In some areas, for example western Pennsylvania, a hatred of the East caused a more radical Whiggism.9 Further, the sheer isolation of most farmers, even those on the seaboard, must be stressed: poor or no roads, few schools, no newspapers. Add the long, hard, diurnal and seasonal rural routine which was necessarily the common lot, and it is not surprising that conservatism, a devotion to the status quo and a remoteness from the crises of empire were so common.l° It should be remembered that indifference was physically much easier in the country than in the towns where Whig vigilance was keener. Thomas Sherar, a farmer of Tryorl County, New York, recalled after the war that he had never been asked to join the rebels because he lived thirty miles from the nearest settlement.
