Another Look at the Boston “Caucus”
Alan Day and Katherine Day
 
ARTICLE Another Look at the Boston “Caucus”
JOURNAL Journal of American Studies 5, no. 1 (1971)
EXCERPT Original journal pages 19-20
JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/27670624
Selection Overview

Day and Day argue that historians have given the Boston caucus only a one-line role even though John Adams said the Revolution was effected by caucuses. Using the surviving 1772–74 proceedings of the North End Caucus, they set out to analyze the caucus system and place it in Boston’s pre-revolutionary social and political life before drawing tentative conclusions about its function, membership, leadership, and significance.

The pro ceedings of one caucus, that of the North End of Boston, have however curiously survived. Although limited in scope, spanning only the years 1772 to 1774, they afford the one direct source of information.3 A major weakness of previous discussions?or rather dismissals?of the ' caucus ' has been a total failure both to analyse the general configuration of the caucus system and to place the North End Caucus into the context of Boston social and political life in the immediate pre-revolutionary period. II The North End Caucus records were first published in 1891, but they had clearly been available to earlier historians. Richard Frothingham, writing in 1849, noted three caucuses?the North End Caucus, the South End Caucus, the Middle District Caucus?and gave examples of their workings.4 It seems strange therefore that more recent historians have not made greater use of them. In particular John C. Miller in his book Sam Adams continually refers to ' the Boston Caucus ' and the ' Caucus Club ', but as an entity, as if it com prised a single machine.5 23 March 1772 is the first date in the records, but it is unlikely that this marks the beginning of the North End Caucus. On the first leaf is the memorandum, ' Began 1767?records lost '.6 John Eliot writing in 1809 had pointed to a caucus meeting in 1768, ' pro bono publico \ This is evidently the North End Caucus, for ' they met in a house near the North battery ' and the change of meeting place to the Green Dragon Tavern, at the time of the destruction of the tea in 1773, corresponds with the records.7 If the North End Caucus had a definite beginning around 1767, the usual textbook quotation for the caucus, an entry in John Adams's diary for an unknown day in February 1763, can only refer to an earlier caucus. This day learned that the Caucus Clubb meets at certain Times in the Garret of Tom Daws, the Adjutant of the Boston Regiment. He has a large House, and he has a moveable Partition in his Garrett, which he takes down and the whole Clubb meets in one Room. There they smoke tobacco till you cannot see from one End of the Garrett to the other . . .8 This would seem in fact to be the South End Caucus.
