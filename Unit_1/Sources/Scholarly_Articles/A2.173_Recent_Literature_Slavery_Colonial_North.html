When I attended high school in the early 1960s, teachers still used Samuel Eliot Morison and Henry Steele Commager's The Growth of the Ameri can Republic. (It was the tenth printing ofthe fourth edition, published in 1950, which emphasizes how long textbooks tend to hang around high school class rooms.) This book, which set the stan dard for American history for a generation (in spite of the authors' un fortunate characterization of the typical antebellum slave as "Sambo"), contained virtually no mention of slavery's exist ence in North America before the nine teenth century. I completed graduate course work in 1974, shortly before Pe ter H. Wood's Black Majority (1974) and Edmund S. Morgan's American Sla very, American Freedom (1975), opened many eyes to slavery's origins and early existence in different colonies. Thus, I began teaching college-level African American history in 1976, struggling to make sense for myself, let alone for my students, of the first two centuries of slavery in mainland North America. An outpouring of exceptional historical literature has changed the ways in which I teach slavery in colonial North America (2). The course content changed to reflect our much greater aware ness and enhanced understanding of the experience of African American men and women (especially the latter) in the North American mainland colonies. This book is a masterful synthesis of slavery's origins and development in the lands that would become the United States. William D. The Atlantic Slave Trade For placing Africa and the slave trade in the context of the Atlantic colonial world, there exists nothing better than John K. Thornton's Africa and Africans in the Making ofthe Atlantic World (1998). Two new books focus on the role of Great Britain and its New World colonies in the developing Atlantic system: Kenneth Morgan's concise Slavery, Atlantic Trade and the British Economy, 16004800 (2000) and James Walvin's Making the Black Atlantic (2000). Rising interest in these matters goes back over three decades. Even Philip D. Curtin could not have known the service he was providing when he published The Atlantic Slave Trade: A Census in 1969. Curtin showed that previous estimates of the number of African men, women, and children sold into the Americas during the centuries of the trade, which reached as high as one hundred million, spiraled back through a web of seeming authority to nineteenth-century guesses. In his attempt to render estimates based on available data, Curtin put the number much lower, under ten million, and in so doing created controversy that set off a search for sources for more accurate study of the trade. The result has been steady accumulation of more raw data?a good part of which (records of some twenty-seven thousand slaving voyages) is now available on CD-rom in David Eltis et al., The Transatlan tic Slave Trade, 1567-1867 (1999). A useful synthesis of many of the findings based on the new data is Herbert S. Klein's The Atlantic Slave Trade (1999). Hugh Thomas's The Slave Trade (1997) is a massive, popular account with a basis in solid scholar ship. Robert W. Harms's The Diligent (2002) is a marvelous account of a single slaving voyage, based on a journal kept by the crew. Origins of Slavery in Mainland North America The chicken-or-egg question over slavery's beginnings on the North American mainland?was it perceived economic necessity or racism that made black slavery the main form of agricultural labor in the colonies by early in the eighteenth century??has perplexed historians for decades. Winthrop D. Jordan's White Over Black (1968), which posits a cause-and-effect relationship between colonial racial attitudes and the colonists' need for labor, has had remarkable staying power, and Betty Wood, in The Origins of American Slavery (1997), reinforces the idea. Russell R. Menard stresses the economic argument in Migrants, Servants and Slaves (2001), noting that British colonists preferred Euro pean indentured servants to African slaves, but the supply of servants simply dried up. Kenneth Morgan in Slavery and Servi tude in Colonial North America (2001) agrees with Menard David Eltis's The Rise of African Slavery in the Americas (2000) adds another element: the nature of African societies and their role in the development of the Atlantic trade and New Wo r slavery. As Berlin emphasizes, however, the situation differed among the colonies, which turned to slavery at different times and for varying reasons, requiring studies that are at least regionally specific for the clearest pictures of slavery's beginnings. Such books would include, for the Chesapeake colonies of Virginia and Maryland, Morgan's and Menard's books (noted earlier) along with Gloria L. Main's Tobacco Colony (1982), T.H. Breen and Stephen Innes's "Myne Owne Ground" (1980), and Kathleen M. Browns's Good Wives, Nasty Wenches, and Anxious Patriarchs (1996), the latter showing how patriarchy advanced along with increasing white control of black slaves in Virginia. Judith Carney's Black Rice (2001) offers the broad background for establishment of slavery in the Low Country colonies of South Carolina and Georgia, with particulars provided by Peter Wood's important study, noted above, Daniel Littlefield's Rice and Slaves (1981), Edward Ball's Slaves in the Family (1998) (a popular book that may have garnered special interest because Ball is a descendant of South Carolina slave owners), Betty Wood's Slavery in Colonial Georgia (1984), and Julia Floyd Smith's Slavery and Rice Culture in Low Country Georgia (1985). Marvin L. Michael Kay and Lorin Lee Carey's Slavery in North Carolina (1995) deals with a colony that fit neither Chesapeake nor Low-Country patterns. These authors pull no punches in describing the brutality of slavery in colonial North Carolina. For colonial holdings border ing the Gulf of Mexico, see Jane Landers's comprehensive Black Society in Spanish Florida (1999), Daniel H. Usner Jr.'s Indians, Settlers, and Slaves in a Frontier Exchange Economy (1992), and Thomas N. Ingersoll's Mammon and Manon in Early New Or leans (1999). Books by Graham Russell Hodges (Root and Branch [1999]), and Joanne Pope Melish (Disowning Slavery [1998], especially the first chapter) deal with the northern colonies. One can see, generally, in the most recent of these studies, greater recognition of the fact that the North American mainland, between 1650 and 1750, fit into a larger Atlantic colonial pattern where enslaved African men and women were fast becoming the preferred laborers. African American Culture Recent historical scholarship teaches us that both the charac ter of black men and women and the nature of their culture were more complex than previously recognized. Philip D. Morgan's Slave Counterpoint (1998) makes these points in detail. Morgan's book includes information on everything from material culture to work (which dominated slave life, but has not always received sufficient attention), from personal relationships to language and literature. One finishes Morgan's book with a deeper understand ing of how different situations in slavery could be over short distances and long periods. James L.
