Selection Overview

Mailer argues that John Witherspoon's support for American independence drew on the Anglo-Scottish covenant tradition, citing Samuel Adams's circle invoking a "Solemn League and Covenant" in 1774. He concludes that Witherspoon's evangelicalism saw independence as necessary for Britain's own revolution, back to the true Reformed spirit of 1707 and its Solemn League and Covenant.

He was the grandson of a man who signed the 1643 document that some Scots sought to renew and that Boston radicals appropriated. In 1768 he moved from Scotland to America to assume the presidency of the College of New Jersey (present-day Princeton University), and in 1776 he became the only clergyman to sign America’s Declaration of Independence from the Parliament of Westminster and from the British king. 3 Witherspoon taught one president (James Madison, B.A. 1771) and one vice president (Aaron Burr, B.A. 1772), forty-nine U.S. representa- tives, twenty-eight U.S. senators, three Supreme Court justices, one sec- retary of state, three attorneys general, and two foreign ministers. More than 11 percent of his graduates became college presidents, in eight dif- ferent American states. Many were concerned with the expansion of Presbyterian piety in the new American confederation. Former student Samuel Doak, for example, settled in eastern Tennessee, preached in the backcountry, chartered Washington College, and founded Tusculum College, named after Witherspoon’s home in New Jersey, in 1818.
