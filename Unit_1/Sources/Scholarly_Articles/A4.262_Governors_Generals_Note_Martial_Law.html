Selection Overview

Steele tests the claim that James II’s colonial administration was essentially military and argues that his royal governors, even the soldiers among them, did not rely on martial law during the crisis of 1688–89 and saw themselves as civil governors.

Notes and Documents Governors or Generals?: A Note on Martial Law and the Revolution of i689 in English America Ian Steele C LAIMS that the first British empire was essentially military, rather than commercial or political, have rightly centered on James II, particularly as duke of York. His influence on his royal brother, as well as his own experience, style of government, choice of colonial governors, and imperial preoccupations, is said to have reflected an essentially military perspective.' This view is compatible with some of the fears, claims, and experiences of Englishmen on both sides of the Atlantic before and during his short, unhappy reign. Yet suggestions that his imperial administration was primarily military need further examination, especially since no written plan of "garrison government" has survived, contrary opinion abounds, and the theory has not been applied to the study of the English empire while James II was king. James's empire was never more fully developed, in personnel and policies, than in the last year of his reign. Then came a strenuous test of how that empire was perceived, when his royal governors were forced to respond to the long crisis that ended the reign in i688-i689. GOVERNORS OR GENERALS? 305 to various levels of resistance,2 did the royal governors react in ways that suggested the primacy of military, or civilian, perceptions of their posi- tions? Although reluctant to use martial law in England, James II had given one stunning demonstration of thoroughly military power in the brutal de- struction of the duke of Monmouth's rising in i685.3 For weeks after their victory, his troops exercised what proved to be the last instance of unfettered martial law in England. Two of James's colonial governors of i689, Edmund Andros and Francis Nicholson, had been army officers involved in suppressing Monmouth's followers, as had others who became military officers in the colonies in the interim.4 Formal colonial addresses congratulated James on his success. Shiploads of convicted rebels were transported to the West Indian colonies. Leaders of all the royal colonies in i689 should have recalled James's own military model for crushing a challenge to the throne. Not only was the wide use of martial law exemplified by the royal response to Monmouth, but it was also clear that the military had much more freedom of action outside England than was allowed within. In Ireland, Scotland, Tangier, or America the imposition of martial law, which effectively suspended the operation of civil authority, met no resistance in English law.
