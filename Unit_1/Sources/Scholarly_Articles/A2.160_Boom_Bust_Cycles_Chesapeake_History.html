Selection Overview

Walsh introduces a 2011 Forum of new Chesapeake scholarship, observing that the 1980s syntheses by Chesapeake School historians provoked few challenges, and welcoming a new generation’s willingness to challenge or refine them. She says that dividing the Chesapeake into subregions and adopting Atlantic perspectives forces historians to rethink conventional explanations. (historiography)

Walsh T WO 1980s syntheses summarizing the findings of Chesapeake School historians working in the Maryland State Archives in Annapolis a decade earlier failed to provoke major challenges to initially controversial arguments about economic and social develop- ment in the region in the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries.1 By the mid-1980s younger scholars saw the early Chesapeake as a field overcrowded and overdone, and so they shifted the locus of new research to mid-eighteenth-century Virginia and Maryland, the Lower South, the middle colonies, and the backcountry, and the focus primarily to cul- tural topics. The January 1999 issue of the William and Mary Quarterly featured articles on the domestic economy of British North America (including the Chesapeake) that tentatively tackled a few items on the lengthy list of research needs and opportunities that John J. McCusker and Russell R. Menard had identified nearly fifteen years earlier. In his introduction to that issue, McCusker similarly lamented that he and Menard had “presumed” that their synthesis presented in The Economy of British America “would stir others either to challenge or to refine” their arguments, but this had “not yet happened.”2 Thus this 2011 Forum is a welcome and invigorating change, showcas- ing the work of a new generation of scholars who have at last decided to revisit and to challenge or refine thirty-year-old arguments about the course of the Chesapeake economy and society. One of the fundamental differences from the previous work, however, is the emphasis they place on early Chesapeake elites. In the 1970s researchers at the Maryland State Archives were energized by the promise that new methods for systematically analyzing county court and parish records offered for learning more about the lives of ordinary European settlers. Since many of the scholars were working for public history museums rather than academia, museum pro- gramming needs engendered a strong commitment to exploring the history of all marginalized people, leading to a search for additional strategies for studying groups poorly represented in conventional literary sources and more abundant local records. There was much less interest in the more traditional subjects of religion and politics or in the deeds and writings of great men—a group Chesapeake School scholars considered stultifyingly overstudied, though they did research the changing composition of political elites, their place in colonial and provincial social hierarchies, and their role in creating local order, often in the context of county-level studies.
