Selection Overview

Watson examines how colonial North Carolina's legal system treated slaves both as property and as responsible persons, tracing the slave code derived from the 1715 and 1741 laws, the 1753 patrol system, and the special courts through which slaves received protections such as trial by jury in cases involving life or limb.

North Carolina Slave Courts, 1715-1785 By Alan D. Watson* The paradoxes of early American slavery have often been cited, and none was more obvious or more confounding than the dual conception of slaves as chat tels and persons. Colonial legal systems reflected tortuous efforts to define the status of bondsmen. Treated as a form of property, which rendered them valuable assets, slaves were also deemed responsible individuals who were sub ject to corporal punishment in criminal matters. Conversely, when life or limb was at stake, slaves required some form of legal protection. Even the harsh South Carolina slave code observed, "natural justice forbids that any person, of what condition soever, should be condemned unheard. . . Protection for North Carolina slaves in the eighteenth century proved neces sary. Slaves posed a constant threat to society because they often suffered from shortages of food and clothing, experienced passionate human feelings of hate and desire, and sought freedom in manifold ways. The dominant whites at tempted by various means to control their bondsmen. The slave code of North Carolina, derived principally from legislation passed in 1715 and 1741, aimed to minimize the freedom and mobility of slaves, to discourage commercial and social relations between slaves and whites, and to reduce the number of run aways and the possibility of slave violence. In 1753 the colony also instituted the search or patrol system, but the precaution failed to deter slave activity deemed illicit by whites.2 Plantation justice, determined by master or overseer, was the primary means of discipline for most slaves. As the slaves' criminal activities heightened in im portance and spread beyond the plantation, they evoked the intervention of formal governmental agencies—the magistracy and the slave patrol. North Carolina Slave Courts, 1715-1785 25 offenses the justices of the peace exercised almost plenary powers of decision, unrestrained by strict rules of judicial procedure. Patrols likewise possessed ex tensive latitude.3 For dealing with slave criminality beyond the scope of ordinary police regula tions, North Carolina, like other British colonies, developed the slave court. De signed to assure slaves a judicial hearing, to curtail the cost of a full-fledged trial, and to expedite justice, which in turn would reduce the loss of valuable slave labor, the slave court was utilized by New York, New Jersey, Pennsyl vania, and Delaware as well as by the southern colonies from Maryland through Georgia. Historians, however, have paid scant attention to this institution which reflected the standards of desired slave behavior as determined by whites, evidenced the fears of white society about a restive slave populace, and revealed the tribulations of the slaves themselves.4 This study, based upon the records of 62 slave trials involving 73 defendants and spanning the years 1715 to 1785, attempts to explain the judicial process used by one colony to contend with crimes committed by slaves. Of course, only a fraction of the proceedings have survived. Allusions to trials for which there are no extant documents appear in fragmentary county records and in provin cial and state legislative papers. However, the random character of the existing records, which involve over half of North Carolina's mid-eighteenth-century counties, including those in which slavery was most pronounced, suggests that the sample adequately reflects the nature of the slave trial in early North Caro lina. The slave court appeared in North Carolina by statutory mandate in 1715. According to legislation in that year, a slave deemed guilty of "any Crime or Of fence whatsoever" could be tried by three justices of the peace, who were in variably slaveowners, and three slaveowning freeholders of the precinct (later county) in which the crime occurred. The senior magistrate of the three justices determined the time and place of the trial. A simple majority of the court was sufficient "to pass Judgment for life or Member or any other Corporal Punish ment" and to ensure the execution of the sentence. In 1741 a more detailed law provided for the imprisonment of any slave suspected of criminal activity.
