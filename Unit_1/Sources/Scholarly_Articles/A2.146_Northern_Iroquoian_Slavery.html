Northern Iroquoian Slavery William A. Starna and Ralph Watkins, SUNY, College at Oneonta Abstract. This article argues that slavery was practiced by Northern Iroquoians the context of what has been previously and exclusively described as an adoption complex. This conclusion is reached after applying Orlando Patterson's (I98 model of slavery to the ethnohistorical and ethnological data on Northern Iro quoians. The constituent elements of slavery, rituals of integration and incorpora tion, and the conditions of slavery are also discussed and documented. In a 1925 issue of American Anthropologist, William Christie MacLeod published an article entitled "Debtor and Chattel Slavery in Aborigina North America." Although this work is unsystematic and without a dis cernible theoretical basis, it does present one of the first comparative dis- cussions of indigenous slavery in the New World and explicitly addresses the presence of this institution among Northern Iroquoian population (cf. Averkieva I966). It follows earlier descriptions, or at least mentions, of slavery in such works as those of Joseph-Francois Lafitau (I977), Lucie Carr (I893), and Sara Henry Stites (I905). Since that time, the efforts o these and other writers have fallen into obscurity insofar as the issue of Iroquoian slavery is concerned. Anthropologists and historians familiar with the Jesuit Relations (Thwaites 1959 [I896-I9OI]; hereafter cited as JR) know that the term slave (esclave) appears there with great regularity. Invariably, it is ap plied in reference to prisoners of war or captives, abductees, and othe nonnatal or nonnative individuals residing in Iroquoian villages.' None theless, to the best of our knowledge, no one in Iroquoian studies ha considered the application, use, meaning, or appropriateness of the ter slave in any serious or systematic fashion. Northern Iroquoian Slavery that the identifier slave has been generally dismissed as having resulted from the Jesuits' ignorance of Iroquoian culture systems, their misreading of the adoption complex, and their ethnocentric bias. On the other hand, it is equally possible that an ignorance of scholars concerning Jesuit cul- tural systems is being expressed, especially concerning what Jesuits knew and had experienced about slavery.2 As far as we know, only two contemporary writers have ever for- mally addressed the question of Iroquoian slavery.3 Anthropologist Bruce Trigger (1976, z: 830) writes: "The French called such prisoners slaves, in reference to the brutal manner in which they were treated. The term failed, however, to take account of the kinship basis of Iroquois society and of the fact that the ultimate aim of this brutality was the assimilation of these aliens as full members of Iroquois society." Daniel Richter (forth- coming), a historian, states simply and without elaboration that slavery is an impossibility among the Iroquois, given what he terms their "relatively egalitarian economies." It is our contention that both Trigger and Richter have moved too quickly and without sufficient examination to solve the problematical issue of slavery among Northern Iroquoians and, importantly, have not fully considered precisely what constitutes slavery. We hasten to add that this is not a stance peculiar to anthropologists or historians. Igor Kopytoff (1982: 2o7; cf. Watson I980: I-3), an anthropologist, clearly focuses the question when he asks, "Why has modern anthropology, which claims that nothing human is alien to it, consistently ignored so widespread a phenomenon?" A significant part of the answer to this question is the fact that there has been an almost exclusive dependence on African-American or New World nonindigenous slavery systems as a comparative form. The result is a formulation or a concept of slave systems based on "our folk ideas and, by osmosis, our scholarly ideas about slavery" (Kopytoff I982: 225). In point of fact, nonindigenous New World slavery systems represent a minority in terms of the overall picture of slavery. Of greater concern and comparative worth are the more numerous indigenous slavery systems of the Old World, including those often-neglected areas of Southeast Asia, Korea, and Muscovite Russia, among others (ibid.; cf. Patterson i982; Rubin and Tuden 1977). Further confusing the question have been the biases and avoidances characterizing and limiting Western scholarly thought in regard to slavery and slave systems.
