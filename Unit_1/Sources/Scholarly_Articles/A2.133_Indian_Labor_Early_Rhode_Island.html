Selection Overview

Sainsbury argues that King Philip's War broke the power of the Narragansetts and that surviving Rhode Island Indians, who rarely resumed tribal life, became economically dependent on the colony's whites, many living in various degrees of servitude with white families. He adds that Indians from the Spanish colonies and Tuscarora captives were brought into New England in the early eighteenth century.

INDIAN LABOR IN EARLY RHODE ISLAND JOHN A. SAINSBURY H ISTORICAL interest in the Indians of southern New England has tended to focus on the early period of colo- nial settlement, when the major tribes still maintained co- herent social structures and presented some threat to English security. The period following King Philip's war of 1675-1676 has been hitherto neglected. This conflict broke the power of the Narragansetts, the dominant tribe of Rhode Island and perhaps the most formidable of the Indian union, virtually ending their existence as a functioning tribal unit. Only a degenerate remnant attempted to preserve a traditional life under the sachem of the Niantic tribe, King Ninigret.1 Most of the surviving Rhode Island Indians never resumed their tribal habits; instead they became economically dependent, either voluntarily or involuntarily, on the whites of the colony. Although their situation has received scant attention from subsequent commentators, it was noted by contemporaries. Dean Berkeley, in a sermon before the Society for the Propa- gation of the Gospel in 1731, stated that the Rhode Island Indians were "nearly all servants or labourers for the En- glish,"2 and John Callender, seven years later, wrote that the Narragansetts not "sheltered under the successors of Ninigret" were "scattered about where the English will employ them."3 The disintegration of the Narragansetts continued through- out the eighteenth century and increased Indian dependence on whites for employment. Many Indians were consequently forced to find alternative subsistence elsewhere.4 In addition to the native Rhode Island Indians who were entering a sub- servient association with whites, Indians were imported from outside the colony, especially during the early eighteenth cen- tury. Indians from the Spanish colonies, taken mainly in the War of Spanish Succession, and Tuscarora Indians captured during their tribe's conflict with the Carolinas in 1711-1713, added to a movement of Indians from the South to the New England colonies, including Rhode Island; this flow was even- tually stopped by legislative action from the New England colonial governments.5 The extent to which Rhode Island Indians had become integrated in various degrees of servitude with white fami towards the end of the colonial period is demonstrated by fact that 35.5 percent of all Indians in the colony were l with white families in 1774; and if the Indians still livin the Charlestown reservation are excluded, the figure ris 54 percent.
