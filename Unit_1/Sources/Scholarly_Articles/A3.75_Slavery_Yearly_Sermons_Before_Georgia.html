Selection Overview

Hertzler argues that on the question of Black slavery in Georgia the yearly anniversary sermons preached before the Trustees did not reinforce the Trustees' ban but, by implication, undercut it. He notes that none of the preachers came out openly for slavery in Georgia, yet most seemed to think the practice, in a Christian context, held good for both slave and master.

Hertzler* origin of slavery in Georgia has been examined in monographs written in the early part of this century,1 and the first part of the Trustee period has been analyzed in a recent dissertation.2 Actions of the Trustees for the Georgia Corporation prohibited slavery at the beginning of the colony, but constant pressure from many sources became so intense and unrelenting that finally, in 1750, the Trustees relaxed the ruling which prohibited slaves in Georgia. While pro-slavery agitation was strong among some of the most vocal of the Georgia colonists,3 Negroes were not desired by all the settlers. The Scots at Darien and the Salzburgers at Ebenezer petitioned the Trustees that Negroes not be allowed, for economic security, and humane reasons.4 James Oglethorpe, himself one of the Trustees, consistently opposed Negro slavery in Georgia and reported that the white laboring men in the colony feared that slavery would endanger their opportunities for work.5 The Trustees were pushed and tugged on this issue not only by the colonists in Georgia, but by forces in England as well. In 1740, Parliament began to consider the law prohibiting slavery in Georgia. Some members urged the admission of Negroes into Georgia and in 1742 there was a three-hour debate on this matter in the House of Commons.
