Selection Overview

Merrell (historiography) reviews how slowly students of early America answered the 1953 call to study Indian-white relations and argues that the field has now arrived. He urges comparing Indian and European communities and studying the contacts among all the peoples on the American landscape, European, Indian, and African, so as to recover the world they made together.

Over the past two decades more people have taken Fenton's advice, and once again the Quarterly helps measure the trend. The last twenty volumes contain just over a score of articles relating to Indians, not to mention three primary sources and as many review essays-one more review essay than even republicanism or Puritanism can claim!4 Add to this the articles on the topic that have appeared elsewhere and the books that have come out, and it appears that Fenton did not visit Williamsburg in vain after all. The study of Indians in colonial British America clearly has arrived, and- if the number of younger scholars busily scratching away at their own plots of that common ground is any indication-it is just as clearly here to stay. The good news, then, is worth celebrating: more people are seeking to understand the native American experience from Columbian Exchange to Jacksonian Removal, and their efforts are being accorded a more promi- nent place in the lists of book publishers and the pages of scholarly journals.
