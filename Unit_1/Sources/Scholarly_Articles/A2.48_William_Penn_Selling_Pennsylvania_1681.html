William Penn and the Selling of Pennsylvania, 1681-1685 RICHARD S. DUNN Professor of History, University of Pennsylvania W illiam Penn is best remembered as a visionary idealist who sup- posed that the men and women who settled in Pennsylvania three hundred years ago would shed their old world hos- tilities, and learn to live together in peace and brotherly harmony. However utopian or foolish Penn's holy experiment may have been, he proved to be an astute and effective salesman. He persuaded Charles II, a cynical and nearly bankrupt monarch, to give him 45,000 square miles of American real estate; he persuaded about six hundred investors to buy shares in this new colony; and he persuaded about four thousand people to join him in emigrating to Pennsylvania. Penn's advertising campaign for his new colony in the early 1680s was the most suc- cessful English colonial recruitment drive since the Puritans had founded Massachu- setts fifty years before. Penn's entrepreneu- rial skill drew nearly fifty shiploads of pas- sengers and cargo to the Delaware River in 1682-1683, and provided the necessary momentum that got the Quaker colony off to a successful start. Penn's effectiveness as a salesman for Pennsylvania seems particularly striking when we consider that emigration from Brit- ain to the other American colonies was de- clining sharply during the 1670s and 1680s.1 The young people who had flocked to New England, the Chesapeake, and the West In- dian islands from the 1620s through the 1660s were now staying at home, because more jobs were available for them in En- gland during the closing decades of the sev- enteenth century.2 Of course Penn was par- ticularly interested in recruiting Quaker investors and colonists, and since the Quak- ers were brutally persecuted in Britain dur- ing the 1670s and 1680s, they had special reasons for wishing to leave. However, Quaker interest in Pennsylvania was not a foregone conclusion. Many Quakers had grave doubts about William Penn's holy experiment. They believed that the true holy experiment was in England, where God's chosen people were being tested through persecution, and that Quakers who escaped this suffering by emigration to Pennsylvania were betraying their divine mission. And those Quakers who shared Penn's call to America had other choices besides Penn- sylvania. West New Jersey and East New Jersey were both Quaker colonies in the 1680s, and the leaders of these two colonies mounted their own advertising campaigns.3 Yet Penn sold much more land and recruited many more colonists than his Jersey neigh- bors. Why was he such a successful sales- man? And what sort of people did he per- suade to invest in Pennsylvania? Inspection of William Penn's business records suggests some answers to these questions. Penn's business records have not received the attention they deserve. The first point to establish is that Penn was in poor financial shape in the early 1680s; hence, one of his prime reasons for wanting to start an American colony was to salvage his fortune. Penn gave no surface impression of being in financial difficulties; he lived comfortably, spent freely, and en- joyed a handsome annual income from his landed estates in Ireland and England. But for many years he had been living well beyond his means, and by 1680 he had contracted loans that totaled more than ?10,000-the equivalent in today's currency of more than a million dollars.4 Thus Penn badly needed a fresh source of income. He grounded his request to Charles II on a claim that the crown owed his father, the late Admiral Penn, a debt of ?11,000 incurred for victualling expenses during the Dutch war of 1665-1667.5 Had the king simply repaid the admiral's debt in 1680, William Penn's financial problems would have been solved. But Penn did not ask for this, be- cause he knew that he had a much better chance of obtaining American land than English cash from the impecunious king. When Charles II granted him the Pennsyl- vania charter in March 1681, Penn an- nounced the news to his fellow Quakers in the following words: "Perhaps, this way of satisfaction has more of the hand of God in it, than a downright payment."6 The king's grant enabled him to expand his ser- vice to Quakerism, and to the cause of re- ligious and political liberty-and at the same time he could make money by attract- ing colonists to Pennsylvania and selling land to them. In the eighteen months between March 1681, when he received the charter, and August 1682, when he left for America, Penn plunged energetically into the busi- ness of promoting and selling his colony. He borrowed many of his promotional tech- niques from earlier colonizers, but he also drew heavily upon his widespread personal connections within the Society of Friends. To start with, he advertised Pennsylvania more widely and shrewdly than any other English colony in the 1680s. Penn was a seasoned publicist, for by 1680 he had pub- lished about fifty books, pamphlets, and broadsides in defense of Quakerism.7 Many of these Quaker apologetics had been bel- ligerent in tone and provocative in content, and Penn was careful to compose his Penn- sylvania promotional tracts in a far more modest and deliberately understated style. Unlike earlier colonial publicists, he never claimed that Pennsylvania was a natural paradise, nor that investment in his project was sure to produce sensational profits. Penn's first promotional effort was a ten- page tract, Some Account of the Province of Pennsylvania in America, which he then re- worked and issued in abridged form as a broadside entitled A Brief Account of the Province of Pennsylvania. In 1682 he pub- lished the Frame of Government or consti- tution that he had devised for the colony, thus presenting solid proof that the colonists would enjoy generous political and religious liberties. By 1685 Penn had composed nine promotional pamphlets about his colony, most of which were published in several different printings or editions.8 As he had done previously with his religious literature, Penn distributed copies of these promo- tional tracts among the leading Quakers he knew in England, Ireland, Scotland, Wales, and Holland. Friends in Rotterdam and Am- sterdam translated a number of these pieces for circulation among Dutch and German readers who knew about Penn from his travels in Holland and the Rhineland in 1671 and 1677.9 In these promotional writ- ings Penn made no mention of his Quak- erism, nor of his plans for a holy experi- ment. He did not need to, for he was well identified as a Quaker. But Penn was clearly trying to reach a non-Quaker audience as well.
