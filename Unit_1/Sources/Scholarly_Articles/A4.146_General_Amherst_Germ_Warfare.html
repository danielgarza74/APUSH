General Amherst and Germ Warfare
Bernhard Knollenberg
 
ARTICLE General Amherst and Germ Warfare
JOURNAL The Mississippi Valley Historical Review 41, no. 3 (1954)
EXCERPT Original journal pages 489-490
JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/1897495
Selection Overview

Knollenberg re-examines the evidence that British officers spread smallpox among hostile Indians during Pontiac’s Rebellion, and finds that earlier accounts by Parkman and Peckham drew conclusions about British responsibility that the evidence does not support. He grants that some British officers harbored a "detestable" intent, but argues that carrying it out is not shown even by circumstantial evidence.

Generul Amherst und Germ W/arfdre By BERNHARD KNOLLENBERG In the course of a study of the use of germ warfare in North America in the eighteenth century, the writer had occasion to re- view the evidence concerning the supposed spreading of smallpox by the British among the hostile Indians during Pontiac's Rebellion of 1763-1764. Readers of the works of Francis Parkman and Howard H. Peckham ' on the Pontiac conspiracy and of the bi- ographies of General Jeffery Amherst 2 are familiar with the incident. Parkman, whose account seems to have been relied on by later writers, stated that in July, 1763, Colonel Henry Bouquet at Car- lisle, Pennsylvania, wrote Amherst of the loss of the British posts at Presqu' Isle, Le Boeuf, and Venango, and that in replying Am- herst made the "detestable suggestion" that smallpox be spread among the "disaffected tribes of Indians." Quoting from some of the items cited below, Parkman gave the impression that Amherst and Bouquet were conspirators and that they may have contrived to execute their scheme. His conclusion was that "There is no direct evidence that Bouquet carried into effect the shameful plan of infecting the Indians though, a few months after, the small-pox was known to have made havoc among the tribes of the Ohio." Peckham added: "A Swiss soldier of fortune, Ecuyer regarded the Indians as savage enemies to be eliminated by any means available. A fatal epidemic was as lawful a method as shooting. A white captive among the Delawares later testified that smallpox raged among them, the Shawnees, and the Mingoes all summer and was still prevalent the next spring. It certainly affected their vigorous prosecution of the war."4 The implication in these accounts is that officers of the British army had employed germ warfare and were apparently responsible for the smallpox epidemic which broke out among the Indians. These conclusions require examination in the light of the material used by Parkman and Peckham, and of other available sources. The following items, arranged chronologically, summarize the evi- dence.' June 23, 1763. Colonel Henry Bouquet, commander of the Southern Department of the British Army in North America, with headquarters in Philadelphia, wrote Amherst, commander in chief, telling of a letter received from Captain Simeon Ecuyer, command- ing at Fort Pitt, reporting an Indian uprising in that region. Bou- quet added: "Unluckily the small Pox has broken out, for which he [Ecuyer] has built an Hospital. ..." June 23. Amherst, in New York, wrote Bouquet, reporting the word from Detroit of Pontiac's attempt, on May 8, to seize the fort there by treachery and of the surprise and killing of Sir Robert Davers, Lieutenant Charles Robertson, and others by Indians near Detroit. Commenting on this news Amherst said: "I cannot but wish, that Whenever we have any of the Savages in our Power, who have in so Treacherous a way Committed any Barbarities on our People, a Quick Retaliation may be made, without the least Exception or hesitation." But neither this letter nor several suc- ceeding letters quoted below between Amherst and Bouquet, ex- pressing their detestation of the Indians for their treachery and barbarities, mention infecting the Indians with smallpox.
