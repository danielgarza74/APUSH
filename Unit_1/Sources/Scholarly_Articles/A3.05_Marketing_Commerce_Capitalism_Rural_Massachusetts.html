Marketing, Commerce, and Capitalism in Rural Massachusetts MICHAEL A. BERNSTEIN AND SEAN WILENTZ The recent exchange between Winifred B. Rothenberg and Rona S. Weiss was a welcome and stimulating contribution to the growing scholarship on early agriculture in the northern United States.' It also echoed older debates among economic, political, and social historians regarding historical method. It seemed to us, however, that in some respects Rothenberg and Weiss argued at cross purposes. We would like to try to help clarify matters so that the important suggestions of both authors are not lost in the heat of Methodenstreit. The points at issue in the exchange are of paramount importance, bearing as they do on almost every controversial issue in antebellum American history. Let us begin with Weiss's remarks. Her concern is with the historical development of capitalism in Massachusetts in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. Her focus is on distinct social relationships which could, at least in principle, be discerned from evidence. Among other things, for example, it should be possible to determine from account books, wage lists, and other material whether rural wage labor was paid on the basis of a capitalist market in labor power or on the basis of non-capitalist forms of reciprocity and justice. Overall, for Weiss, the particular object of analysis is the social transformation whereby classes emerge, and a new ruling class establishes hegemony over the economy, society, and politics-an analysis that Marxists and non-Marxists have shown can be undertaken with empirical rigor.2 Such an analysis constitutes a large and important agenda for American historians, involving the determination of the extent to which capitalism and its attendant social relations existed in America in a given epoch-a testing of the long-asserted (but never verified) claim that capitalism arrived in this country with the first shiploads of white settlers.3 Rothenberg's concern is with markets and commercial activity-not with capitalism and relations of production. The subject of her enquiry may be related but it is hardly identical to Weiss's; Rothenberg is, in our view, perfectly correct to claim that propositions about marketing can be established independently of any propositions about capitalism. Rothenberg's method allows her to quantify certain forms of behavior involving the movement of surplus output to market and the determination of its market price. She believes that the specificity of her definition of commercial activity provides the outlines of a verifiable, objective test of the extent to which Massachusetts farmers were commercially oriented. The problem here is that while Rothenberg need not refer to capitalism, she does refer to "commercialism," thereby blurring the distinction between marketing and com- merce. In fact, her calculations alone tell us nothing about commercial orientation. Her exclusive focus on some of the formal aspects of marketing behavior, divorced from any consideration of exchange relations or cultural variation, obscures some very important issues. How, why, and for what ends did marketing come about? What, in fact, occurred in the markets apart from what she infers from her behavioral data? If historians express no interest in such questions, they must assume both that marketing emerges out of an innate and overriding propensity to "truck, barter, and exchange," and that throughout all history, exchanges of market goods, for cash or barter, have involved some fixed economic principles, psychological imperatives, cultural desires, and exchange rela- tions. Alas, for Rothenberg's argument, historians and anthropologists have shown that just the opposite can be the case-that in other times and other places, marketing behavior (for example, price convergence) which may seem to our eyes to involve commercial exchange relations and profit-maximization may actually involve very different forms of exchange and reciprocity, forms in which the pursuit of self-interest is alien and in some cases antagonistic to commercial relations.4 These historical and anthropological studies proceed not from any preconceived notions about the mentality of those being studied. Rather, they proceed from testable, empirical evidence to show that marketing in various times and places has involved forms of exchange that cannot be called commercial or profit-maximizing. Of course, none of this excludes the possibility that marketing in eighteenth century Massachusetts was commercial. But neither is it defensible to assume, as Rothenberg does in her original article and in her reply to Weiss, that her data on marketing provide prima facie evidence of commercial exchange. Rothenberg may choose to ignore or dismiss the historical and anthropological literature that runs counter to her assumptions. Even so, she must then provide, in her words, her own "functional language in which to speak about" human motivations and forms of exchange in eighteenth century Massachusetts-a "language" sensitive to historical change and cultural variation-if she is to remove her own ideological and cultural biases about the market from her analysis. Until she does so, her criticisms of those she labels "New Left" historians remain at best irrelevant.5 Our own hunch is that Rothenberg and Weiss really disagree about the significance of marketing activity in early Massachusetts. But neither has clearly stated what she thinks that significance is. For Weiss, the primary question is the existence of capitalism. "Capitalism" is, of course, a malleable term of fairly recent origin, one that covers a cluster of economic, social, political, legal, and cultural relations.
