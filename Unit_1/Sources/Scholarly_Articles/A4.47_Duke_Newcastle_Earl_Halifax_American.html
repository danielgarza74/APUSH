Selection Overview

Clayton argues that the Newcastle ministry perceived French ambitions in America from 1749 and made a real attempt to head off general war, but that its efforts were defeated by maneuvering in London and by events in the Ohio country.

CLAYTON St Joohn's College, Cambridge I On the fourth of July 1754 a garrison of Virginians, under the command of the young George Washington, marched its British colours out of a small log fort in an isolated valley of the Appalachian mountains, where it had capitulated to a French detachment the previous evening. Washington's defeat had an impact upon world history no less significant than did his more famous victories subsequent to a more dramatic removal of British colours, on another fourth ofJuly twenty-two years later. The French expulsion of British colonials from the Ohio valley led to nine years of war in America and quickly escalated into seven years of general war, so wide in its geographical extent that Churchill called it the first world war. At the end of hostilities in 1763 the acquisition from France of Canada and a number of West Indian islands laid the foundations of the nineteenth-century British empire. The question of why the events in a remote part of the Ohio valley escalated into a general war is an important one, but the American origins of the war have been surprisingly neglected by historians.' CLAYTON Higonnet, the duke of Newcastle, secretary of state for the northern department until March 1754 and first minister thereafter, expressed no real concern about the Anglo-French skirmishes in America before May 1754: indeed all British ministers did not really care about the Ohio valley until they were persuaded of its importance by the lieutenant-governor of Virginia, Robert Dinwiddie. The British claim to the Ohio region was, Higonnet claims, really a very thin one. Dinwiddie is said to have convinced Whitehall, particularly the Board of Trade under its industrious new president the earl of Halifax, that the British claim to the Ohio was, however, indisputable. Dinwiddie's motives were allegedly those of 'greed, incompetence, or imperialism, but in any case from private motive'. This is said to have been important because 'Had Halifax not backed Dinwiddie, and Dinwiddie not backed the Ohio Company, Newcastle might never have had to fight over Fort Necessity, for it would not have existed'.3 But this now conventional interpretation is both misleading and in many ways erroneous. The argument expressed here is that the American origins of the Seven Years' War did not involve a series of historical accidents triggered by the private motives of British colonials. The formulation of British policy was decisively influenced not by cupidity in America, but by chicanery in London and the courts of Europe. But the chicanery in London did not begin until the autumn of 1754, when an opposition faction led by the duke of Cumberland outmanoeuvred the Newcastle ministry. Throughout the period 1748-55 the duke of Newcastle's perception of French ambitions in America was a reasonable response to the information he was receiving. A series of openly aggressive actions begun by the governors of New France after 1748 were perceived in London as part of a general threat to both the security of the British American colonies and the peace of Europe. The Board of Trade had been genuinely convinced, long before it had even heard from Dinwiddie, that France had no claim to the Ohio valley. Newcastle and Halifax seriously believed that unless the French attempts to encroach upon and encircle the British colonies from Georgia to Nova Scotia were defeated, the very existence of those colonies would be threatened on the expected resumption of war with France. It will be shown that every colonial governor who wrote to the Board of Trade about such matters expressed sentiments similar to those of Dinwiddie, but it will be argued that the Board did not simply follow the lead of colonial governors. Reports of French encroachments in North America were passed from the Board of Trade to the duke of Newcastle by the earl of Halifax. This information only further confirmed Newcastle in the opinion that he had expressed on many occasions after 1749 that the French intended to provoke a resumption of war when they had insinuated themselves into a position stronger than their situation at the end of the War of the Austrian Succession in 1748.
