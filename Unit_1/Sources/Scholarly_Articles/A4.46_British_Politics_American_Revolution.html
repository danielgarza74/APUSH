British Politics and the American Revolution
Ian R. Christie

EXCERPT Original journal pages 205-206

Selection Overview

Christie argues that British participation in the events leading to the Revolution has been explained in the wrong terms and that British politics cannot be understood without the policies at issue. He holds that the heavy emphasis on constitutional controversies has obscured British colonial policy, and he ends by noting that British opposition groups genuinely feared a threat to the constitution yet never organized a revolutionary movement, so that British political attitudes grew apart from those of Americans.

British Politics and The American Revolution* Ian R. Christie A paper of this title must inevitably be extremely selective. The sub- ject is complex and the available material vast in bulk. As Paul Langford has recently shown with his study on the first Rockingham administration' one could conceivably do justice to it at the rate of one volume a year for much of the period 1763 to 1776. I can do no more in the compass of this paper than pursue one or two themes which appear to be emerging from the mass of recent detailed schol- arship. Moreover, I am going to take a major, though I hope justi- fied liberty with my title, and treat of policies as well as politics. After all the second is not fully intelligible without the first. This paper does not adopt a "structure of politics" approach. Thanks to the initiatives of Sir Lewis Namier in that field we now have a pretty full knowledge of the structural framework of British politics during the revolutionary crisis, and this provides useful guidelines for understanding the ebb and flow of political controversy-as the re- cent work of Paul Langford and Peter Thomas bears admirable wit- ness.' But knowledge of political structure tells us little about the e sence of the arguments connected with the American question. We cannot understand the politics unless we understand the policies which were at issue. My second reason for devoting part of this paper to policies is my belief that some re-orientation of perspective is necessary if we are to understand the British participation in the events leading up to the American Revolution. It appears to me that at present the British involvement tends to be explained in the wrong terms. However, these various studies, excellent in their special fields, have not fully accounted for the drive and direction of British policy in the years of the American revolutionary crisis, have not pulled the separate as- pects each writer has treated into a common focus. Before I unmask my batteries and put forward my own sugges- tions about this 'common focus,' there is a further reflection about the historiography of this subject which I should like to put before you. It is my view that a proper understanding of British colonial policy has actually been obscured by the excessive amount of atten- tion and emphasis given to the constitutional controversies of the re- volutionary period. It is easy to understand the preoccupation of American historians with these controversies. Political and historical tradition in the United States has led to emphasis on two themes of fundamental importance connected with the origin of the American state: the struggle for liberty, and the containment of the exercise of political power. As a tool for unraveling events in America after 1763 this tradition seems to me most valuable and serviceable. It does, however, tend to mask sight of the real springs of British pol- icy. It leads towards over-simple explanations of the conflict between Britain and the American colonies in terms of constitutional black and white, or right and wrong, resting on the assumptions that the British were either very ignorant, or very corrupt, or very sinful, or all three, to fail to grasp the rectitude of the colonists' position.
