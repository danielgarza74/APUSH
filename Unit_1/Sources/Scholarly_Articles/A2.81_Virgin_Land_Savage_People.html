Virgin Land and Savage People
Francis Jennings
 
ARTICLE Virgin Land and Savage People
JOURNAL American Quarterly 23, no. 4 (1971)
EXCERPT Original journal pages 519-520
JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/2711704
Selection Overview

Jennings argues that the “virgin land” idea, which he finds intrinsic to Turner’s frontier theory, was a myth subsidiary to the larger myth of civilization overpowering savagery, and that the earliest explorers and colonizers did not hold it; he follows its development and revision in seventeenth-century New England through the Indian deeds, and concludes that historians must reject the civilization-savagery mythology and study Euro-Americans and Amerindians within a single process of acculturation.

FRANCIS JENNINGS Cedar Crest College Virgin Land and Savage Peoplel PROBABLY THE MOST COMPREHENSIVE OF AMERICAN CONQUEST MYTHS IS that which presents European colonization and Indian subjection in terms of civilization (Good) overpowering and annihilating savagery (Evil). Within the framework of this conception, strife between two social orders is transmuted into a theory of progress, the victors become a chosen people and their conquered opponents become nonpeople. A glance at any news- paper editorial page reveals contemporary variants of the chosen people self-image. In spite of modern sophistication, ethnocentric delusions of in- herent superiority still powerfully sway intellectuals as well as the multi- tude. A good beginning has been made in the study of the origins of these ideas insofar as they derive from Euro-Amerindian relationships. Roy Harvey Pearce has shown how "civilization" was defined as the reciprocal of "savagery" to such a degree that the two conceptions merged in one myth.2 Wilcomb E. Washburn has described "The Moral and Legal Justi- fications for Dispossessing the Indians," with particular emphasis on the southern colonies and states.3 The present study pursues similar interests in other contexts. It attempts first to show the development of a "virgin land" myth subsidiary to that of savagery, and then the application and revision of that myth in 17th century New England. For method it relies heavily on new analyses of the nature and function of the Indian deeds so prominent in the records of that time. 520 American Quarterly The conception of America as virgin land is intrinsic to the frontier theory of Frederick Jackson Turner and his followers. In its fully devel- oped form it has been stated thus: "The frontier movement is an invasion of a land assumed to be vacant as distinguished from an invasion of an oc- cupied or civilized country, an advance against nature rather than against men. . . Inherent in the American concept of a moving frontier is the idea of a body of free land which can be had for the taking."4 Such conceptions are not to be found in the writings of the earliest ex- plorers and colonizers, who routinely assumed that their first business was to find the residents of their new worlds. As Swedish Peter Lindestrom put it, "the trade, traffic and commerce of the Christians would be of small value in that place, if the savages would not be there."5 For Virginians, change came with the discovery of tobacco's value as a staple commodity. Whereas the fur trade had required Indian trappers and hunters, tobacco could be produced more lucratively by the colonists themselves. Indians were transformed from participants in the old trade to hindrances upon the new trade. The reason was precisely contrary to George E. Ellis' dictum that Indians "required enormous spaces of wilder- ness for their mode of existence."6 What Virginia's colonists wanted from their Indian neighbors was cleared land. It was the Indian farmer, not the Indian hunter, who stood in the way.7 In 1622 the alarmed Indians rose in a desperate effort to drive away or exterminate the intruders. The failure of that rising entailed large con- sequences in both the world of reality and that of ideas. In London, Samuel Purchas took the long view and gave shape to certain ideas that grew to dominate English thought about the land and native inhabitants of Amer- ica. Purchas' distinction was the discovery of the nonperson-nonland qualities of savages and the world they lived in.
