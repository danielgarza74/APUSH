The Swap Not Heard ’Round the World: Ordering the Declaration’s Grievances
Robert G. Parkinson (credentials not shown on page)
 
AUTHOR Robert G. Parkinson (credentials not shown on page)
WEBSITE Journal of the American Revolution (All Things Liberty)
PUBLICATION September 1, 2026
Selection Overview

Parkinson argues that Congress's editing of the Declaration's final grievances shows what its delegates feared in 1776. He says they cut Jefferson's slave-trade passage, added a charge that the King had "excited domestic insurrections," and swapped the last two grievances so the list ended with enslaved and Native people allied with the King.

Excerpt · 1161 words

Jefferson’s slave trade grievance caused a ruckus in Congress. “Southern gentlemen whose reflections were not yet matured to the full abhorrence of that traffic” objected, he later remembered. But not only them. “Our northern brethren also I believe felt a little tender under those censures, for tho’ their people have very few slaves themselves yet they had been pretty considerable carriers of them to others.” Jefferson squirmed as delegates took their pens to this particular part of his draft. They cut the reference to “treasonable insurrections” and the past tense portion of the slave trade grievance. The present tense part, however, was more than acceptable. Alerting the colonial population to British attempts to “incite,” “excite,” or otherwise “tamper with” the enslaved population in America had been one of their chief concerns over the past year.

Whereas news of slave insurrections had been one of the most concealed and silenced topics in print before the 1775, now it was something the Revolution’s leaders broadcast as widely as they could. One British official wrote at the end of 1775, “the newspapers were full of Publications calculated to excite the fears of the People . . . Massacres and instigated insurrections were Words in the mouth of every Child.”

That had not happened accidently; patriot political leaders like John Adams, Benjamin Franklin, and their colleagues had put those words in their mouths.

[. . .]

The delegates to Congress were not going to let this opportunity slip away. After all their investment making the public aware of British conspiracies to get enslaved people to participate in putting down the Revolution, they had to feature it in the Declaration. So they wrote a new phrase: “He has excited domestic insurrections amongst us.” The term “domestics” was a popular euphemism for enslaved people in 1776. They added it to the grievance referencing Native peoples aiding the King, in effect crafting a double-shot at the King for plotting to get all sorts of people to help destroy American liberty. That grievance would now read: “He has excited domestic insurrections amongst us and has endeavoured to bring on the merciless Indian savages, whose known rule of warfare is an undistinguished destruction of all ages, sexes, and conditions.”

[. . .]

Then they did something that has gone almost entirely unnoticed for two hundred and fifty years. Had they stopped there, the order of the final grievances would have been: 23. Waging war; 24. Destroyed our lives; 25. Foreign mercenaries; 26. Enslaved people and Native people; 27. Impressment.

But that’s not the order in which they appear in the Declaration. The final copy does not end with impressment. That means someone in the room made a motion to swap the order of the final two grievances.

On Jefferson’s rough draft, now preserved at the Library of Congress, there are two tiny Xs marked in pencil next to the final grievances that indicate the swap. In even tinier handwriting Jefferson wrote “He has constrained, etc” next to where the impressment grievance was now to appear. That is the only suggestion of the swap. Ever after, when Jefferson made copies of his rough draft for his friends, like for James Madison in 1783, he silently made the swap, as if it had been his idea all along.

In all of our many, many studies of the Declaration, only one scholar has noticed this before. In 1943, Julian Parks Boyd, who would soon become the editor of the Papers of Thomas Jefferson, auditioned for the job by writing a forensic analysis of all the minute changes to the Declaration’s drafts as it went through its many rounds of editing. In The Declaration of Independence: Evolution of the Text, Boyd wrote exactly four words about what happened: “Congress swapped the sequence.” That’s all. Not why they did so—or what it might mean. It has been a swap not heard ’round the world.

[. . .]

So what? Why did they make this move? None of the other grievances were switched around like this. Why these two?

Perhaps it was the problems surrounding impressment. Although impressment was undoubtedly a visceral charge, it was lacking in factual evidence and massive public outcry for the first year of the war. This was certainly not the case with African Americans and Indigenous people in 1775-1776. Congress needed the grievances to be iron-clad, to prove to the “candid world” their reasons for revolution were unimpeachable. William Barry was already carrying a great deal of water for the final one to rest on his shoulders.

Or maybe the swap was made was to placate Jefferson, who really wanted the grievances to end with a statement on slavery. We don’t know who raised their hand to suggest the change. Maybe it was Jefferson himself. But someone did.

Whoever made the motion, the swap tells us a great deal about what was going on in the minds of the fifty-five delegates to the Congress on July 4, 1776. There was and is enormous power in those two tiny pencil marks. It was not an accident that the bloody and visceral language of the twenty-seventh grievance seems like a rhetorical climax to the “long train of abuses” listed in the Declaration. The delegates took specific steps to ensure everyone who read or listened to the Declaration in 1776 would have that feeling. They were adamant that fears of enslaved and Native peoples working with the King must conclude the list. Even though it has gone almost entirely unnoticed, it is clear that, with purpose and intention, Congress made certain that the Declaration’s grievances would end with race.

All that fuss about the last few grievances reveals an extraordinary amount of contingency and chance. Jefferson, Adams, Franklin, and their colleagues rushed about during those hot days of late June and early July 1776 making lots of changes to this part of the Declaration, in part as a response to stories that were “ripped from the headlines.” There are still things we are still learning about those hectic days. Even in this most picked over of mines, hidden treasure remains. But what they landed on: embedding those particular words at that particular spot in that particular document proved to be a powerful statement about who was and was not included as part of the American people on July 4, 1776.

We need to understand the consequences of what happened during those days, of the power of the words Jefferson and Congress chose to feature at the Declaration’s climax. There were no asterisks there to exempt all the African Americans or Native peoples who sided with the Revolutionaries, of which there were considerable numbers. Rather, they were all lumped together as one collective set of enemies, working with the tyrant King, to deprive Americans of their life, liberty, and their capacity to pursue happiness. The notion that people of color are somehow not fully part of “us” or the “we” is not a recent phenomenon. It is as old as the United States itself.
