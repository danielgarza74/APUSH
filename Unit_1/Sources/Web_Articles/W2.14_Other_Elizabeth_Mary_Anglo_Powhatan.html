The Other Elizabeth and Mary: Anglo-Powhatan Marriages and the Virginia Company of London
Kelly L. Watson, PhD, Director of the Center for Women’s and Gender Studies, West Virginia University
 
AUTHOR Kelly L. Watson, PhD, Director of the Center for Women’s and Gender Studies, West Virginia University
WEBSITE Commonplace: The Journal of Early American Life
PUBLICATION July 2026
Selection Overview

Watson follows two Powhatan women, Elizabeth and Mary, who traveled to London with Pocahontas in 1616, and uses Elizabeth’s 1622 marriage in Bermuda to ask how the Virginia Company used marriage between English men and Native women as a tool of empire. She describes the roles of women and marriage in Powhatan society and argues that the Company’s hopes for peace and influence through these marriages failed.

Excerpt · 929 words

In the Spring of 1622, the English Governor of Bermuda, Nathaniel Butler, hosted a lavish wedding at his new home. More than one hundred guests were in attendance, including some who had only recently arrived on the island. There was a feast filled with “all sortes of prousions that the Illands could afford in a very plentifull manner.” Butler and others hoped that some of the guests, particularly those on their way to Virginia, would carry tales of the grandeur of the affair to their new homes. The fledgling Bermuda colony and its sponsor, the Virginia Company of London (VCOL), needed all the good press that it could get.

We know nothing about the groom except that he was reported to be “as fit and agreeable husband as the place would afford” (which, of course, was intended to be a little joke about the rough character of Bermuda planters). Surprisingly, we have slightly more information about the bride. We know that she was a Native woman from the Powhatan Confederacy in Virginia (or Tsenacomoco) who travelled to London in 1616 as part of Pocahontas’s retinue and that after her Christian baptism she took the name Elizabeth. No record of her prior name(s) exists. This marriage between a Powhatan woman and an English man is one of the few documented cases of its kind in the 17th century-Atlantic world. Of course, Pocahontas’s marriage to John Rolfe was the most famous union between a Native Virginian and an Englishmen.

In the first quarter of the 17th century, the Virginia Company of London experimented with the potential of Anglo-Powhatan marriages to foster peace, spread Christianity, and increase English territorial claims. Intercultural marriage had long been employed by imperial powers in their attempts to establish peace and dependency among colonized people. In this way, marriage served as a technology of imperialism by creating kinship relations and obligations between colonizer and colonized. In the Spanish colonies to the south, for example, the mixed-race offspring of white men and Indigenous women sometimes became powerful leaders who helped to translate, both literally and figuratively, the cultural divide between Europeans and Indigenous Americans. For the VCOL, their support of the marriages of Pocahontas and later Elizabeth, demonstrates both the financial precarity of the company and its ambitions to “civilize,” pacify, and profit from Virginia.

The Politics of Gender and Marriage in Virginia

Women’s roles in Tsenacomoco were complex and varied heavily depending upon the relative status of the woman and her maternal family line in addition to localized traditions. Women and men had parallel responsibilities with women taking the lead on household and most agricultural labor. Men were expected to hunt and participate in war parties. Powhatan society was not patriarchal in the modern sense: women were able to inherit chieftainships, but they did so rarely. The matrilineality of most Virginia tribes meant that power was inherited through the maternal line, but maternal male relatives were prioritized first before female relatives. So, for example, when a male werowance died, the position would typically be inherited by the closest male relative on the mother’s side. Englishmen often failed to understand these nuances of Indigenous power structures.

Most marriages in the Powhatan Confederacy were entered into voluntarily by both parties, but marrying for political reasons was quite common among the powerful. Powhatan himself had multiple wives, but this seems to have been uncommon outside of the highest status individuals. His polygamist household was always changing and growing; as the Confederacy acquired new allies and influence, Powhatan would marry a woman from these new communities. She would live among his household until she became pregnant and returned to her natal village. The child stayed with the mother for the first few years of their life but then moved to Powhatan’s household for their formative years. The mother was free to remarry after this point. Unlike English marriages, there was more flexibility, both in terms of permanency and sexual fidelity, among Viginia Native peoples. Women were eligible for marriage soon after puberty. Perhaps Elizabeth viewed her marriage to an Englishman in Bermuda as political and thus temporary.

Although men were in charge of formal diplomacy, women played key roles in local hospitality when English visitors arrived. It was quite common for English visitors to be offered a female bedfellow. This would not have been considered shameful, as sex was understood to be a key experience in developing stable friendships and alliances with other communities. As the daughters of werowances, Mary and Elizabeth likely would have been expected to marry for the benefit of their community.

We do not know what happened to Elizabeth after her marriage in Bermuda. There is simply no evidence to reveal if she ever returned to Virginia or if her marriage lasted. From the perspective of the VCOL these attempts to promote Powhatan-English marriages to solidify their power in Tsenacomoco came to naught. Pocahontas and Elizabeth were expected to birth a new generation of imperial citizens who would sing the praises of English power and culture but they failed to do so. Marriages between English men and elite Native women had the potential to advance English territorial claims, to grow their influence among Indigenous leaders, and to help spread Christianity. By employing marriage as a technology of imperialism, the Virginia Company hoped to turn its struggling overseas colonies into stable, productive English communities. When the promised peace didn’t come, the VCOL quickly pivoted in 1621 to sending willing white women to Jamestown as potential brides. This plan came with a greater likelihood of growing a stable, and decidedly English and Christian, population, in the Chesapeake.
