American Indian Influence on the U.S. Constitution
Robert J. Miller (credentials not shown on page)
 
AUTHOR Robert J. Miller (credentials not shown on page)
WEBSITE Encyclopedia Virginia (Virginia Humanities)
PUBLICATION September 16, 2026; updated October 28, 2026
Selection Overview

Miller argues that the founders knew Native governments through diplomacy and that Iroquois ideas of union and federalism, along with the practical problem of Indian affairs, helped shape the Constitution. The excerpt covers Franklin and the Albany Plan and Native examples of federalism and separated powers.

Excerpt · 994 words

Across what is now the United States and over hundreds of years, tribal governments encompassed a wide array of forms, from relatively simple to complex, and from nearly autocratic to very democratic. In addition, Indigenous Americans and tribal governments significantly shaped the history of the English colonies and the early American states and the United States. Indian affairs were some of the most critical “foreign affairs” that the states and the U.S. government faced in the early national period and were cited as a primary reason for developing the U.S. Constitution with its strong federal government.

Prominent founders such as George Washington, Benjamin Franklin, Thomas Jefferson, James Madison, James Wilson, and John Adams were acquainted with Indigenous peoples, tribal governments, and Indigenous theories of governance. Many had worked in diplomatic settings with Native nations as treaty negotiators and commissioners in the decades before the drafting of the U.S. Constitution, which helped shape their political thinking. Tribal cultures and governments had positive and negative influences on the Constitution, as the founders were influenced both by Native ideas regarding governance and by the perceived threats posed to the new nation by the existence of Indian nations.

Throughout his career, Benjamin Franklin had extensive contacts with the tribal nations while negotiating treaties, conducting diplomatic meetings, and printing Indian treaties for the Colony of Pennsylvania. In 1744, Franklin represented Pennsylvania at a significant diplomatic meeting with representatives of the Haudenosaunee, or Iroquois Confederacy, where Franklin and other colonial representatives were advised by the Iroquois leader Canasatego that the English colonies should form a union like the Iroquois Confederacy. Demonstrating how one arrow was easily broken but a bundle of arrows was unbreakable, Canasatego told them, “Our wise Forefathers established Union and Amity between the Five Nations; this has made us formidable; this has given us great Weight and Authority with our neighboring Nations. We are a powerful Confederacy; and, by your observing the same Methods our wise Forefathers have taken, you will acquire fresh Strength and Power.”

This advice did not go unheeded. Pennsylvania Governor George Thomas thanked Canasatego for this advice, Franklin printed Canasatego’s speech in 1744, and Cadwallader Colden, a well-known early American historian, reprinted it in his 1747 book on the Iroquois. In addition, Franklin wrote a widely distributed 1751 letter in which he noted, “It would be a very strange Thing, if six Nations of ignorant Savages should be capable of forming a . . . Union, and be able to execute it in such a Manner as that it has subsisted Ages, and appears indissoluble; and yet a like Union should be impracticable for ten or a Dozen English Colonies, to whom it is more necessary, and must be more advantageous.”

Historians note that Franklin applied Canasatego’s advice in 1754 when he began promoting his Albany Plan, which was one of the first proposals to advocate a union of the thirteen colonies and is cited as a forerunner of the U.S. Constitution. According to Franklin biographer Carl Van Doren, Franklin “admired the Iroquois confederation, and plainly had it in mind in his earliest discussion of the need of union among the colonies.” Historian Julian Boyd states that Franklin “found his materials” for the Albany Plan “in the great confederacy of the Iroquois.”

The more democratic Indian nations may have been better models for the founders than were European governments. James Wilson of Pennsylvania, a member of the Continental Congress’s Committee on Indian Affairs, an Indian commissioner, and treaty negotiator for Pennsylvania, said at the Constitutional Convention that “the British Government cannot be our model.” Charles Pinckney, a South Carolina Constitutional Convention delegate, said in 1788 that the delegates were skeptical of European governments since “from the European world no precedents are to be drawn for people who think they are capable of governing themselves.” John Adams analyzed various tribal government structures in his 1787 three-volume treatise on constitutions, where he appears to use Native governments as examples of the separation of powers. He thought it would be worthwhile “[t]o collect together the legislation of the Indians” because while the “sovereignty is in the nation . . . the three powers are strong in every tribe.” He said that “an accurate investigation of the form of government of the ancient Germans and modern Indians” would be worthy because “in both, the existence of the three divisions of power is marked with a precision that excludes all controversy.” Adams also noted that in Native governments the “democratical branch, especially, is so determined, that the real sovereignty resided in the body of the people and was exercised in the assembly of king, nobles, and commons together.” He pointed to the Mohawk Nation in particular as having “complete individual independence,” and noted that their tribal leaders deliberated national affairs in councils and left major decisions to “a national assembly.”

Most of the founders also would have been familiar with tribal governance as an example of federalism, where a federal government handles national affairs, but semi-autonomous towns and bands handle local issues. The Iroquois Confederacy, the Shawnee, Cherokee, and Creek, among others, operated under federalist principles, with national governing councils devoted to national and foreign affairs and tribal towns deciding local issues. The Iroquois Confederacy, for example, had a federalist system with a central government under a constitution known as the Great Binding Law of the Five Nations that was written on sacred wampum belts. Many of these federalist governments also had checks and balances on civil and military affairs to avoid the concentration of power in individual hands and protected a wide range of individual freedoms and democratic rights that the U.S. Constitution came to include, such as freedom of religion, citizens’ initiatives, veto and recall provisions, and women’s suffrage. Women played important roles in the Iroquois Confederacy and many other tribal governments and were heavily involved in political and economic decision-making, an Indigenous practice not adopted by the United States in the Constitution ratified in 1788.
