Bad Maps, Courtroom Clashes, and Agrarian Life in 17th-Century New England
Nathan Braccio, Assistant Professor of History, Clark University
 
AUTHOR Nathan Braccio, Assistant Professor of History, Clark University
WEBSITE Commonplace: The Journal of Early American Life
PUBLICATION October 2026
Selection Overview

Braccio argues that the crude sketch maps New England farmers drew in the 1660s and 1670s were useful to colonists even when they failed in court, because they presented a landscape of English ownership and left out Algonquian claims. He ties the maps to disputes over land seized from the Pequot and Niantic and to the settlers’ efforts to remake the region on English terms.

Excerpt · 915 words

The spatial culture of English colonists in the Northeast underwent major changes in the 1660s and 1670s. Colonists, buoyed by military victories over their Algonquian neighbors and a sense of political domination, sought to transform the Northeast into something more familiar to them. As they cut back forests, filled in swamps, built roads, and left marks of their English culture on the land, they increasingly employed surveyors and “artists” to describe it in an English manner. By the 1650s, these “artists,” trained in mathematics and drafting, traced some of the first survey maps of the region, generally when legal conflicts over borders emerged between towns or colonies. For colonists, these top-down visualizations were new. Previously, they had almost exclusively characterized landscapes (including borders) through written descriptions that mirrored perambulation. Perambulations were a traditional activity in English society in which communities would come together and walk around boundaries, noting important markers for future recollection, sometimes through mnemonics. In New England, town governments appointed local men to write accounts of walking past landmarks, including marked trees, rocks, stakes, roads, fences, and bodies of water. The subjective experience of the perambulator expressed in the document offered an eye-level perspective of traveling through the land; Such accounts centered the passage of time, as each sentence listed a new feature sequentially, mirroring the activity itself.

For colonial farmers in the 1660s, survey maps novelly visualized an entire plot of land. A glance replaced the need to read a multi-page description. Perhaps the aesthetic or technical qualities of these maps left lasting impressions on farmers. They likely would have first encountered them during clashes over borders in which a town or colony summoned surveyors to create maps to be powerful courtroom evidence. Many farmers may have seen their property threatened by such maps. In the 1640s and beyond, even the colonial governments of Massachusetts Bay (who made maps) and Connecticut (who initially did not) debated whether documents served as strong legal evidence in their border contests.

As mentioned above, New England townspeople drew these sketch maps as English spatial culture was changing. In addition to the advent of surveyors, colonists were shifting how they kept records about the landscape. While colonists regularly discussed Indigenous communities in their land records during their first twenty years in New England, often relying on their guidance and using their placenames, this was no longer the case by the 1640s. Colonists, at least on paper, replaced Algonquian experts with English surveyors. These surveyors wrote surveys and made maps using new placenames, coinciding with acts by colonial governments to rename towns with more English sounding names throughout the 1640s and 1650s. For example, in English records Tunxis became Farmington, Seacunck became Rehoboth, and Naswett became Eastham. I also found that the rise in intra-English land disputes furthered this archival shift; a proliferation of records using English terms and centering on English ownership of the land presented the illusion of a region devoid of Algonquian inhabitants.

These maps, and the land disputes they are inseparable from, need to be understood in that context. They visualized a colonial fantasy of “New England” that excluded the presence and often, the legacy of Algonquian-speaking communities. The New London/Lyme dispute took place on territory that the English had seized by “right of conquest” thirty-four years prior. Consequently, when one of the 1671 brawlers declared “The Land is ours and hear we will stand and spend our Blouds Befour we will lose itt,” he was echoing a legacy of violence towards Indigenous peoples. Pequot and Niantic wigwams still dotted the territory, albeit as “tenants” of English landlords or by “grants” declared by English towns. Tellingly, the testimony of all English involved ignored that the Niantic sachem Joshua had also made claims to Black Point and other parts of the contested land. According to documents from the following year, 1672, the Niantics had been “improving” the land. The Connecticut General Court, unlike the men from New London and Lyme, acknowledged the claim. However, they also attempted to undermine Joshua’s assertions of sovereignty in their declarations, requiring him to pay the English of Lyme “a wolfe’s head every year.” In Algonquian political culture, subjects of a sachem paid them wolf pelts as an acknowledgement of their authority. Here, the English asserted that they held that sovereignty.

With this struggle for legitimacy and colonial claims of sovereignty in mind, it seems to be no coincidence that the townspeople of Lyme and New London, living in conquered Pequot land, made more sketch maps than appear in any other local land record in New England. This mirrored the activities of trained surveyors elsewhere. On the border of Massachusetts and New Hampshire, surveyors from the Bay made dozens of maps of disputed territory that ignored the existence of any New Hampshire claim. And in 1671 in Conecticut, through English dispute and vernacular mapping, Indigenous claims were further rendered invisible.

Ultimately, these maps were made by invaders and colonists. They walked on pathways not of their own making, filled in unfamiliar swamps, and cut back forests that bewildered them. Even on the colonial periphery of a budding English cartographic culture, settlers used maps as tools to claim ownership and power. While the maps may not have won court battles, they helped the colonists present a version of the Northeast that was theirs. Thus, in the context of the settlers’ seizure of land, these “bad” maps were not useless, but rather effectively served settlers in naturalizing their conquest of Native land.
